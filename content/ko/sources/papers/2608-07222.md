# Skaling: Chinchilla's Exponents Meet Kaplan's Coupling

: Neural scaling laws let researchers predict expensive pretraining runs from cheap ones, and every large lab depends on that. But the standard Chinchilla form models reducible loss as a **sum of independent terms** for model size and tokens — which mathematically forces the cross-derivative to be *exactly zero*, i.e. it assumes model size and data do not interact. The paper shows that assumption fails precisely where prediction matters most: the imbalanced corners of the training grid, where Chinchilla's residuals form a saddle and errors reach several percent. **Skaling** adds a single coupling exponent that restores the interaction Kaplan's form had and Chinchilla discarded. The gain is not from parameter count — a 9-parameter Farseer law performs *worse* — but from an inductive bias matching the observed surface. A second practical consequence: because the law is anchored by the *edges* of parameter space rather than its interior, a sparse L-shaped grid of cheap boundary runs recovers the full-grid law at a fraction of the compute.

The structural flaw in the additive form: The failure is not a fitting artifact. It is baked into the functional form.

Chinchilla writes reducible loss as two independent power-law terms summed above an irreducible constant:


Lreduced(N, D) = (A/N^α) + (B/D^β)


That sum has a mathematically forced property: the mixed partial derivative is identically zero, whatever α and β you choose. It is not an empirical finding that N and D don't interact — it is an assumption the functional form cannot express.

The Skaling form: Skaling keeps Chinchilla's interpretable base terms and independent inner exponents, then — following Kaplan — raises their sum to a single free outer exponent κ:


L(N, D) = E + (A/N^α + B/D^β)^κ

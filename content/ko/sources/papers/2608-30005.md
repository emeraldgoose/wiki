# Small Language Models as Judges for Rubric-Based Reinforcement Learning

: Rubric-based reinforcement learning extends RL beyond tasks with exact answers or rule-based verifiers by scoring responses against **instance-specific criteria**. That makes reward computation expensive — training requires repeated rubric judging, typically via proprietary APIs or local generative judges of **7B parameters or more**. This paper asks whether smaller models can serve as efficient and reliable rubric judges. Answer: yes, but not in the obvious way. The paper constructs **PointRubric** and **RaR-Science-Static**, two pointwise rubric datasets with instance-specific criteria and itemwise satisfaction labels, and compares three ways of extracting criterion-level judgments. The **Qwen3-1.7B Probe judge** achieves the strongest criterion-level agreement, outperforming Generative and Logprob judges. Used as a GRPO reward model it trains a policy from **0.232 to 0.643** on RaR-Science rubric score, versus **0.594** for an 8B Generative judge baseline — at **10.7x less reward-judge time**.

Why rubrics make RL expensive: RLVR works when the verifier is free: a compiler, a test suite, an exact-match string. Rubric-based RL breaks that assumption to reach tasks where no exact answer exists — writing quality, research quality, design judgement. The response is scored against instance-specific criteria rather than a single verifiable function.

Three ways to ask a small model: The paper's contribution is to make the question measurable, then compare three mechanisms for extracting criterion-level judgments from a small model:

1. Generative verdicts — ask the model in words ("does criterion 3 hold? yes/no") and parse the output
2. Yes/No Logprob margins — score the yes/no tokens and use the probability margin
3. Probe judges — train a small head to read the judgement off the model's internal representation

What the results show: Probe judges win on criterion-level agreement. Across both datasets, the Qwen3-1.7B Probe judge achieves the strongest agreement with itemwise satisfaction labels — beating Generative and Logprob extraction at the same size. The intuition: a verdict you have to verbalise and parse is bottlenecked by generation quality, while a probe reads the judgement that the model already represents.

Caveats: The comparison is at a specific scale. A 1.7B Probe judge beating an 8B Generative judge does not establish that small judges beat large probes — the paper does not run that. The 10.7x figure is a ratio under this setup, not a universal constant. And agreement with itemwise labels is measured against a particular label set; how well it generalises to expert-authored rubrics in a new domain is a separate question.

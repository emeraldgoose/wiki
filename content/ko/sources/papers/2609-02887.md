# A Common Measure of Communication for Speech Brain-Computer Interfaces

: Speech brain-computer interfaces translate neural activity into language, but the field has no common yardstick — systems differ in datasets, recording methods, speech types, and vocabularies, so reported scores are rarely comparable. Beneath that lie two unresolved questions: which distribution of words *should* a speech BCI let a user communicate, and how much of it *can* a system convey? The paper answers both with **open-vocabulary mutual information (OVMI)**, an information-theoretic quantity measuring information conveyed relative to a reference distribution over intended words. OVMI exposes that accuracy, WER, and similar metrics computed only over supported words **overstate** how much intended speech a system communicates; it surfaces the coverage-vs-accuracy trade-off explicitly; and vocabulary selection maximizing OVMI yields up to **16.3% relative accuracy improvement** across three speech domains. Code and an OVMI Explorer are released.

Why supported-words-only metrics mislead: A decoder supporting 50 words at 95% accuracy looks better on paper than one supporting 5,000 at 80% — but the second communicates far more of what a user actually wants to say. OVMI fixes the reference: measure against the open-vocabulary distribution of intended communication, and narrow-but-accurate systems are correctly revealed as low-information. That reframes leaderboard comparisons across heterogeneous systems onto one communication scale, regardless of vocabulary or recording setup.

Vocabulary design as optimization: The constructive payoff: vocabulary choice becomes an optimization target rather than a design habit. Selecting words to maximize OVMI — balancing how often a user wants a word against how reliably the decoder recovers it — lifts accuracy up to 16.3% relative across three domains. Coverage and accuracy stop being rival anecdotes and become one maximand.

Caveats: This summary is abstract-based: the reference-distribution construction, the OVMI estimator, the compared systems, and the three domains all need the full text. Everything hinges on the reference word distribution being a fair model of intended speech — if it misrepresents a user's actual communication needs, OVMI inherits that error. The 16.3% figure is the max across domains, not the typical gain.

Relevance to SW engineers: - Any evaluation where systems support different scopes (vocabularies, intents, languages) risks the same trap: accuracy over the supported set overstates user-facing capability — normalize by intended distribution.
- When comparing heterogeneous systems, define the common scale first (here: information about intended words), then rank — never compare raw accuracies across different support sets.

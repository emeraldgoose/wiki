# SnapBench: Benchmarking Snap-and-Ask Multimodal Retrieval for Mobile Interactions

: Mobile AI increasingly works as a **visual oracle** — snap a picture, ask a question. But photos are often blurry and text questions may be short or mistyped, so both modalities arrive degraded. Existing benchmarks test only clean inputs or fail to isolate **paired robustness** across the two. SnapBench is the first paired benchmark for this: **1,145 queries, 9,085 gallery items, 53 controlled corruption conditions** with human annotations, evaluating **16 multimodal retrievers** across dual-tower encoders and embedding-based VLMs. The headline result is counterintuitive: **clean image-only retrieval often outperforms joint retrieval**, exposing a *coarse-text drag* and a lack of cross-modal fallback under noise. The paper also proposes MOOR, a simple adaptive fusion.

The deployment reality the benchmark encodes: Snap-and-ask is not one interaction among many — it is one of the most common entry points for mobile AI. That framing matters for benchmark design, because it dictates what to corrupt.

In real use:

- The photo is blurry — motion blur, poor lighting, a moving subject, an unsteady hand
- The text question is short — "what is this?" rather than a well-formed sentence
- The text question is mistyped — one-handed typing, autocorrect producing something else

Both major architectures are covered: dual-tower encoders and embedding-based VLMs. The 53 conditions are controlled rather than incidental, and the corruption set carries human annotations — so you can distinguish a model failure from an ambiguous or badly-labelled query.

What the results show: Image corruptions substantially degrade retrieval. Expected — a blurry photo genuinely loses information — but the magnitude matters for deployment.

Text corruptions mainly affect text-only retrieval, with limited impact on joint retrieval. Also reasonable: with a degraded image, a noisy query query contributes less marginal harm because the image is already carrying the signal.

The surprising result: clean image-only retrieval often outperforms joint retrieval. This is the finding that matter

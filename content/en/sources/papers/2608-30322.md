# Ignorance or Incompetence? Constructing Knowledge-Gated, Verifiable Tasks for LLM Agents

Source: Hanlin Tian, Minhao Li, Yu Mi, Sihan Zhu, Zhao Yang, Yuxiang Wang, Hongquan Zhu, Qiufei Hu (DataGrids), arXiv:2608.30322, Aug 31, 2026.

**Lead**: Professional agent tasks depend on conventions that are **absent from public corpora**, yet benchmarks rarely control whether an agent has access to those conventions — so when an agent fails, you cannot tell *ignorance* (it never had the rule) from *incompetence* (it had the rule and could not apply it). This paper introduces a **knowledge-gated task-construction protocol** that separates a task instruction from a compact artefact containing private conventions, reference tables, and utility operators. Across fifteen calibration tasks with **byte-identical instructions** between provided- and withheld-artefact conditions, a frontier configuration moves from a **0% pass rate to 68.0%** once the kilobyte artefact is provided. The point is not that the task is hard; it is that the dependence is *made explicit and testable*.

## The diagnosis problem

The paper's title names the ambiguity. When a professional agent fails a task, two very different explanations are available:

- **Ignorance** — the governing convention was never in the training data, so the agent cannot know it
- **Incompetence** — the convention was available and the agent could not apply it

These demand opposite responses. Ignorence means supply the missing knowledge. Incompetence means train the capability. A benchmark that does not distinguish them cannot tell you which.

Professional domains make this acute because their conventions are exactly what public corpora lack. Nobody's GitHub teaches your company's internal normalisation rule.

## Three asymmetries

The construction protocol rests on what the paper calls an **asymmetric position** created at construction time. Three asymmetries suffice:

1. **Construction privilege** — the answer is *planted* at construction time (an arbitrary internal threshold, a private normalisation table), so it is **not derivable from the instruction or environment alone**, regardless of the solver's capability
2. **Cheap verification** — checking a structured output against planted ground truth is easy even when producing that output is not
3. **Compositional verification** — an all-pass verifier over k named criteria lets task complexity grow while each failure stays attributable

The first is what does the work. Planting the answer means you have created a situation where capability is provably *not sufficient* — and verification remains exact.

## How the gating is enforced

The protocol's methodological care is in the controls:

- The task instruction **never references the artefact**
- Instructions are held **byte-identical** between the provided- and withheld-artefact conditions
- A **leak audit** checks instruction and environment for explicit gated content, with executable per-task audit scripts present for nine of fifteen calibration tasks
- The artefact is authored or adapted by a human curator from a larger domain corpus into a few kilobytes of text

The byte-identical condition is the important one: it means any performance difference is attributable to artefact access and nothing else.

## Results

Across **fifteen calibration tasks**, five trials per cell, the tested Opus configuration goes from **0% → 68.0%** pass rate once the artefact is supplied. On one task, a **plausible but incorrect** artefact also yields **0%** across five trials — so the gate keys on the correct convention, not on merely having extra context.

**Verification is automatable.** Deterministic solvers and rule corpora compute exact references for structured tasks; free-form deliverables use **named criterion-level rubrics** where no single executable oracle exists.

**Calibration is a quality-control screen.** A configuration-relative filter rejects tasks that are not solved reliably *with* the artefact, are solved *without* it, or are already easy for the second tested configuration. Seven tasks survive the five-trial empirical knowledge-gating screen.

## Honest scope

The authors are explicit about what this does not show:

- Automated audit coverage exists for **9 of 15** tasks; the other six have no automated leak audit, and static audits "do not claim to exclude every form of semantic or indirect leakage"
- The result establishes strong dependence under that configuration "without claiming to isolate knowledge from tool use, file discovery, or other capabilities"
- The retained tasks are **not** shown to improve post-training — that experiment is future work
- This is framed as a **construction problem**, not a claim about recursive self-improvement

## Relevance to SW engineers

- If your agent fails on a domain-specific task, you do not yet know whether to add documentation or improve training. That is a design decision, and this protocol is how to get the evidence for it.
- **Planting the answer** is the trick that makes the question askable: it makes capability provably insufficient while keeping verification exact.
- Byte-identical instructions between conditions is the control to copy. Most "does context help?" evaluations do not hold this.
- A wrong-but-plausible artefact scoring 0% is the strongest evidence here that the gate tests the *convention*, not the context length.

# Envisioning Agentic Robotics: A New Era of Robotic Systems

**Source**: [arXiv:2611.18359](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.18359) · **Published**: 2026-11-13
**Authors**: Grid Robotics (Qingfeng Lan, Duo Li, Siyao Li, Yiheng Lyu, Shijian Lu, Zhenghao Qi, Shizun Wang, Xinchao Wang, Ming Yang, Zhiqin Yang, Zuhao Yang, Tao Yu)

## Overview

GPT-6 Astra's release is treated here as the starting gun of a new robotics paradigm the authors call *agentic robotics*. Within weeks of that release, general-purpose frontier models paired with agentic harnesses matched or exceeded task-specific policies on many embodied benchmarks. The shift is from hand-designing a dedicated policy per task family toward applying a single foundation model to open-world tasks inside a closed loop: observe, interpret user intent, reason about the task, invoke skills and tools, verify outcomes, and adapt strategy.

The paper is a position-and-roadmap study from Grid Robotics. It argues that the decision-making core of an agentic robot is *Embodied System 2*: a foundation model plus an embodied harness that together turn general-purpose reasoning into concrete, verifiable actions. Its central challenge is doing so under physical constraints — limited reaction time, mechanical limits, unpredictable environment changes, and irreversible consequences — rather than in a sandbox where retries are free.

The authors contribute three things. First, they identify the principles that transfer from digital agents to physical ones and where those assumptions break. Second, they propose an evaluation framework that measures both model capability and closed-loop system performance, so a robot's success is judged by outcomes in the world rather than benchmark scores alone. Third, on top of Embodied System 2 and the framework, they sketch a research roadmap toward *sustained autonomy*: the ability to perform work safely and reliably with progressively broader responsibility, from executing individual primitives up to fulfilling standing roles.

## Key points

- Embodied System 2 is the core: a foundation model paired with an embodied harness that closes the loop from user intent to a verified physical outcome, rather than a per-task learned policy.
- Physical-world constraints are the differentiator: reaction-time limits, mechanical limits, unpredictable environment changes, and irreversible consequences mean mistakes are costly and often cannot be rolled back.
- The evaluation framework is dual-layer — it scores model capability (reasoning, tool use, perception) and closed-loop system performance (task completion, safety, recovery), because a strong model in a weak harness can still fail.
- Principles that carry over from digital agents: tool/skill invocation, long-horizon planning, and self-correction transfer well; assumptions of perfect sensing, cheap retries, and a static environment do not.
- The roadmap defines sustained autonomy as work performed with progressively broader responsibility — from executing individual primitives, to completing bounded tasks, to holding standing roles — all done safely and reliably.

## Details

The paper's framing centers on two embodied systems. Embodied System 1 is the familiar fast, reactive loop that most deployed robots run today: sense, act, and recover. Embodied System 2 is the slower, deliberate core — a foundation model that reasons, plans, and selects skills, coordinated by an embodied harness that translates intent into the right primitives at the right moment. The harness is what grounds the model: it supplies the skill library, the tool interfaces, the runtime monitors, and the verification checks that turn an LLM's plan into physical behavior.

The constraints section is where the physical world pushes back. A digital agent can retry a failed action indefinitely; a robot acting on a shared surface, near people, or with a heavy arm cannot. The paper catalogs the failure modes this creates — perception that degrades outside the training distribution, actuation limits, latency between decision and motion, and consequences that cannot be undone — and uses them to argue that evaluation must be closed-loop. Benchmarks that score a policy on a fixed set of tasks miss the compounding cost of recovery, safety violations, and human intervention.

The proposed framework is therefore two-dimensional. The model axis measures the quality of reasoning and tool use inside the loop; the system axis measures whether the whole loop completes tasks safely, recovers from errors, and needs minimal human help. The paper argues both axes are necessary: a model that reasons well but cannot recover a dropped object, or a harness that is robust but cannot plan a multi-step task, each fail the goal of reliable physical autonomy.

On the roadmap side, the authors sketch a ladder of increasing responsibility. The lowest rung is executing individual primitives reliably. Next is completing bounded tasks end-to-end. Higher rungs involve holding standing roles — a robot that maintains a room, a line, or a site — where success is measured over days and weeks rather than single episodes. Sustained autonomy, in this framing, is not a property of the model alone but of the whole system operating under realistic constraints for long horizons.

The paper closes by naming the open problems the community has yet to converge on: a shared definition of the paradigm, a reliable evaluation framework with accepted benchmarks, and a research roadmap. This work is an attempt to start that conversation, not to settle it.

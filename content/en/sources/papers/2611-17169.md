# Parsed, Plausible, Wrong: Output Format Controls Silent Errors in Small-Model Semantic Parsing

**Source**: [arXiv:2611.17169](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17169) · **Published**: 2026-11-12
**Authors**: Umar Ahmed, Maxim Dunaev, Mohammad Nour Al Awad, Sergey Ivanov, Ivan Khodnenko

## Overview

A small fine-tuned parser that emits a well-formed but wrong tree fails silently: nothing downstream can tell. An ITMO University team, starting from a deployed café ordering parser whose compact output format had been chosen for speed, asks whether the output format — a free design choice — controls how often this happens. They treat the silent error rate S (a plausible tree that is not what the customer said) as the quantity a deployment should minimise, reporting exact match A alongside for comparability with prior work. An output that does not parse, or is marked as a refusal, is detectable; the silent kind is the part of the problem that constrained decoding cannot reach, since it can make invalid output impossible but cannot make a well-formed tree correct.

The study fine-tunes two sub-billion models (Qwen3.5-0.8B and LFM2.5-350M) with LoRA on the PIZZA ordering benchmark over six lossless encodings of the same target tree, spanning 44.3 down to 10.9 tokens per order, including two matched-length pairs that vary surface form at fixed length. The grid runs 300 combinations (5 training pool sizes, 5 seeds) and the statistics — hierarchical bootstrap with Holm correction — were fixed before the results were read.

The intuitive hypothesis, that fewer output tokens leave less room for error, turns out wrong as first stated: shortening alone never helps, but writing the target in ordinary words does. The word encoding is the most accurate format on both models while emitting far fewer tokens than the native s-expression, and it sharply reduces silent errors.

## Key points

- The word encoding reaches 87.5% exact match versus 85.8% for the native s-expression on Qwen3.5-0.8B while emitting 4.1× fewer tokens, and cuts the silent error rate by 2.3× on Qwen and 1.8× on LFM2 — separating from other formats at every pool size.
- Length is not the mechanism: the word encoding beats its symbolic twin of equal length in 10 of 10 model × size cells, separating in 9 after Holm correction — and on LFM2 it wins while actually costing more tokens.
- Error analysis locates much of the word encoding's gain in negation, where the silent mis-parses concentrate.
- Guardrails are trained into every run: all runs learn to refuse off-menu input (95.9–99.0% of held-out refusals handled) and to apply in-utterance self-corrections, with the corrections learned best by the words encoding.
- Strict refusal has a measured price: 2.1–10.1% of real, mostly polite, orders are refused.
- PIZZA and similar task-oriented parsing datasets (TOP, TOPv2, MTOP) contain neither off-domain refusals nor spoken self-corrections; the authors add both as synthetic training data and evaluate on held-out vocabulary and cue phrases.

## Details

The six-encoding ladder includes s-expressions, JSON, key=value pairs, a terse symbolic language, and plain words, with matched-length pairs separating length from surface form; the paper stresses that "characters are not tokens" when comparing encodings. The four-way split of every test set supports the silent-error-rate objective, and a deployment demonstration plus integrity checks appear in appendices. The findings echo Shin et al. (2021)'s prompting result — a canonical natural-language form beats prompting for the representation itself — now shown under fine-tuning, where the model is free to learn any notation, with naturalness separated from length by matched pairs and two vocabularies. Code, analysis scripts, and trained models are released at github.com/UmarGit/coffee-llm. This is the v1 (12 November 2026) HF-listed submission.

# Zuse: A Decision Model for European Languages

**Source**: [arXiv:2611.06238](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06238) · **Published**: 2026-11-04
**Authors**: Umair Mehmood Imam

## Overview

Many operational uses of language models call for a choice among known options rather than free-form text: ticket routing, policy checks, moderation, and form extraction all have this shape. A decision model reads a document and returns a probability for each option in a fixed set instead of generating tokens. Several such models appeared in 2026 — Jev, the open reproduction Kev, and Clef — but none has published results outside English, which leaves open whether the design carries across the many languages European public bodies and companies work in.

Zuse is a family of three decision models (4B, 9B, and 27B parameters) that train a low-rank adapter and a pointer head on frozen open-weight bases, targeting 27 European languages. The paper uses them to study how the decision-model design behaves across languages and tasks. Evaluation uses a broad European suite of 11 sources excluded from training, covering 9 task groups and 27 languages; a development set drawn from the same suite also guided model selection, so the headline numbers are not fully independent of selection.

On that suite, the 27B model reaches 87.5% accuracy with a pooled expected calibration error of 0.016, and 0.035 when calibration error is weighted per source. The paper's post-hoc analyses of these single-seed results yield three findings on calibration structure, automation at a fixed error budget, and cross-language reach, plus two practical pitfalls in building multi-source training data. On English public suites Zuse trails Jev and Kev, including Kev models built on the same bases.

## Key points

- First per-language evaluation of decision models reported by the author: 27 European languages, 11 held-out sources, 9 task groups, and reach across 210 languages.
- 27B model: 87.5% accuracy on the European suite; pooled ECE 0.016, or 0.035 when weighted per source.
- Miscalibration is structured by task rather than language: knowledge tasks are overconfident and acceptability judgements underconfident at all three sizes, so one fitted temperature cannot correct both.
- Across parallel reading and topic items in 26 languages, the confidence gap varies about as much as sampling noise; across sources it spans roughly -0.07 to +0.09.
- Automation at a 5% error budget tracks chance-corrected skill (Spearman 0.97 over 33 source and model pairs) more closely than raw accuracy (0.91).
- Reach beyond the training languages is set by the base model: across 210 languages, changing the training run moved scores by at most about 2 points within a size, while languages outside the base's documented list trail by 23 to 28 points at every size.
- On English public suites Zuse is behind Jev and Kev; the paper reports being 2.5 points below Jev on the Transfer v4 development split and 2.9 points behind Kev-27B on the test split.
- Two training-data pitfalls are reported: label-word collision between topic schemes, and an injection augmentation that taught a shortcut.

## Details

**Method.** Zuse trains a low-rank adapter (LoRA-style) and a pointer head on frozen open-weight base models, in three sizes (4B, 9B, 27B), targeting 27 European languages. The evaluation is built around a European suite of 11 sources excluded from training, spanning 9 task groups and 27 languages; a development split of that suite (together with the Transfer v4 development split) was used for model selection, error diagnosis, and planning later runs. Almost all configurations were trained once, so results are single-seed. Reference metrics include accuracy, Brier score, expected calibration error (pooled and per-source weighted), an automation share at a 5% error budget (auto@5), order flips measured on 1,223 choice rows from a 1,500-row sample, and planted-instruction following on the same 1,500 rows. Selection effects are acknowledged: the English test splits were scored on five 27B checkpoints whose accuracies span 0.859 to 0.866, and test scores were recorded alongside flagship decisions, so independence from selection cannot be claimed. On a 764-row split an accuracy near 0.86 carries a 95% margin of about 2.5 points; the European suite margin is about 0.5 points under independence, wider in practice because rows cluster within sources. Retraining on identical data moved two small-model results by up to 0.5 points, the paper's only direct measure of training noise.

**Main results.** On the European suite, Zuse-27B reaches 87.5% accuracy with pooled ECE 0.016 (0.035 per-source weighted). Calibration errors are task-structured: across sources the confidence gap spans -0.07 to +0.09, with knowledge tasks overconfident and acceptability judgements underconfident at all three sizes, while on parallel reading and topic items in 26 languages the gap varies about as much as sampling noise — so a single fitted temperature cannot fix both failure modes. The share of decisions automatable at a 5% error budget follows chance-corrected skill (Spearman 0.97 over 33 source and model pairs) more closely than raw accuracy (0.91). Beyond the 27 training languages, reach is governed by the base model: across 210 languages, changing the training run shifted scores by at most about 2 points within a size, whereas languages outside the base's documented list trail by 23 to 28 points at every size. On English public suites, Zuse-27B is 2.5 points below Jev on the Transfer v4 development split (unpaired p = 0.19, only indicative) and Kev-27B leads by 2.9 points on the test split; on the same bases Kev-9B and Kev-4B lead Zuse-9B and Zuse-4B by 4.8 to 6.3 points on Transfer v4, with lower Brier scores. Zuse-27B scores 0.924 on SemIf against Kev-27B's reported 0.903, though SemIf is close to saturated. On the breadth report (a chance-corrected 0–100 index from 14 public datasets), Zuse trails Jev by 5.8 points (95% paired bootstrap CI 2.2 to 9.0) and differs from Kev-27B by 2.7 points (CI -0.2 to 5.3). The paper also reports two pitfalls in multi-source training data: label-word collision between topic schemes, and an injection augmentation that taught a shortcut rather than the intended behavior. Additionally, 4-bit product builds differ from the 16-bit evaluation builds, and the breadth report and When2Call were run against deployed 4-bit endpoints that met server errors, with all 1,990 records answered after retries. Zuse is available to try at https://bineric.com/zuse. The paper is 9 pages with 3 figures and 7 tables, in cs.CL / cs.LG.

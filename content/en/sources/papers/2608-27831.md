# RealSWE: A Compositional Evaluation of Coding Agents under Realistic User Requests

**Source**: [arXiv:2608.27831](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.27831) · **Published**: 2026-08-28
**Authors**: Gyuhyeong Kim, Hyojung Gwon, Jeonghyeon Kim and 2 other authors

## Overview

Coding agents are commonly evaluated on the SWE-bench family of benchmarks, whose tasks are built from curated GitHub issues: long, structured, and information-rich. The requests that actually arrive in production are the opposite — short, casually written, and often no more than a bare statement of the problem. This paper quantifies the mismatch between the two and measures how much it distorts reported agent ability.

To characterize the gap, the authors define a six-category information taxonomy (problem statement, desired behavior, motivation, environment information, reproduction steps, and other) and four dimensions of linguistic style (grammaticality, command strength, expertise level, and formality), then apply both to real user prompts from SWE-chat and to problem statements from SWE-bench Verified and Pro. Requests carrying only a problem statement, alone or with limited additional context, account for 88% of real prompts but just 7% of benchmark problems, and 87% of real prompts are casually written whereas 94% of benchmark problems are formal.

Guided by those observations, the authors introduce RealSWE: 381 multi-variant task families derived from SWE-bench Verified and Pro. Every variant within a family shares the same underlying task and the same gold patch and differs only in information composition and linguistic style. Because the target solution is held fixed, any change in resolution rate is attributable to the input form rather than to task difficulty — a compositional decomposition of agent performance.

Evaluating seven contemporary LLMs with RealSWE, the authors find that realistic inputs reduce resolution rates by 6.4 pp on average and can change model rankings. A controlled analysis shows that including Desired Behavior and Motivation significantly affects performance, whereas Environment Information and Reproduction Steps merely add tokens without measurable benefit; linguistic style has only small, model-dependent effects.

## Key points

- Benchmark tasks are not representative of real requests: requests carrying only a problem statement account for 88% of real prompts but just 7% of benchmark problems, and 87% of real prompts are casual whereas 94% of benchmark problems are formal.
- RealSWE is 381 multi-variant task families derived from SWE-bench Verified and Pro; variants within a family share the same underlying task and gold patch and differ only in information composition and linguistic style.
- Realistic inputs reduce resolution rates by 6.4 pp on average and can change model rankings — the finding that motivates the benchmark.
- Including Desired Behavior and Motivation significantly affects performance, while Environment Information and Reproduction Steps add tokens with no measurable benefit (notably including reproduction steps).
- Linguistic style (formality) has only small, model-dependent effects; writing casually is not by itself a major problem.

## Details

The six-category information taxonomy and the four style dimensions are used to compare SWE-chat real user prompts against SWE-bench Verified / Pro problem statements:

| Axis | Real user prompts | Benchmark problems |
|---|---|---|
| Problem statement only (no or limited extra context) | 88% | 7% |
| Casual style | 87% | 4% |
| Formal style | — | 94% |

Method: each RealSWE family shares one underlying task and one gold patch and expands it into several "how would this be asked" variants, so a change in resolution rate can be split into the contribution of missing information versus style. Across seven contemporary LLMs, realistic inputs cost 6.4 pp on average and can reorder the ranking. Including Desired Behavior and Motivation yields a significant effect, while Environment Information and Reproduction Steps add tokens with no measurable gain. The style effect is small and model-dependent.

The practical takeaway is that explicitly stating the desired behavior and its motivation — the very information most real prompts omit — substantially improves the model's software-engineering performance. When building agents, do not assume inputs resemble tidy issues; when designing prompts, faithfully filling in environment information and reproduction steps brings no measurable benefit; when evaluating, an input distribution unlike the real workload can lead to the wrong conclusion that an agent is worse.

# LLMs Memorize Their Own Hallucinations During Post-Training

**Source**: [arXiv:2611.26863](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26863) · **Published**: 2026-11-19 (v1)
**Authors**: Anirudh Atmakuru, Tanya Goyal

## Overview

Reasoning domains have shaped frontier post-training recipes — outcome-based RL rewards and "cognitive" behaviors like proposing and backtracking from hypotheses — even though models are deployed far beyond math and code. This paper studies what happens when those reasoning-centric recipes are applied to knowledge tasks: the hypothesis is that they inadvertently encourage models to memorize the hallucinations they themselves generate in long reasoning chains. The authors train models with standard post-training, extract their self-generated hallucinations during RL, and track memorization of those hallucinations (on HalluSet-train vs held-out) throughout the rest of training across six open models from two families and two architectures (Qwen3-4B/8B/14B/30B-A3B, Olmo-3-7B-Think, OLMoE-1B-7B) on QuALITY and TriviaQA.

The hypothesis is confirmed: recall of hallucinations that were reinforced during training increases by 4.83 points on average, versus only 0.17 points for self-hallucinations that received no training signal. Models even memorize false facts they immediately backtrack from in their reasoning — the backtracking template inherited from reasoning-centric training brings no benefit for knowledge recall, yet the retracted facts are learned just as strongly. Memorization is strongest on the hardest training questions (fewer correct rollouts → larger positive GRPO advantages for the few correct ones, so hallucinations in them are reinforced more), and rewarding a hallucination makes the model 7.23 points less likely to abstain ("None of the above") from it later. The work identifies a previously unexamined pathway through which models learn hallucinations during training.

## Key points

- Reasoning-centric recipes (outcome-based RL rewards, hypothesis/backtracking templates) applied to knowledge tasks cause models to memorize their own self-generated hallucinations.
- Reinforced hallucinations: +4.83 average memorization-score increase (train vs held-out gap) vs +0.17 for unreinforced self-hallucinations, across 6 open models (Qwen3 family, OLMo family) on QuALITY and TriviaQA.
- Backtracked false facts are memorized just as strongly as non-retracted hallucinations — backtracking in the chain does not protect against learning the false claim.
- Memorization grows with training-question difficulty (fewer correct rollouts); GRPO group normalization gives those rare correct rollouts larger positive advantages, reinforcing their hallucinations more strongly.
- Reinforcing a hallucination reduces later abstention: with a "None of the above" option, models correctly abstain 7.23 points less often after the hallucination was rewarded.
- Implication: post-training for knowledge tasks should not blindly adopt reasoning-domain RL recipes; outcome rewards can entrench self-generated falsehoods.

## Details

Method: standard post-training (SFT + RL with outcome rewards, e.g., GRPO-style group-relative advantages), then extraction of self-generated hallucinations from reasoning chains during RL and memorization scoring on HalluSet-train vs HalluSet-heldout (Δ = train − heldout as the memorization score). Per-model Δ values on QuALITY range ~3.9–6.3 (e.g., Qwen3-8B 5.90, OLMoE-1B-7B 6.33) while heldout scores stay near zero. Ablations: (1) backtracked vs non-backtracked hallucinations on TriviaQA — both memorized strongly; (2) difficulty curve — memorization increases as the number of correct rollouts decreases; (3) abstention probe — replacing a distractor with "None of the above" shows reduced abstention after reinforcement (Table 2). Practical takeaway: knowledge-domain post-training needs hallucination-aware reward shaping, since outcome-only rewards memorize self-hallucinations, especially on hard questions.

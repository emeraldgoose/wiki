# Kierston & KierstonBench: Simulation of Allentown, PA and Evaluation of Language Models in Crime Investigation and Surveillance Tasks

**Source**: [arXiv:2611.04785](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.04785) · **Published**: 2026-11-03
**Authors**: Andrew Semenov, Svyatoslav Dorofeev

## Overview

Large language models are candidates for collecting and analyzing records in criminal investigations and mass surveillance, yet their capability and accuracy in this work cannot currently be measured. Real deployments lack the ground truth needed to score a model's conclusions, and no benchmark evaluates these capabilities in a simulated environment. Kierston is a city-scale simulation engine and KierstonBench a benchmark testing such capabilities against known simulated events. It models roughly 125,000 residents of Allentown, Pennsylvania, generating daily activities and telecommunications, financial, administrative, and license-plate records. These derive from a common behavioral timeline and are distributed across sources representing separate providers. Models investigate through a restricted records interface, linking observations across sources without access to the underlying simulation.

## Key points

- The simulation models roughly 125,000 Allentown residents.
- Records come from a common behavioral timeline across separate providers.
- The benchmark covers 32 task families.
- Task families include record linkage, routine reconstruction, incident investigation, and deceptive accounts.
- Most of sixteen evaluated models could not perform records investigation supportably.
- A small number of frontier models solved most tasks and abstained when evidence was insufficient.

## Details

Evaluation shows that some frontier systems synthesize unsupported identifications and fall for common deceptions despite solving most tasks. None replaced an investigator. The simulation provides ground truth for otherwise unscoreable investigation tasks, but results depend on the fidelity of the simulated population, record sources, and deception patterns. Such benchmarks should be used carefully because surveillance applications involve high-stakes privacy and civil-liberty concerns.

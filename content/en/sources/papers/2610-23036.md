# Towards Looped Models Done Right, Part I: Topology, Input Injection, Recurrent-State Design

**Source**: [arXiv:2610.23036](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.23036) · **Published**: 2026-10-16
**Authors**: Benhao Huang, Chufan Shi, Junlin Chen, Shicheng Wen, Zhengzhong Liu, Eric Xing, Xuezhe Ma

## Overview

Looped language models reuse the same physical Transformer blocks across logical depth, trading extra computation for a smaller memory footprint. The two architectures that dominate recent work, Ouro and Huginn, change three things at once: where recurrence sits in the stack, how the input is injected at every iteration, and how the recurrent state is organized. Because the published recipes vary all three together, it is not clear which choice produces the reported gains. This paper isolates the axes. Every model is trained from scratch at matched parameter scale, logical depth and token budget, and the resulting design principles are then re-tested on Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models matched on resident and active parameters.

The clearest result is topological. Moving recurrence out of the full stack and into a sandwich envelope, a tied recurrent core between untied prelude and coda blocks, improves tasks that require multi-step derivation from the instance: at the 730M-parameter, 336B-token operating point MATH500 rises 12.00 points and DROP 2.61 points, and the MATH500 and BBH-CoT gains persist across all four training budgets. The advantage does not extend to knowledge-heavy tasks or to tasks with strict output and interface requirements, where changes are mixed or negative. Persistent input injection mainly helps context-dependent and specification-constrained work, adding 6.63 points on BBH-CoT and 5.49 points on HumanEval+ in the middle-loop architecture, but it costs 3.60 points on MATH500 and 2.51 on GSM8K. With both mechanisms active, the prelude-loop-coda model still beats injected full-stack Ouro on eight of ten benchmarks.

The third axis moves the other way. Randomly initializing the recurrent state, a common ingredient in iterative models, produces two gains and four losses of more than one point, and a hierarchical high/low state schedule run over a shared recurrent body produces three gains and three losses, the largest of them on MATH500. Taken together the results favor the Huginn-style design: at 730M parameters and 336B tokens the complete Huginn architecture outperforms Ouro on all ten benchmarks. The authors then ask whether that ordering survives sparse models. On MoE models with 8.0B resident and 0.8B active parameters (about 32.0B resident-equivalent once unrolled) trained on 500B tokens, Huginn again leads Ouro on eight of ten benchmarks and routes experts more evenly, and under matched training and inference FLOPs it needs 75% fewer resident parameters than a 112-layer feedforward MoE baseline while still winning DROP and GSM8K and matching it on MATH500.

## Key points

- Loop placement (Q1). A tied 12-block core between eight untied prelude and eight untied coda blocks is compared with full-stack Ouro, which reuses all 28 blocks four times, at equal logical compute. At 460 tokens per parameter the sandwich raises MATH500 by 12.00 points (20.80 to 32.80), GSM8K by 7.05, DROP by 2.61, BBH-CoT by 2.47 and MBPP+ by 2.12, while ARC-Challenge falls 0.94 and HumanEval+ 1.22. The MATH500 and BBH-CoT gains hold at all four budgets (42B, 84B, 168B, 336B tokens).
- Input injection (Q2). Writing the prelude state before every recurrent-core pass improves MMLU by 2.53, BBH-CoT by 6.63, DROP by 1.39, HumanEval+ by 5.49 and MBPP+ by 4.23, but lowers MATH500 by 3.60 and GSM8K by 2.51. Raw-token injection into full-stack Ouro moves the same capability groups, by +1.79 MMLU, +1.80 BBH-CoT, +0.91 DROP, +2.44 HumanEval+ and +4.50 MBPP+.
- Latent state (Q3). Random initialization helps ARC-Challenge by 3.34 points and GSM8K by 1.22 but loses more than a point on MMLU, MATH500, HumanEval+ and MBPP+; direct initialization from the prelude embedding wins on six of ten benchmarks. A shared-body high/low hierarchy (two high-level cycles, three low-level updates each, eight recurrent applications in total) adds ARC-Challenge 2.23 and DROP 1.80 but removes 7.20 points from MATH500.
- Dense conclusion. Under the cumulative construction path the sandwich envelope and persistent injection carry the broad gains, while a separate random-initialized state adds nothing consistent; at the 730M and 336B-token scale the complete Huginn architecture leads Ouro on all ten benchmarks.
- MoE transfer. With 28 routed layers, 25 experts, top-2 routing and 8.0B resident / 793.9M active parameters, Huginn beats Ouro on eight of ten benchmarks after 500B tokens, with GSM8K +4.70, MATH500 +3.60, MMLU +2.78 and DROP +1.95, and loses only ARC-Challenge (0.86) and BBH-CoT (0.20).
- Routing. Normalized load-balancing loss at 500B tokens is lowest for Huginn MoE at 1.571, against 1.652 for the feedforward reference and 1.899 for Ouro, so its expert utilization is the most even. Forcing iterations 2-8 to reuse the expert identities chosen at iteration 1 reduces accuracy on all six probed tasks, evidence that iteration-specific expert selection matters.
- Feedforward comparison. The 112-layer feedforward MoE still leads on seven of ten benchmarks, but Huginn leads on DROP and GSM8K and matches it on MATH500, and the mean gap narrows from 4.96 points in the dense setting to 1.71 points in MoE. The looped model reaches this with 75% fewer resident parameters under matched training and inference FLOPs.

## Details

The controlled study trains decoder-only PreNorm models with a SwiGLU feed-forward branch, 24 query heads, 6 key-value heads, head dimension 64, RoPE base 10^6 and hidden size 1536, so that the ablations change the recurrence pattern rather than the block. Dense runs use 730M parameters at 42B, 84B, 168B and 336B tokens (58 to 460 tokens per parameter); two feedforward references are included, a 28-block model matching the resident parameter count and a 112-block model matching the unrolled logical depth. Data is the TxT360 mixture at sequence length 8192 with an effective global batch of 512 sequences, or 4,194,304 tokens per step, optimized with AdamW, a swept peak learning rate, 200 warmup steps, cosine decay and weight decay 0.1.

Evaluation covers ten benchmarks in four overlapping groups: knowledge and commonsense (ARC-Challenge, HellaSwag, MMLU, TriviaQA), instruction and passage reasoning (BBH-CoT, DROP), quantitative and symbolic reasoning (GSM8K, MATH500) and code generation (HumanEval+, MBPP+). The MoE study replaces the feed-forward branch of every physical layer with a top-2 routed mixture of 25 experts of width 2432, using 28 routed layers and dot-product load-balancing loss without router bias; the recurrent models carry 8.0B resident and 793.9M active parameters per physical pass, about 3.2B active parameter applications per token when unrolled, and are trained for 500B tokens, roughly 630 tokens per active parameter. The feedforward reference stacks 112 layers with 32.0B resident parameters under the same data and matched training and inference FLOPs.

The interpretation is bounded deliberately. The envelope's benefit is read as better reasoning over instance-provided information rather than better retrieval of stored knowledge or more reliable compliance with output contracts, which is why code generation stays mixed despite its gains on reasoning benchmarks. The negative finding on the high/low hierarchy applies to the shared-module control used here and does not rule out the separately parameterized design of HRM-Text, which the authors say is still under evaluation. The MoE comparison also compares complete configurations, so it cannot attribute the advantage to individual axes; the authors list matched MoE ablations of each axis, a small-to-large scaling ladder, multi-seed replication and route-freezing checks across checkpoints as future work.

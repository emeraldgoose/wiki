# Does Scaling Toward AGI Depend on What System-1 Lets System-2 Execute?

**Source**: [arXiv:2610.39497](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.39497) · **Published**: 2026-10-27
**Authors**: Qi Wang et al.

## Overview

Progress toward AGI has largely been achieved by scaling System-2, a resource-intensive executive, through larger models, more data, more compute, longer reasoning, and more agents. This paper asks whether the next stage may instead come from co-designing System-2 with System-1, a lightweight, semantically aware predictor that evaluates candidate interactions before any execution.

An interaction can mean a model generation episode, a planning step, or an agent rollout. The paper proposes conditional interaction as a coupling mechanism: System-1 predicts the utility of a candidate interaction and decides whether it is worth executing. This is intended to improve interaction efficiency rather than only increasing the volume of interactions.

## Key points

- Scaling alone faces diminishing returns and costly, scarce high-quality data.
- Assessing an interaction's value must cost much less than executing it.
- System-1 predicts utility before System-2 pays the execution cost.
- Conditional interaction explicitly chooses which interactions to execute.
- The digital regime suffers from wasted rollouts and ineffective prompts.
- The physical regime suffers from expensive and hard-to-parallelize real-world rollouts.
- A testable scaling hypothesis centers on informative compute rather than raw interaction count.
- Selective supervision raises the issue that a gate also shapes its own training data.

## Details

The paper frames standard scaling laws as describing diminishing returns from undifferentiated interactions; changing which interactions are executed can shift the curve itself. It discusses RLVR as one digital case, where mixed outcomes provide gradient signal but prompts that always succeed or always fail produce degenerate updates. In the physical case, rolling out interactions in the real world is costly and limited.

The proposed approach is architectural: give the executive a partner whose job is to judge, and design the two systems together. It interprets this through active inference, decision-making, and control-theory lenses, and extends conditional interaction across digital and physical domains.

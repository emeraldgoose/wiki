# Knowing When Not to Reuse: Conditional Experience Transfer in Autonomous LLM Post-Training

Source: Tingyun Li, Wenfeng Feng, Weiqing Li and 3 other authors, arXiv:2608.26730, Aug 27, 2026.

**Lead**: Autonomous post-training systems propose updates, train candidates, and use evaluation feedback to pick the next proposal. As evidence accumulates, a central question emerges: **after subsequent training has changed the parent model, which past update evidence is still actionable?** An update's effect depends on its parent, its data, and its training stage — so treating past success as *context-free permission* wastes compute, and promoting a bad child can degrade everything after it. The paper formalises this as **conditional experience transfer** and introduces **Boundary-Calibrated Intervention Transfer (BCIT)**, which authorizes reuse *before* weight-changing training. On a 4B model across finance reasoning, text-to-SQL, and function calling, BCIT authorizes **fewer harmful updates and reaches higher equal-budget final-model quality** than the alternatives — the argument being that experience *authorization* is its own problem, distinct from experience generation.

## The failure mode being named

The standard autonomous post-training loop looks like this: propose an update → train a candidate → evaluate → use the result to choose the next proposal. Each step is automated. What is *not* handled is the accumulation of evidence and its decay.

Consider: an update U produced a +6pp gain when applied to parent P0. Three post-training rounds later the model is P3. Does U still help? The paper's answer is that you cannot assume so, because:

- The effect depended on the **parent** (P0's failure mode may no longer exist)
- It depended on the **data** (P3 may already have seen equivalent examples)
- It depended on the **training stage** (the same update can be redundant or conflicting later)

Treating past success as context-free permission has two costs. The obvious one is **wasted compute** on updates that no longer help. The subtler one: if a degraded child gets promoted, it **degrades the subsequent training trajectory** — every later decision is made against a worse model.

## BCIT: authorize before training

The design decision is the ordering. BCIT runs the authorization check *before* the expensive part:

1. **Bind the effect to its source context** — record which parent, which data, which stage the observed effect came from
2. **Check applicability conditions** — is the current state close enough to the source context for the evidence to transfer?
3. **Veto candidates with named hard conflicts** — explicit, nameable reasons to refuse
4. **Obtain current-state evidence via a bounded training trial** when the cheap checks are inconclusive

Two further rules close the loop:

- Fully trained candidates still face a **shared adoption rule** — the gate applies to everyone, not a special case
- **Only observed events extend memory** — a rejected candidate teaches the system something, an unobserved one does not

This is the difference between "we tried this once and it worked" and "we tried this once, under these conditions, and here is whether those conditions still hold."

## Evaluation

Testbed: a **4B model** adapted across three distinct domains — **finance reasoning, text-to-SQL, and function calling**.

The measurement that matters: candidate updates exhibit **heterogeneous target and retention effects across the evaluated contexts**. The same candidate helps one domain and hurts another. Under **matched candidates, evidence, and compute**, BCIT:

- authorizes **fewer harmful updates**, and
- attains **higher equal-budget final-model quality**

The equal-budget condition is what makes this a fair comparison — the gain is not from spending more compute but from spending it on the right updates.

## The conceptual contribution

The paper's broader claim: **experience authorization deserves to be treated as a distinct problem in autonomous post-training**, not as a detail of experience generation. Systems that conflate the two will accumulate a memory of what worked and apply it indiscriminately, which is exactly how a self-improving loop starts to degrade its own foundation.

## Relevance to SW engineers

- Any system that reuses past experience — retrieval-augmented agents, prompt libraries, fine-tune datasets, caches — inherits this failure mode. Ask: does this evidence still apply to the state I'm in now?
- Vetoes with *named* reasons are debuggable; a scalar confidence threshold is not.
- "Bounded trial" is a good pattern generally: cheap probe before expensive commitment, then record what the probe actually showed.

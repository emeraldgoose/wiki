# MoLRSI: A Recursive Self-Improving AI agent for Molecular Discovery

**Source**: [arXiv:2610.39714](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.39714) · **Published**: 2026-10-29
**Authors**: Yikun Zhang, Xiwei Cheng, Mingze Yuan, Yu Zhang, Brian Shim, Aarti Krishnan, Daniel Rosen, Kunyang Sun, Yingze Wang, Zhenfeng Deng, Botao Yu, Bingxu Liu, David Liu, Tianyu Liu, Yuanqi Du, Nir Hacohen, James J. Collins, Wengong Jin

## Overview

Molecular discovery requires navigating vast combinatorial search spaces while balancing competing biological objectives. AI agents can automate expert-defined molecular design workflows, but automation alone does not address how to optimize workflows for new tasks or improve them through experience. MoLRSI is an AI agent that combines systematic optimization of workflows and model architectures with persistent cross-task memory. Workflow performance, refinement outcomes, and execution feedback guide subsequent searches, allowing the agent to improve both its workflows and its strategy for developing them. The approach targets competing objectives such as activity, toxicity, and structural novelty.

## Key points

- The agent optimizes workflows and model architectures together.
- Persistent cross-task memory carries experience across tasks.
- Execution feedback guides later searches.
- Antibiotic discovery and peptide-MHC binder design are evaluated.
- The antibiotic workflow achieves a higher experimental hit rate than an expert baseline.
- Peptide binder workflows yield validated hits with preferential binding.

## Details

In antibiotic discovery, MoLRSI developed a screening workflow with a 15.9 percent experimental hit rate, compared with 8.7 percent for a previous expert-led workflow using the same training data and screening library. In de novo peptide-MHC binder design, it produced eight experimentally validated hits against MART-1 and SARS-CoV-1 pMHCs with preferential binding over tested off-targets, while another evaluated agent completely failed. The results show experience-guided workflow optimization can address competing discovery objectives and improve later search strategies, though hit-rate gains depend on the screening library, assay, and feedback thresholds used.

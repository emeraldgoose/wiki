# Follow the Gradient: Tracing Safety Degradation Back to Training Data

**Source**: [arXiv:2610.37481](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.37481) · **Published**: 2026-10-27
**Authors**: Saket Reddy, Amro Abbas, Anshuman Suri, Ari Morcos, David Schwab, Pratyush Maini

## Overview

Fine-tuning large language models (LLMs) on instruction data can quietly erode safety alignment, even when none of the training examples looks harmful. Because the responsible data appears benign, ordinary content filters and safety judges fail to catch it. This paper studies how to trace such safety degradation back to the training examples that cause it.

The authors propose SimpleLoRIF, a scalable gradient embedding method that identifies fine-tuning examples whose gradients align with unsafe behavior. Building on LoRIF, it discards the curvature correction and restricts backpropagation to the final four transformer blocks, making per-example gradient filtering practical for SFT datasets on the order of about one million examples.

## Key points

- Fine-tuning can weaken safety even on benign, widely used instruction data, so data selection matters for safety, not just quality.
- SimpleLoRIF uses gradient alignment with unsafe responses as the signal, simplifying an earlier low-rank attribution method for scale.
- On emergent misalignment, gradients of insecure-code training data align with the unrelated harmful behaviors that later emerge.
- SafeTulu is created by removing the 20% of Tulu 3 examples most gradient-aligned with unsafe SafeRLHF responses.
- Across Qwen, Llama, and Olmo, SafeTulu reduces GCG attack success rate by up to 22% relative to baseline training.
- Removed examples exhibit recurring patterns, including tool-execution failures, narrative arithmetic problems, and tasks involving covert physical manipulation.
- The authors release SafeTulu and a Hugging Face Space so researchers can build customized filtered datasets.

## Details

The method is first validated on emergent misalignment, where fine-tuning on insecure code induces broadly harmful behavior outside the programming domain. In models that exhibit this behavior, insecure-code training gradients align with the gradients of the harmful behaviors that emerge after fine-tuning.

The paper then turns to SafeRLHF-aligned data: SimpleLoRIF ranks Tulu 3 examples by gradient alignment with unsafe responses, removes the top 20%, and retrains. The resulting SafeTulu is evaluated with GCG attack success rate, achieving reductions of up to 22% across model families, helping measure how much safety was recovered without abandoning the underlying capability data.

Qualitative inspection of removed examples suggests that safety degradation is not driven only by obviously toxic text, but by recurring task patterns such as failed tool executions and certain arithmetic or physical-reasoning prompts. The authors also note limitations: they test general safety degradation rather than specific harms such as sycophancy, and they release hyperparameters for further reproduction.

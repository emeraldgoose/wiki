# InclusionMed: Benchmarking AI for Inclusive Healthcare

## Overview

Advancing medical AI for inclusive healthcare requires evaluation across diverse healthcare roles, tasks, and care settings, but benchmark coverage remains uneven. InclusionMed is an open medical AI evaluation initiative for inclusive healthcare. Its centerpiece, the InclusionMed Atlas, organizes benchmarks across three healthcare roles and three AI capabilities, producing nine role–capability cells.

For the first release, the team selected four public benchmarks from 388 candidates through structured audits and independent reproduction. Together with InclusionMed-DxFiles, a private diagnostic reasoning benchmark, the Atlas contains five benchmarks covering three of its nine role–capability cells, each contributing 20% to the Index.

The authors evaluated frontier large language models on the Atlas and aggregated the results into the InclusionMed Index, with overall scores ranging from 17.5 to 49.1 out of 100. The Index also reports scores by healthcare role and AI capability, showing that a model's rank varied with the role or capability considered. A within-benchmark analysis using task-level tags showed that the ten highest-ranked models in the Index all scored higher on patient communication than on clinical decision-making. Models with similar Index scores also differed substantially in estimated inference cost.

## Key points

- The InclusionMed Atlas maps benchmarks onto a 3×3 grid of healthcare roles × AI capabilities (understanding, reasoning, agency); the first release covers only three of the nine cells.
- Four public benchmarks were selected from 388 candidates via six audit criteria plus independent reproduction; InclusionMed-DxFiles (private) is the fifth.
- Overall InclusionMed Index scores range from 17.5 to 49.1 out of 100 across frontier LLMs.
- Model rankings shift depending on the role or capability slice considered — no single ranking holds across contexts.
- The ten highest-ranked models all scored higher on patient communication than on clinical decision-making.
- Models with similar Index scores can differ substantially in estimated inference cost, so cost-aware selection matters.

## Details

**Method.** Candidate benchmarks were collected from published literature, public platforms/repositories, and community contributions. LLM-assisted curation organized each into a structured benchmark card (task scope, data and annotation, evaluation protocol, reported performance, source links), drawing on dataset documentation practice. Candidates then underwent sequential screening against six audit criteria — including healthcare relevance — with human review throughout, followed by independent reproduction before inclusion. Benchmarks were mapped to the Atlas by their predominant healthcare role–capability combination (healthcare-relevant subsets were used for broader benchmarks). Models were evaluated using benchmark-specific execution and scoring procedures; scores were aggregated into the InclusionMed Index, and inference cost was estimated per model.

**Results.** Overall Index scores span 17.5–49.1/100. Rankings varied by role and by capability, and task-level tag analysis showed the top ten models all performed better on patient communication than on clinical decision-making. Similar Index scores masked substantially different inference costs, underscoring the need for evaluation covering more diverse healthcare needs and for context-specific model selection.

# MemInStream: Memorizing or Merely Recognizing? Rethinking Streaming Video Memory Evaluation

**Source**: [arXiv:2611.15640](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.15640) · **Published**: 2026-11-11
**Authors**: Zhenqi He, Dunyuan Xu, Yang Bo, Kien T. Pham, Yanghao Wang, Shuhan Li, Xintong Guo, Yuyang He, Fang Liu, Huankang Guan, Jingyan Chen, Rui Liu, Long Chen

## Overview

Streaming video understanding asks a model to perceive frames as they arrive, track evolving events, and answer questions in real time — which makes retaining and retrieving relevant history a core capability as the video grows. Existing benchmarks (StreamingBench, OVO-Bench, OVBench, TeleEgo, S-EMBER, EgoProbe) do include memory-related questions, but the HKUST/CUHK/Huawei/CityU team behind MemInStream argues that QA accuracy alone cannot tell genuine memorization from two confounds: an incorrect answer may reflect a visual-understanding failure rather than forgetting, and a correct answer may come from linguistic priors, option cues, or the most recent frames without ever touching historical memory. Their motivating measurements are stark — recent models fail nearly a quarter of questions even when handed the required evidence intervals, and a text-only baseline is competitive on evaluated memory benchmarks.

MemInStream's answer is an evidence-controlled validation protocol. Every question is annotated with temporally localized evidence intervals, and a question survives validation only if models can solve it when the evidence is given (memory solvability) yet cannot resolve it from recent observations, post-evidence observations, or the textual input alone (memory necessity). This disentangles memory limitations from understanding failures and strips out memory-independent shortcuts.

The resulting benchmark organizes streaming video memory into four categories and 17 fine-grained tasks — Episodic Retrieval, State Tracking & Updating, Multi-Evidence Integration, and Contextual Memory — spanning entity/attribute/action/spatial/temporal recall, latest-state retrieval and correction memory, ordering, counting, multi-hop binding, and dialogue-dependent identity bindings.

## Key points

- Scale and structure: 3,799 questions across 4 categories and 17 fine-grained tasks, tied to 8,217 annotated evidence intervals (2.16 per question on average; mean interval 9.98 s, median 6.0 s); 56.7% of questions need multiple evidence intervals.
- Deep temporal dependencies: median distances from latest and earliest evidence to the query are 36 s and 96 s; 34.6% of questions have their latest evidence at least 60 s before the query and 66.0% require evidence starting at least 60 s earlier.
- Strict scoring: unified single- and multiple-answer format where the model must select the complete set of correct options without knowing how many exist, scored by exact-match accuracy of the full set.
- StreamForest is the strongest online model (best average across all four categories; 37.06% on State Tracking & Updating and 37.34% on Multi-Evidence Integration, above the evaluated proprietary models), yet overall accuracy stays low across many tasks.
- Hardest tasks: Multi-hop Memory Binding reaches only 13.07% (Gemini-3.8-Flash, 30 frames) and 23.86% (StreamForest); StreamForest scores 52.54% on Entity–Identity Binding but just 18.28% on Conversational Context Memory.
- More frames help offline models on episodic retrieval (Gemini-3.8-Flash 26.38%→44.96%, Doubao Seed-2.1-Pro 20.93%→38.63% from 4 to 30 frames) but not uniformly across categories; swapping Qwen2.5-VL for Qwen3-VL lifts FluxMem and Response-G1 across all four categories.
- Memory augmentation is measurable: JoyAI-VL-Interaction with memory enabled at a fixed 60-frame/2 fps budget gains +9.2 points on Episodic Retrieval (31.8→41.0), +15.3 on State Tracking (28.6→43.9), +10.3 on Multi-Evidence Integration (22.3→32.6), and +2.8 on Contextual Memory (26.9→29.7).

## Details

Construction runs candidate collection, question generation, evidence annotation, and quality validation, with evidence-controlled checks on timestamp correctness, interval relevance/completeness, and answer consistency; ambiguous or inconsistent questions are dropped. Analysis sections quantify the protocol's premises: accuracy jumps sharply when the input window fully covers the annotated evidence (Gemini-3.8-Flash 90.6%/86.2% at full overlap with 4/30 frames versus 21.8%/19.8% with no overlap; Doubao 19.2%→76.3% under 30 frames), and models with short recent-video windows collapse once evidence falls outside them — AURA, which sees only ~30 s of recent video, drops from 36.6% to 18.0% between the 10–30 s and 30–60 s bins in one distance analysis, while StreamForest (44.1%), Response-G1 (32.2%), and MOSS (33.9%) hold up at ≥300 s distances. The authors note these cross-bin comparisons are suggestive rather than isolated measures of memory decay, and list as future work longer videos with greater evidence–query separation.

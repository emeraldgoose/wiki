# V-JEPA-LM: Video-Language Models That Read Dynamics Beyond Static, Text-Aligned Representations

**Source**: [arXiv:2611.17831](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17831) · **Published**: 2026-11-13
**Authors**: Keuntae Kim, Dongjun Kim, Yong Suk Choi

## Overview

World-model video encoders such as V-JEPA learn from video alone how scenes change — direction, speed, order and object state — yet the video LLMs built on top of them still follow the image–text recipe of caption and question–answer training. Because each caption or question names only a few attributes of a clip, the language model learns to retrieve those attributes rather than to read the clip. The dynamics the encoder carries therefore go unused: on identical held-out speed-ratio items, a linear probe reads the answer from frozen V-JEPA features at 87% accuracy while the VLM built on the same features answers at chance.

A team at Hanyang University first locates where the dynamics lives in a frozen encoder. In predictive encoders such as V-JEPA, reversing, mirroring or re-timing a clip moves its tokens along a change component that points the same way across videos — measured cross-video consistency 20 to 100 times higher than SigLIP's. In frame-wise, text-aligned encoders like SigLIP, no such shared component exists: time reversal merely reorders frame-wise tokens, so the arrow of time is not in any token's content and can only be computed from token positions.

The fix is counterfactual supervision that forces the language model to read the change. Pixel transformations turn public videos into answer-balanced question variants whose answers are set by the transformation itself, so two versions of a clip that differ only in the change require the change to separate their answers. Fourteen constructions in six families — sign flips (reversal, mirroring, camera moves), event order, temporal position, speed and rate, counts, and stopped or impossible events — are rendered from 33 public sources without any new manual annotation.

Trained in a single stage on 4.53M public samples with 32 visual tokens per frame, where open video LLMs use several stages with 9M to 172M samples and 64 to 256 tokens per frame on average, the resulting V-JEPA-LM models (1B to 8B on Qwen3 and Llama) lead every size class in average score across eleven video benchmarks: the 4B model scores 72.14 against 61.08 for the best open 4B and 64.79 for the best open 8B model.

## Key points

- The gap is real and measurable: on 540 held-out speed-ratio items (three classes, chance 33.3), frozen V-JEPA features give a linear probe 86.7% and the bridge of the caption+QA VLM keeps 83.5%, but the VLM's own answer sits at chance (33.7%); adding counterfactual rows lifts the answer to 92.8% with the same encoder and mixture.
- Where the dynamics lives: reversal, mirror and speed consistency (κ) is 20–100× larger in V-JEPA than SigLIP; text-aligned ViCLIP shows no shared reversal or mirror direction (κ at most 0.002), and pixel-predicting VideoMAE shares speed and mirror but not reversal (κ 0.003). SigLIP's time direction (65.3%) and speed (73.5%) are still readable by a probe from token positions — the relational route, not token content.
- The counterfactual generator renders 509k rows from 33 public sources (6.1% of sampled training rows) across 14 constructions in 6 families; each answer follows mechanically from the pixel transformation, per-source answer balance is enforced, no new annotation is needed, and 29% of rows come with an explicit same-clip counterpart holding the opposite answer.
- Single-stage recipe: a frozen V-JEPA 2.1 encoder (ViT-L for 1B/2B, ViT-g for 3B–8B) reads 32 frames; a spectral bridge (a fixed PCA rotation and rescaling, then a trainable MLP) compresses 9,216 patches to 1,024 tokens; the language model trains in full on 4.53M rows drawn from 175 public sources — 2 to 38 times less data than open video LLMs report.
- Main results (twelve scores, one evaluation protocol): 4B 72.14 vs best open 4B 61.08 and best open 8B 64.79; 2B 66.02 vs best open 2B 52.45; the Llama variants also lead their classes (68.97 on Llama-3.2-3B, 62.21 on Llama-3.2-1B). Margins concentrate on dynamics benchmarks: TOMATO 53.5 vs at most 36.5 and Vinoground video 66.8 vs 33.8 in the 4B class.
- Against Meta's unreleased V-JEPA video LLMs on the six benchmarks both report, the 8B models lead by 6.7 points (Qwen3-8B) and 4.9 (Llama-3.1-8B) while using a twentieth of the training data.
- A controlled encoder × supervision study separates the two: at a matched budget the counterfactual rows add +4.5 average (2B) and +38 on speed questions (65.3 vs 32.4 at 8k steps), whereas an arrow-of-time-only variant (61.17 final) or the mixture's 22 annotation-based temporal-QA sources (58.21) do no better than captions+QA alone (61.53).
- Generalization holds beyond the training constructions: on six held-out sets (3,294 items) V-JEPA-LM averages 94.5–96.3 against 43.5–55.5 for open models, including unseen speed ratios 1.6/2.5/4.0, an unseen corpus and an unseen simulator; on successive playback (mid-clip re-timing) it scores 92.5–99.2 vs 19.4–51.9.

## Details

Training is deliberately light: a bridge-only warm-up of 0.6–0.8% of steps keeps the language model frozen while the spectral bridge adapts, then one continuous stage trains everything at a 150–300 global batch with a 2,560-token maximum sequence. Compute is 499 H100-hours for the 4B model and 229 for the 2B (at most 650 across the family). At inference, the 1,024-token clip representation gives 2–18× fewer prefill FLOPs than same-size open models, with first-token latency of 70–182 ms. Six models are released as Llama-V-JEPA-LM-Qwen3-1.7B/4B/8B, -Llama3.2-1B, -Llama3.2-3B and -Llama3.1-8B with weights, inference code and model cards; the data generator and training code are slated to follow on GitHub.

The analysis explains when the supervision pays. The encoder decides what can be read — on direction, counting and fine actions SigLIP already trails by 19.7, 7.7 and 11.5 points before any rows are added — while the supervision decides what is read: speed, camera motion and state change gain most (the rows add 32.8 speed points on V-JEPA vs 11.4 on SigLIP). With the rows, 0.94–0.98 of the relative-speed answer is differential rather than content-driven, and counterfactual training builds a shared speed axis inside the language model (κ 0.035 to 0.648); removing the 8 estimated speed directions from every visual token drops unseen speed combinations from 98.9 to 65.7 and TOMATO from 50.9 to 46.4, while a random subspace of the same energy leaves them at 97.2 and 49.9. Explicit pairs mainly buy consistency — both videos of an unseen speed-ratio pair are answered correctly 96.7% vs 84.4%, with gains on state change (+4.5) and reversal/antonym (+4.6) against a loss on count (−3.1) — though the unpaired arm covers 22% more distinct videos, and the speed sources contain no explicit pairs at all.

Limitations are stated plainly: all models read 32 frames per clip (seconds to a few minutes), so hour-long video is out of scope; image QA, documents and OCR are not targets because V-JEPA is never aligned with text; and open models trained on 2–38× more data keep an edge on knowledge-heavy benchmarks such as NExT-QA and PerceptionTest.

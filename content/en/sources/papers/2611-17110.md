# SALA: Semantically Aligned Latent Actions Enable Dual-Axis Scaling of Embodied Foundation Models

**Source**: [huggingface](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17110)
**Date**: 2026-11-19

## Overview

Embodied foundation models such as vision-language-action (VLA) models depend on diverse robot interaction data, which are expensive to gather at scale. Egocentric human videos offer a complementary source of manipulation experience, and latent action models (LAMs) are a popular way to exploit them because they avoid explicit pixel prediction during policy learning and can be more training-efficient and cheaper at inference than world-action models. The catch is that most existing LAMs learn latent actions purely from video reconstruction, so their representations tend to cluster by visual appearance rather than by the underlying action, which makes it hard to align human motions with their robot counterparts.

SALA (Semantically Aligned Latent Actions) addresses this by building semantics into the latent action space. Rather than requiring paired human–robot clips, it uses natural-language descriptions of unpaired human and robot clips to estimate semantic similarities, then turns those similarities into soft neighborhood structures that pull semantically related human and robot latents together. A robot-side action reconstruction objective grounds the latent space directly in control. The result is a shared latent action space that provides a unified supervision signal for training an embodied foundation model on both human and robot video.

The authors report that this latent action supervision enables scaling along two axes: pre-training on increasing amounts of general human video consistently reduces downstream robot action prediction error, and post-training on increasing amounts of task-specific human video substantially improves real-world robot task success rates.

## Key points

- Targets the human-to-robot embodiment gap without paired human–robot demonstrations.
- Uses language descriptions of atomic actions in unpaired clips as the bridge between embodiments.
- Converts language-similarity estimates into soft neighborhood (softmax-over-similarities) targets to supervise cross-embodiment alignment in latent space.
- Grounds the latent space in control via a robot-only action decoder that reconstructs action chunks from latents and initial robot state.
- Shows dual-axis scaling: more general human video in pre-training lowers robot action prediction error; more task-specific human video in post-training raises real-world success rates.
- Avoids world-action-model-style pixel prediction, aiming for better training efficiency and lower inference cost.

## Details

**Method.** SALA trains a latent action tokenizer on a human video dataset and an independently collected robot dataset. Each clip is represented by endpoint observations; robot data additionally carry the initial state and subsequent action chunk. Human and robot clips share a visual encoder, a quantizer, and a visual decoder trained with visual reconstruction losses. On top of this shared backbone, a robot-only action decoder reconstructs the action chunk from the latent and the initial state, grounding robot latents in control; human clips pass through the same space without action labels.

For cross-embodiment alignment, SALA encodes each clip's atomic-action language description with a frozen text encoder and measures pairwise semantic similarity between human and robot clips in a batch. Because exact counterparts rarely exist, each human clip is described by a soft neighborhood over the robot clips (a softmax over language similarities), and a cross-entropy alignment loss matches that language-derived neighborhood distribution to the corresponding neighborhood in latent space, with the robot latents treated as stop-gradient anchors. This robot-anchored semantic transfer pushes human latent actions toward semantically related, control-grounded robot latents instead of merely visually similar ones.

The resulting aligned latent action space is then used as a unified supervision signal to train an embodied foundation model on both human and robot videos.

**Results (as reported by the authors).** The paper reports two scaling findings: (1) pre-training on progressively more general human video consistently reduces robot action prediction error on downstream tasks, with no additional robot data; (2) post-training on progressively more task-specific human video substantially improves real-world robot task success rates. Specific benchmark numbers are not reproduced here; see the original paper for quantitative tables and experimental setup.

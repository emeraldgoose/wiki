# PolyRoute: A Twelve-Language and Code-Mixed Benchmark for Typed-Decision Routing, with Baselines and a Leaderboard

**Source**: [arXiv:2611.13843](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13843) · **Published**: 2026-11-10

## Overview

Typed-decision encoders answer a question about a text in a single forward pass — the workhorse pattern behind support-ticket routing and similar triage systems — but their behavior outside English is rarely measured on a fixed yardstick. PolyRoute is a released benchmark and protocol built to close that gap: 322 native-language support messages covering 12 languages and 9 writing systems, 156 code-mixed or romanised messages (Hinglish, Spanglish, Arabizi), 967 optional native-language training rows, and a task protocol for a three-way department-routing decision. The authors are explicit that all texts and labels are LLM-generated.

Label quality is handled with a two-stage pipeline. Candidate labels come from Qwen3.8-27B with a blind second pass, and 91% of candidates survive it; an independent model, gemma4-31b, agrees with the kept labels on 97.2% of test items. To rule out memorization inflating scores, the largest character 5-gram similarity between any test text and any training text is 0.33, so success cannot be explained by near-duplicate strings.

The 13-system leaderboard quantifies the multilingual gap. The English-root checkpoint scores 61.2%, the shipped multilingual checkpoint 77.3%, and the best fine-tuned system 96.6% — and a classical TF-IDF with logistic regression reaches 91.9%, well above the shipped multilingual checkpoint. Generalization is probed with four withheld languages, where the three multilingual systems score 77%, 90%, and 93%. On code-mixed text the English root (85%) is nominally ahead of the shipped multilingual checkpoint (79%), though the paper flags p=0.134 — not a significant difference.

Two measurement caveats make the benchmark unusually honest. First, the split resolves differences only above roughly 4 points, so leaderboard gaps smaller than that should not be over-read. Second, because the text is LLM-generated, results measure performance on this specific constructed distribution rather than on organic support traffic.

## Key points

- Task: a three-way department-routing decision made in one forward pass by a typed-decision encoder, evaluated across native-language, code-mixed, and romanised text.
- Scale: 322 native-language messages in 12 languages and 9 writing systems, plus 156 code-mixed or romanised messages (Hinglish, Spanglish, Arabizi) and 967 optional native training rows.
- Labels: generated by Qwen3.8-27B, kept by a blind second pass at a 91% retention rate, with independent gemma4-31b agreement on 97.2% of test items.
- Leakage control: the largest character 5-gram overlap between any test text and any training text is 0.33.
- Leaderboard (13 systems): English-root checkpoint 61.2%, shipped multilingual checkpoint 77.3%, best fine-tune 96.6%.
- Classical baseline: TF-IDF with logistic regression reaches 91.9%, comfortably above the shipped multilingual checkpoint — fine-tuning on native rows matters more than model family for this task shape.
- Withheld-language generalization: the three multilingual systems score 77%, 90%, and 93% on four languages kept out of the split.
- Code-mixed text is the one place the English root stays competitive: 85% versus 79% for the shipped multilingual checkpoint, with p=0.134, so the nominal lead is not significant.
- Comparability rule: the test split resolves system differences only above about 4 points; smaller gaps are within measurement resolution.
- Transparency: all texts and labels are LLM-generated, stated as part of the release rather than hidden.

## Details

- The paper (25 pages, 18 figures, 26 tables; cs.CL, cross-listed cs.AI; v1 submitted 10 Nov 2026) positions PolyRoute as a benchmark plus protocol, with the leaderboard intended as a fixed yardstick for non-English routing behavior.
- The 96.6% best fine-tune versus 61.2% English-root gap is the clearest single takeaway: without multilingual training data, an English-only router misroutes roughly one in three non-English messages, while fine-tuned systems close the task almost entirely.
- The 77.3% shipped multilingual checkpoint versus 91.9% TF-IDF+logistic-regression comparison suggests teams should not assume a larger multilingual checkpoint beats a properly fitted classical pipeline on this distribution.
- Withheld-language results (77/90/93) show the fine-tuned and stronger multilingual systems degrade gracefully on unseen languages, but the 77% floor still corresponds to roughly one misroute in four.
- The 0.33 maximum 5-gram similarity and 97.2% independent label agreement are the two leakage/quality controls worth copying when constructing similar LLM-generated evaluation sets.
- The ~4-point split resolution means the English-root lead on code-mixed text (85 vs 79) should be treated as unresolved by this benchmark rather than as evidence of code-mixed superiority.

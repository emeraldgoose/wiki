# Unbundling Multimodal Vision Encoding

**Source**: [arXiv:2611.17334](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17334) · **Published**: 2026-11-12
**Authors**: Ellis Brown, Chris Hoang, Shengyi Qian, Shang-Wen Li, Mike Lewis, Marjan Ghazvininejad, Luke Zettlemoyer, Lucas Beyer, Mengye Ren, Jakob Verbeek, Saining Xie, Hu Xu

## Overview

How a multimodal language model should encode visual input is still contested: most models bolt on a pretrained vision encoder, some recent recipes train the encoder from scratch alongside the language model, and others drop the encoder entirely. The authors observe that published models cannot settle this debate because a vision encoder bundles three design choices at once — a visual prior from upstream pretraining, dedicated vision capacity that only vision tokens use, and pre-fusion placement of that capacity before the multimodal backbone — while published systems also differ in data, architecture and compute.

The paper therefore pretrains everything from scratch under one controlled protocol. Four recipes share one backbone architecture and common data: a pretrained encoder, a scratch encoder, an encoder-free model, and an in-fusion variant that puts vision-only capacity inside the backbone's earliest layers. Comparisons happen at matched lifetime compute, the sum of every training stage that produces the final model, evaluated on visual question answering and language benchmarks. Main comparisons use the Qwen3 1.7B and 4B configurations with dedicated vision capacity fixed at 0.4B parameters, and a scaling study runs a 0.6B to 6.4B ladder.

Three results organize the findings. The visual prior is not necessary: a jointly trained scratch encoder matches a pretrained one without a separate stage. What matters is dedicated vision capacity: an encoder-free model trails on visual understanding even when its backbone is enlarged by the encoder's parameter count. And that capacity need not precede fusion: vision-only modules added in-fusion to early backbone layers perform comparably to a pre-fusion encoder and can lower time to first token, since no separate encoder must run before the backbone.

## Key points

- Four recipes are compared — pretrained encoder, scratch encoder, encoder-free, in-fusion — differing only in how visual input is encoded; everything is trained from scratch with no external pretrained models.
- Main comparisons use Qwen3 1.7B and 4B backbones with dedicated vision capacity fixed at 0.4B parameters; the scaling study uses a 0.6B to 6.4B ladder that grows vision capacity in proportion to the backbone.
- Lifetime compute spans budgets from 6.5e20 to 2.9e22 FLOPs, or 56B to 1.11T token-equivalents, reaching 788 tokens per parameter at 1.7B and 306 at 4B.
- Motivating the metric: SigLIP 2, a common encoder choice, is trained on 40B image-text samples, about 10 trillion image tokens, which counting only multimodal training would hide.
- Training data mixes text-only and image-text sequences 70:30; text comes from Nemotron-CC v2 and v2.1, image-text pairs from MetaCLIP 1.2 (English subset), with captions regenerated by Qwen2.5-VL-32B and shared by every recipe.
- Finding one: the visual prior is not necessary — a scratch encoder trained jointly is on par with a pretrained encoder, and even a free pretrained encoder does not help a backbone trained from scratch.
- Finding two: dedicated vision capacity is key — the encoder-free model trails on visual understanding even when its backbone is widened by the encoder's 0.4B parameters.
- Finding three: vision capacity can move in-fusion — adding vision-only capacity to the earliest backbone layers is comparable to a pre-fusion encoder, and scaling laws show in-fusion tracking the encoder while encoder-free trails at every size.
- Finding four: the comparisons carry over — starting from a pretrained 1.7B language model via midtraining leaves them unchanged, and in-fusion can lower time to first token.
- Serving benchmark on one H200 with 16 concurrent requests, each with one 448-pixel image and 256 text tokens: encoder-free takes 146 ms at 1.7B (31 percent less than the Large encoder) and 340 ms at 4B (16 percent less).
- The encoder recipe is 3 percent slower to train at 4B, and its separate stack uses 27 narrower layers at the 0.4B budget versus in-fusion's eight wide layers.
- Evaluation averages 12 language benchmarks for Avg. Text, including MMLU, ARC-Easy and Challenge, HellaSwag, PIQA, WinoGrande, BoolQ and RACE.

## Details

### The controlled protocol

The fusion boundary is defined as the first layer in which vision and text tokens interact: computation before it is pre-fusion, computation inside the shared backbone is in-fusion. Each image's vision tokens attend bidirectionally among themselves and text tokens attend causally to all earlier tokens. A warmup-stable-decay schedule lets one run supply many training lengths, with any checkpoint decayed to 5 percent of peak learning rate over the final 10 percent of training. Peak learning rates are 3e-4 at 1.7B and 2e-4 at 4B, scaled with the inverse square root of total parameter count.

### What matters and what does not

At matched lifetime compute, separate encoder pretraining is optional, and adding more encoder pretraining improves recognition but not VQA even when upstream compute is treated as free. Widening or deepening the encoder-free backbone by 0.4B parameters does not close the visual understanding gap, so the encoder's advantage lies in capacity dedicated to vision rather than total parameter count. In-fusion's budget partly sits in vision-only attention projections, which help on their own in additional 4B ablations.

### Serving and training speed

A separate encoder serializes inference: each image must finish encoding before the backbone starts, and the saving grows with resolution because more vision tokens make the encoder a larger share of time to first token. MoT, which copies the first k backbone layers for vision tokens, is about as fast as in-fusion up to 0.4B vision-only parameters but its added latency grows faster with budget. Under continuous batching of mixed text and image requests, in-fusion's vision-only modules run in the same forward passes as text-only requests, whereas a separate encoder adds latency only to image requests.

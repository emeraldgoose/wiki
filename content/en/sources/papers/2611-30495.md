# DexDuet: Learning Contact-Preserving Bimanual Dexterity from Human Demonstrations

**Source**: [arXiv:2611.30495](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.30495) · **Published**: 2026-11-23 (v1)
**Authors**: Haoran Zhang, Haokai Ding, Han Zhou, Zuxing Lu, Heng Zhang, Xingxing Zuo

## Overview

People routinely manipulate objects with both hands — holding a bottle while opening its lid — and learning such bimanual skills from human demonstrations is a natural route to robotic manipulation. But robot hands differ from human hands in size, kinematics, and actuation, so demonstrations cannot be replayed directly. Recent methods bridge the gap by retargeting human motion to robot hands and training in simulation to track the demonstrated object trajectory. Their fundamental limitation is that object tracking alone does not determine how the hands interact with the object: even a robot that tracks the trajectory accurately can touch the wrong object parts or slide its fingers, making the manipulation unstable.

DexDuet instead treats contact preservation as a first-class objective: whenever a human hand holds an object part (touches it with stationary contact points), the corresponding robot hand must hold the same part. The objective is formalized as minimizing object-state error subject to a bound on contact drift over a window of frames. DexDuet addresses three challenges — coordinated yet role-specific bimanual control, preserving human contacts across different robot embodiments, and difficult manipulation phases — with three components. (1) Two interaction rewards compare each robot hand with the human hand relative to the object. (2) The bimanual policy is factorized into two per-hand policies that share one network and condition on each other's observations, trained with per-hand PPO clipping so one hand's update is not blocked by the other's. (3) A-RSI (assisted reference-state initialization) is an adaptive curriculum that keeps a separate object-assistance level per demonstration segment — lowering it where segments succeed and raising it where they fail, under a decreasing global ceiling — and starts episodes preferentially in high-failure segments, from keyframes the policy has already reached.

## Key points

- Benchmarks: four suites over three simulators and five robot embodiments — DexMachina suite (7 ARCTIC demonstrations of 5 articulated objects, Genesis simulator, Inspire/Allegro/XHand/Schunk hands), SPIDER suite (7 ARCTIC demos, Genesis, Inspire), ManipTrans suite (10 OakInk2 demos, Isaac Gym, Inspire, 4,096 parallel envs), and the V2D challenge (30 episodes, MuJoCo Warp at 200 Hz, Unitree G1 humanoid with Dex3 hands). Baselines: DexMachina, SPIDER, ManipTrans (retrained), ConTrack (rerun), CHORD on V2D.
- Headline: across the four benchmarks, DexDuet improves object-tracking ADD-AUC by 11–41 points over the corresponding baselines while consistently achieving better contact preservation (Contact-AUC, which scores how little each hand's contact points drift on the held part over 6-frame windows, threshold grid 0.5–5 cm).
- Matched-tracking test: among 122 paired runs whose ADD-AUCs differ by at most 3 points (e.g., ~94 on the DexMachina suite), DexDuet preserves the human's contacts better in 117 cases; a logistic regression on the pair choice gives Contact-AUC a large positive coefficient (3.79 ± 0.45) vs. a small negative one for ADD-AUC (−0.61 ± 0.27). On the DexMachina suite DexDuet's mean ADD-AUC is 88.7 vs. DexMachina's 52.5, with higher Contact-AUC on all seven demonstrations.
- Example per-hand results (DexMachina suite, XHand): DexDuet mean ADD-AUC 93.6 vs. DexMachina 60.7 and ConTrack 30.7; Contact-AUC 79.5 vs. 38.2 and 14.4.
- Ablations (DexMachina, Inspire): removing both interaction rewards drops Contact-AUC from 80.5 to 49.0; removing only the hand-object contact reward drops it to 65.4 and only the hand-graph reward to 71.5. A unified (non-factorized) policy drops Contact-AUC to 62.1, and replacing A-RSI with a single-level VOC curriculum drops it to 74.2 (and stalls on Ketchup-300, where A-RSI reaches zero assistance by ~0.77× of the update cap).
- Interaction rewards: hand-graph reward aligns palm normal, palm direction, and each finger's base-to-tip direction plus fingertip gaps between robot and human hand (both expressed relative to the object); hand-object contact reward compares, per hand region (palm, thumb, each finger) and object part, the distance to the part and the closest point on it.
- A-RSI mechanics: per-segment assistance h_b (VOC gain levels), adjusted every 40 updates from segment success rates (thresholds 0.8/0.4), never above a ceiling that decays over updates; episode start segments drawn from p(b) favoring high failure rate, with 25% forced starts at frame 0; keyframe starts restore stored states (capacity 256) only from episodes that later succeeded, avoiding failure-reproducing restarts.
- Compute: 4,350–11,500 PPO updates per policy (1.67–4.42B transitions on DexMachina/SPIDER, 0.58–1.51B on ManipTrans); a typical DexMachina run is ~21.7 h on one GPU. Evaluation from frame 0, no assistance, deterministic actions (100 rollouts in Genesis, 256 per demo on ManipTrans, official evaluator ×4 runs on V2D).
- Real-world replays: on a Unitree G1 with Inspire hands the robot lowers and places a box, carries and puts down a bag, and pushes an office chair while walking; on two Franka arms with Allegro hands, both hands reorient a bottle held above a table and place a gamepad held by both hands onto a block.

## Details

Problem formulation: given a T-frame human demonstration with hand poses and object-part poses, learn a policy reproducing it under two requirements — the object follows demonstrated states (object error averaged over sampled points on each part in the part frame) and robot hands preserve human hand-object contacts (contact drift over Δ frames bounded by δ_max). DexDuet's policy factorizes the joint action as a product of two Gaussian per-hand policies sharing one actor network; each maps its own observation (joint state, wrist pose, keypoints, object state and tracking error, previous action) plus the partner's state and previous action to joint position targets. Training uses PPO with the probability ratio clipped per hand (not jointly), with a centralized critic and per-hand value heads.

The base reward (object tracking + retargeted reference-motion imitation) is augmented with λ_hg·r_hg + λ_hoc·r_hoc. The hand-graph reward builds a graph on hand keypoints whose edges are unit vectors (palm normal, wrist-to-middle-finger-base, each finger base-to-tip); direction error compares edge alignment and gap error compares fingertip distances (thumb-to-finger and neighboring-finger pairs, scaled by κ to account for hand size). The hand-object contact reward divides each hand into palm/thumb/finger regions and, for each region and object part, compares the region-to-part distance (capped at d_max) and the closest point on the part between human and robot, using only retargeting-matched fingers when the robot hand has fewer fingers. On Isaac Gym (which reports only net contact force), force on a hand link is attributed to the part within 1.5 cm.

A-RSI replaces uniform assistance schedules: each ~15-frame segment keeps its own assistance level, updated from per-segment failure rates; episodes start from stored keyframes (captured with probability 0.002 per step only from episodes that reach the end with mean tracking reward ≥ 0.5) or from reference states drawn uniformly in the start segment. Compared with DexMachina's single-level VOC schedule, A-RSI reaches zero assistance by update ≈ 0.77×U_cap on the DexMachina suite and trains much shorter on hard demos like Ketchup-300 where the single level stalls.

Contact-AUC metric: hold windows are (hand, part, frame) triples where the human hand touches the part at both ends of a 6-frame window with human drift < 1 cm (contact = within 5 mm); robot drift is set to infinity if no point touches or the object is lost (>5 cm mean ADD error). Each window is scored by the area under 1[δ̂ < τ] for τ from 0.5 to 5 cm on a 0.5 cm grid (same rule as ADD-AUC), and Contact-AUC is the mean over windows and rollouts. It agrees with human raters' judgments. Limitations noted by the authors include embodiment gaps in contact mapping (fewer-fingered hands use only matched fingers) and the need to attribute contacts from net forces on PhysX-based simulators.

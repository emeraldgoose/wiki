# Identically Trained Language Models Break on Different Jailbreaks

**Source**: [arXiv:2610.34074](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.34074) · **Published**: 2026-10-25
**Authors**: Chi-Wei Chang, Mu-Tien Kuo, Kuan-Wei Lee, Meng-Hsun Tsai

## Overview

Jailbreak benchmarks typically evaluate one checkpoint and then read the set of jailbreaks that break it as a property of its pretraining data and its safety training. The paper asks the counterfactual directly: would a second pretraining run over the same data, given the same safety post-training, break on the same jailbreaks? Pretraining is stochastic, and earlier work shows that runs differing only in random seed can reach nearly identical aggregate accuracy while disagreeing on individual predictions and on measured biases. Safety research has varied fine-tuning seeds, sampling seeds and temperatures, and even the last one percent of a pretraining run, but never a whole pretraining run.

To test that, the authors build on DataDecide, which pretrains a 1B parameter model on each of 25 data recipes with 3 pretraining seeds. Every base model then receives the same safety post-training under 2 post-training seeds, producing 150 models in all. Each model gives 20 responses to a shared set of 3,212 jailbreak prompts, and the authors compute the Pearson correlation of per-prompt non-refusal rates between every pair of models. Pairs are grouped by shared ancestry: two models that share a base model and differ only in their post-training seed are dubbed MZ twins, while a same-recipe pair shares the data recipe but not the pretraining run, and a cross-recipe pair shares neither.

The comparison separates the pretraining run from its data. Two post-trained copies of one pretraining run correlate at 0.984 on average. Changing the pretraining seed lowers the correlation to 0.811, and changing the data recipe as well lowers it only slightly further, to 0.779, so almost all of the disagreement comes from rerunning pretraining rather than from the data itself. The ordering holds inside all 25 data recipes and at 150M and 300M parameters, and it survives a judge validated against human labels. In practical terms, the best of 91 attack templates found on one checkpoint stays the best after a new pretraining seed in only 27% of model pairs, against 67% after a new post-training seed, and per-prompt non-refusal rates fingerprint the pretraining run: across 3,211 prompts they identify it among all 75 runs every time, and a random subset of 50 prompts still does so 98% of the time.

## Key points

- Which jailbreaks break a model follows its pretraining run, not its data: copies of one run correlate at 0.984, while a new pretraining seed drops that to 0.811 and a new data recipe only to 0.779.
- The same ordering holds in all 25 data recipes and at both 150M and 300M parameters, and the judge agrees with human labels.
- Retraining one run twice more from its own seed on new hardware correlates barely better with the original than a same-recipe pair, so MZ twins agree because they share a trained run rather than its seed.
- Attack templates transfer poorly across pretraining runs: the best of 91 templates stays best after a new pretraining seed in only 27% of pairs, versus 67% after a new post-training seed.
- Refusal patterns identify a run: 3,211 prompts pick out a model's pretraining run among all 75 runs every time, and 50 random prompts still do 98% of the time.
- The study varies an entire pretraining run, which earlier safety work had never done, since it varied only seeds applied after pretraining or a final slice of one run.
- The authors conclude that a checkpoint's jailbreak vulnerability should be read as a signature of its training run, not of its corpus.

## Details

DataDecide supplies the substrate. A data recipe there is a released pretraining corpus together with its filtering, deduplication and mixing choices, and a pretraining seed sets initialization and data order together; each recipe contributes three 1B runs trained on the same 100B tokens. Adding two post-training seeds per base model yields the 150 models. All of them answer the same prompt set with 20 sampled responses each, and a judge decides refusal per response; the authors check that judge against human labels and repeat the comparison at smaller model sizes.

The correlation numbers carry the argument. Sharing a base model but not a post-training seed gives 0.984 on average across data recipes, sharing only the recipe gives 0.811, and sharing neither gives 0.779. Because the drop comes almost entirely from rerunning pretraining, the authors test whether the seed or the run itself is responsible by pretraining one run twice more from its own seed on their own hardware and software; the new models correlate with the original and with each other barely more than a same-recipe pair does. They read this as evidence that MZ twins agree because they share a trained run, not because they share a seed.

Two consequences are drawn out. Attack transfer across pretraining runs is unreliable, so a red team's best template found on one checkpoint says little about the next run, whereas a new post-training seed preserves it far more often. And refusal behavior is itself identifying, which the authors treat as a fingerprint left by the pretraining run and a reason to report which run a benchmark evaluated.

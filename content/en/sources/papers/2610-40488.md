# rTaste: Do Research Agents Have Taste? Evaluating Early Stage Research Judgment

**Source**: [arXiv:2610.40488](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40488) · **Published**: 2026-10-27
**Authors**: Art Kanke

## Overview

Research agents can execute much of the empirical workflow, but it is less clear whether they can decide which experiments are worth running before outcomes are known. rTaste evaluates early-stage research judgment by giving six frontier models seven recent NLP papers and asking them to propose five minimum viable experiments from each.

Each model works both as a single researcher and as a research team. It proposes minimum viable experiments (MVEs), predicts whether each will pass a threshold it sets, and ranks them by predicted return. Across seven seed papers and 415 MVEs, 194 are judged sound, valuable, and new.

## Key points

- Tests research judgment before experimental execution rather than only task completion.
- Covers seven seed papers and 415 proposed minimum viable experiments.
- Distinguishes MVEs by soundness, value, novelty, and obviousness.
- Larger models propose roughly three sound, valuable, new MVEs out of five.
- Two smaller models propose about one such MVE per seed on average.
- Two models produce many MVEs judged beyond the seed paper and non-obvious.
- Frames early-stage judgment as a distinct capability for research agents.

## Details

Existing benchmarks often specify a fixed task and measure completion, or evaluate ideas before they are executed. rTaste instead asks which experiments an agent should propose, how accurately it forecasts outcomes, and how it chooses among proposals. It also compares single-researcher and team settings for the same agent.

The evaluation highlights differences among frontier models in proposing valuable and non-obvious work. It suggests that proposing good experiments is a measurable skill that can be separated from implementation success.

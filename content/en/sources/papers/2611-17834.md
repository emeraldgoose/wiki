# PaperCritic: Training AI Reviewers to Assess the Soundness of Papers

**Source**: [huggingface](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17834)
**Date**: 2026-11-19

## Overview

As AI conference submissions grow rapidly, AI reviewers are being considered as a way to keep up with review demand. The authors argue that simply imitating human reviewing is the wrong target: peer review serves several purposes at once — assessing soundness, interestingness, and clarity — and the latter two are inherently subjective. Instead, this work focuses solely on soundness, which the authors treat as the most important and most objective criterion, and reframes AI reviewer training as a precision–recall problem over individual review points.

Under that formulation, the authors build PaperSound-23K, a quality-labeled dataset of 23K review points drawn from 1K ICLR submissions, combining human-written and LLM-generated (synthetic) review points with labels for soundness, claim linkage, and severity. They then train an AI reviewer model, PaperCritic, with reinforcement learning, using the F1 score of precision and recall as the reward.

Experiments show that PaperCritic outperforms frontier models and models trained with other AI reviewer training methods on an in-domain test set. Perhaps more surprisingly, learning to review ICLR submissions transfers to reviewing Nature-family papers and even to out-of-domain reviewing tasks such as code review, legal contract review, and feedback on story writing.

## Key points

- Reframes AI reviewing as joint maximization of precision (soundness and substantiveness of generated review points) and recall (coverage of high-quality reference review points), rather than holistic review-and-score imitation.
- Introduces PaperSound-23K: 23K quality-labeled review points for 1K ICLR (2020–2023) submissions, sourced from OpenReview, with ~62% of points human-written and the remainder LLM-generated.
- Trains PaperCritic via reinforcement learning with F1 of precision and recall as the reward, using a many-to-many matching between generated and reference review points for the recall computation.
- Labels each review point for soundness, claim linkage, and severity; human–model agreement is close to human–human agreement (e.g., Spearman 0.654 vs 0.640 for soundness).
- In-domain results beat frontier baselines and prior AI reviewer training methods.
- Transfers to Nature-family papers and to out-of-domain tasks: code review, legal contract review, and story-writing feedback.
- Authors: Hyun Ryu (KAIST AI, CMU LTI), Ji Yong Cho, Eunho Yang, Moontae Lee, June Yong Yang (LG AI Research), Sean Welleck and Carolyn Rosé (CMU LTI).

## Details

**Method.** Instead of training on whole reviews, the paper defines review generation as a multi-objective problem: maximize both the precision of generated review points (how sound and substantive they are) and recall (the fraction of unique high-quality reference review points matched by at least one generated point, with many-to-many matching allowed). The pipeline: (0) sample 1K ICLR 2020–2023 submissions from OpenReview with proportionate stratified sampling across year, acceptance status, and research area; (1) collect papers and human reviews via the OpenReview API and convert PDFs to text with DeepSeek-OCR, plus LLM-generated synthetic reviews to cover concerns humans miss; (2) extract review points with an LLM (regex parsing for structured synthetic reviews), filtering out non-soundness points and points with external citations; (3) extract 3–6 main claims per paper and 1–4 pieces of evidence per claim, human-verified. Inter-annotator agreement between humans and the model is measured with Spearman correlations across soundness, claim linkage, and severity, and the aggregated high-quality (binary) label.

**Dataset statistics.** PaperSound-23K is split into 889 training papers (20,933 review points, avg 23.55/paper) and 100 test papers (2,282 points, avg 22.82/paper). Quality-label distribution over the full set: 6,286 sound and substantive, 6,483 sound but not substantive, 10,446 not sound.

**Results.** PaperCritic outperforms frontier models and models trained with other AI reviewer training methods on the in-domain test set. The learned soundness-reviewing behavior also transfers to Nature-family papers and to out-of-domain reviewing tasks — code review, legal contract review, and feedback on story writing — suggesting the model internalizes a general notion of soundness assessment rather than venue-specific review style.

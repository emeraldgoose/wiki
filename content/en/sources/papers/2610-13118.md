# The value of information and the emergence of communication in evolved e-puck robots

**Source**: Montes-Gonzalez, arXiv, 2026-10-09

**Lead**: This paper investigates how communication emerges in evolved robot teams by framing it as a value-of-information problem. Using e-puck robots in a collective foraging task, the author shows that communication signals evolve when the benefit of sharing food location information outweighs the metabolic and neural costs of producing and processing signals. The evolved communication reduces collective search time and demonstrates that communication is an adaptive strategy for managing information value in cooperative multi-agent systems.

## Background

Understanding the evolutionary origins of communication is a fundamental question in biology and robotics. Prior work has shown that communication can evolve in simulated agents when it provides a fitness advantage, but the precise conditions—particularly the role of information value versus cost—remain underexplored. This study uses embodied evolution with physical e-puck robots to ground the analysis in real-world sensorimotor constraints, avoiding the "reality gap" of pure simulation.

The foraging arena contains randomly placed food sources. Robots must locate and collect food while minimizing energy expenditure. Each robot has a neural controller evolved via NEAT (NeuroEvolution of Augmenting Topologies) that processes local sensory inputs (proximity sensors, ground sensors for food detection) and can emit a binary communication signal received by nearby robots. The signal has no predefined meaning; its semantics emerge from the evolutionary process.

## Experimental Design

**Robot Platform**: e-puck robots with 8 infrared proximity sensors, 3 ground sensors (for food detection), a speaker for emitting a tone (communication signal), and a microphone for receiving it. Controllers are feedforward neural networks evolved onboard.

**Task**: Collective foraging. 4 robots operate simultaneously in a 1.5m × 1.5m arena with 4 food sources. Each trial runs for 300 seconds. Fitness is the total food collected by the team.

**Evolutionary Algorithm**: NEAT with a population of 100 genomes, evolved for 200 generations. Each genome is evaluated in 5 trials with different food placements. The communication signal is a binary tone (on/off) emitted at each timestep; nearby robots (within ~30cm) receive it as an additional input.

**Conditions**: Two main treatments:
- **Communication allowed**: Robots can emit and receive signals.
- **Communication disabled**: Signal emission/reception is disabled (control).

**Information Value Manipulation**: Food distribution varies across experimental blocks:
- **Clustered**: Food sources close together (high local information value).
- **Dispersed**: Food sources far apart (lower local information value).
- **Dynamic**: Food sources move during the trial (changing information value over time).

## Results

**Communication Emerges When Information Is Valuable**: In the clustered and dynamic conditions, communication evolves rapidly (by generation ~50) and is maintained. In the dispersed condition, communication either fails to evolve or is lost. This matches the value-of-information hypothesis: signaling is only adaptive when a robot's local discovery significantly reduces search time for others.

**Signal Semantics**: Evolved signals function as "food found" beacons. Robots that discover food emit the signal persistently; receivers orient toward the signal source. Signal production correlates with ground-sensor activation (food detection), not random emission.

**Collective Performance**: Teams with communication collect 23–37% more food than non-communicating controls in clustered/dynamic conditions. In dispersed conditions, performance is equivalent, confirming that communication is not universally beneficial—it is a conditional adaptation.

**Cost Sensitivity**: When an explicit metabolic cost is added to signal emission (fitness penalty per timestep signaling), communication evolves only when the information benefit exceeds the cost threshold. This demonstrates a sharp transition: below the threshold, communication is ubiquitous; above it, communication disappears entirely.

**Neural Economy**: Controllers that evolve communication are not significantly larger than non-communicating ones, suggesting that the neural overhead of processing the signal is minimal compared to the foraging policy itself. The signal integrates into existing sensorimotor pathways rather than requiring a dedicated "communication module."

## Relevance to Software Engineers

- **Value-of-information as a design principle**: In multi-agent systems (swarm robotics, distributed sensors, agent-based simulations), communication should be triggered by an explicit estimate of information value versus transmission/processing cost, not by fixed schedules or heuristics.
- **Emergent protocols beat predefined ones**: The evolved signals carry exactly the information needed for the task—no more, no less. Hand-designed protocols often over-communicate (wasting bandwidth) or under-communicate (missing critical events). Letting agents discover their own signaling semantics via reinforcement or evolution can yield more efficient protocols.
- **Embodied constraints matter**: The physical communication range (~30cm), signal modality (audio), and sensor noise shaped the evolved behavior. Simulators that abstract these away may produce communication strategies that fail on hardware.
- **Conditional communication is robust**: The sharp cost-benefit transition means systems can be tuned to communicate only when it matters, automatically adapting to task changes (e.g., food distribution shifts) without retraining.

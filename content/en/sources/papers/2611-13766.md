# FAME-QP: A GPU-Accelerated Quadratic-Programming Solver for Real-Time Model Predictive Control

**Source**: [arXiv:2611.13766](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13766) · **Published**: 2026-11-10
**Authors**: Santosh Mohan Rajkumar, Debdipta Goswami, Stephanie Stockar

## Overview

Linear model predictive control turns every control step into a convex quadratic program, and the QP must be solved inside the sampling period. Interior-point and active-set solvers either struggle to warm start or scale badly with constraint count, while ADMM-based solvers such as OSQP and ProxQP warm start easily but iterate; on top of this, modern robotics pipelines already live on the GPU for simulation, inference, and learning, so routing every QP through a CPU solver adds transfer latency. GPU QP solvers exist — cuOSQP, qpax, CuPIQP, and the closest prior work ReLU-QP, which recasts ADMM as a weight-tied network with pre-inverted dense operators — but they carry dense-operator storage and dispatch overheads, and ReLU-QP's offline precomputation resists online matrix changes.

FAME-QP (Fused ADMM with Multimode Execution for QP solution), from Ohio State's Mechanical & Aerospace Engineering department, keeps the pre-inversion and penalty-bank idea of ReLU-QP but attacks its overheads on three fronts. First, constraint-dependent operators: constraint rows that act on individual decision variables (box constraints, and the diagonal block of split constraints) are absorbed elementwise instead of being stored densely, cutting operator storage by up to 4x and iteration time by up to 3x. Second, execution: the whole ADMM iteration is fused into either one stacked-kernel matrix-vector product or two sequential kernels over a factored operator, captured as a CUDA graph so host dispatch happens once per check interval rather than per kernel. Third, adaptation: a bank of five pre-inverted operators at penalties spaced by factors of 10 is probed at setup to pick the starting penalty, switched by selecting stored entries during the solve, and refreshed lazily — re-inverting only the active entry — when problem matrices change between solves.

The execution configuration is chosen automatically per problem shape: a device-calibrated iteration-time model (kernel-node overhead plus operator streaming time, cached per device and precision) prunes the search, and the surviving forms and tile configurations are timed inside CUDA graphs on placeholder operators of the true dimensions; the single-kernel mode is kept unless the two-kernel form measures more than 10% faster. Precision is also managed: the penalty bank is always inverted in float64, while operators and iterates can run in float32 or float64, with an automatic rule based on a power-iteration estimate of the KKT-matrix condition number and a solved_inaccurate fallback when single-precision residuals stagnate.

Experiments cover matched-accuracy cold solves on dense random QPs, closed-loop LMPC on random unstable systems, and MuJoCo control of a Unitree G1 humanoid and a Skydio X2 quadrotor with time-varying Koopman-lifted dynamics. FAME-QP posts the lowest solve latency among the GPU baselines at every tested size — 1.95 ms at n = 1400 in float32 versus 326 ms for cuOSQP — and stays below the 2 ms control period at every tested horizon in the humanoid push-recovery task, where ReLU-QP exceeds the budget from horizon 15 upward.

## Key points

- Problem class: dense convex QPs of condensed linear MPC (minimize 1/2 zᵀPz + qᵀz subject to l ≤ Gz ≤ h), solved with ADMM using the pre-inverted matrix M = (P + σI + GᵀρG)⁻¹; data is Ruiz-equilibrated once at setup in ten passes, and the penalty is parameterized as ρ·diag(b) with b = 10³ on equality rows.
- Constraint structures: box (G diagonal, m = n), split (G = [D; C] with nonsingular diagonal D plus dense rows C), and general (all rows dense). Under standard condensing, input bounds alone give box structure and adding state bounds gives split structure; LQR pre-stabilization couples input constraints into the general structure.
- Operator forms: the stacked form W = [M, MGᵀ; GM, GMGᵀ] computes both coupled updates in one fused kernel; the factored form stores F = [M, MCᵀ] and computes s̃ = Cz̃ in a second kernel. Factored saves m_c² operator entries but costs an extra kernel; box structure reduces storage from 4n² (stacked) or 3n² (factored) dense entries to n² + n.
- Penalty bank: five operators at penalties spaced by factors of 10, initially centered at ρ₀ = 0.1; in-solve adaptation applies OSQP's residual-balancing rule and snaps proposals more than a factor of 5 from the current value to the nearest stored entry; the dual variable λ is retained across switches because its optimum does not depend on ρ.
- Starting-penalty probing: at setup, each bank entry runs a trial of up to 400 iterations from zero with adaptation enabled, recording time and iterations to reach 1/100, 1/10, and 1× the requested tolerance; the fastest trial at the tightest commonly reached tolerance sets the starting ρ and the iteration estimate j_est; an endpoint win re-centers and re-probes the bank (at most two rebuilds). For split constraints the dense-row weight γ is probed over 10⁻³-10 and fixed.
- CUDA-graph replay: j_chk = 10 complete iterations plus the device-side termination test are captured as one graph; each replay needs a single host call, and the host reads only five values (residual norms, scales, stop flag) per replay; a stall is declared when residuals fail to shrink 10% within 12 checks.
- Automatic mode selection: t_iter ≈ n_ker·τ + t_stream(b_op) with τ and streaming times calibrated once per device and precision over 0.25-64 MB operator sizes; stacked form predicted faster when m_c < √(τ·BW/β); forms needing over 60% of free GPU memory or over 1.5× the best predicted time are discarded, and final selection times the survivors inside CUDA graphs, keeping single-kernel unless two-kernel measures over 10% faster.
- Warm starts and updates: between solves, new q, l, h are rescaled with the stored factors and r rebuilt; changes to P or G trigger re-inversion of the full bank or, in lazy mode, only the active entry (others marked stale until a penalty switch selects them); warm starts can shift the previous solution one stage along the horizon.
- Precision policy: bank always float64; automatic selection estimates κ(K) by power iterations and picks float32 when u₃₂κ(K) ≤ 0.01ε, float64 when u₃₂κ(K) ≥ ε, otherwise times both and prefers float64 unless float32 wins by more than 20%; stalled float32 solves return solved_inaccurate if they pass the test at 100× tolerance.
- Random-QP benchmark (accuracy matched to Clarabel at 10⁻¹⁰, ℓ∞ error ≤ 10⁻³): FAME-QP float64 beats all GPU baselines at every size and all CPU baselines for n ≥ 220; speedups 1.2-25.9× over DAQP (best CPU) and 1.5-4.3× over ReLU-QP (best GPU) for n = 220-1400; at n = 1400 mean solve time 1.95 ms in float32 and 5.01 ms in float64 versus 326 ms for cuOSQP.
- Random-system LMPC (n_u = 10-50, n_x = 3n_u, horizon 40, 50 closed-loop steps): lowest mean step time at every size, 0.658-4.23 ms; 3.2-7.3× faster than ReLU-QP and 3.2-13.8× faster than πMPC; closed-loop costs agree within 0.06% across methods, and FAME-QP's largest input-bound violation is 2.0×10⁻³ versus 1.4-4.3×10⁻² for ReLU-QP.
- Unitree G1 push recovery in MuJoCo (29 joints, 2 ms control period, 80 N/0.1 s torso push): QP time 2.3-3.1× lower than ReLU-QP (0.650 vs 1.47 ms at N = 10; 1.04 vs 3.25 ms at N = 20), staying below the control period at every tested horizon while ReLU-QP exceeds it for N ≥ 15 — doubling the largest feasible horizon from 10 to 20; balance recovery is comparable (CoM RMS 18.7-20.5 mm for both).
- Skydio X2 quadrotor KQ-LMPC (Koopman-lifted dynamics, both P and G change every step, 50 Hz, N = 70, n = 280): mean QP time 12.5 ± 3.2 ms including matrix updates, warm-start shifts, and host transfer, versus 59.2 ± 4.8 ms for CuPIQP (4.7×); 99th-percentile latency 17.6 ms inside the 20 ms period (CuPIQP: 72.1 ms); tracking position RMSE 2.7 mm versus 3.4 mm, maximum error 8.5 mm for both.
- Ablations: fusion plus graph replay cuts per-iteration time from 140 μs (unfused, eager) to 24.6 μs (graph) to 4.37 μs (fused graph) at n = 128, replacing 14 kernels with one; structure extraction cuts storage 1.67-4.00× and iteration time 1.07-3.03× with solutions unchanged to 10⁻¹²; at n = 512, m_c = 128 the stacked operator is 3.12 MiB / 17.3 μs against ReLU-QP's 24.5 MiB / 86.3 μs; automatic mode selection picks the faster form at 30 of 36 sweep points (misses within the 10% margin; never excludes the faster form), while always stacking or always factoring is up to 2.2×/1.5× slower.
- Penalty probing value: at tolerance 10⁻⁵, probed starts cut iterations 1.6-3.2× and complete-solve time 1.4-3.3×; combined with fusion the gains multiply to 2.8-4.2×, with setup cost recovered after roughly 36-122 solves; since probed FAME-QP and default ReLU-QP solve in similar iteration counts (30-38 vs 30-32), the speedup over ReLU-QP comes mainly from per-iteration cost.
- Lazy matrix updates: in the recorded quadrotor KQ-LMPC sequence, full bank refresh makes updating (not iterating) the dominant per-step cost; lazy refresh roughly halves update and median step times and shortens the tail, with identical iteration counts, solutions equal to numerical precision, and unchanged tracking error.
- Hardware and defaults: NVIDIA RTX 3080 Laptop GPU (48 SMs, 16 GB) with an Intel i7-11850H; float64, σ = 10⁻⁶, α = 1.6, equal absolute/relative tolerances, checks every 10 iterations, iteration limit 2×10⁴ (10⁵ in closed-loop LMPC and G1 experiments). Open-source code: github.com/santoshrajkumar/fame-qp.

## Details

### Algorithm structure

FAME-QP implements the standard relaxed ADMM iteration for the box-constrained reformulation of the QP. After one-time modified Ruiz equilibration, the setup phase detects the constraint structure, selects the execution mode, builds the five-entry penalty bank, captures the CUDA graph, and probes the starting penalty (and γ for split constraints). Each solve then rescales incoming data, refreshes operators if P or G changed (fully or lazily), warm-starts z and λ (reconstructing s by projecting Gz onto the box), and replays the graph until the unscaled primal and dual residuals meet the termination test, a stall is declared, or the iteration limit is hit. Because the multiplier update keeps μ at zero after initialization, the iteration reduces to one matrix-vector product with the pre-inverted M plus elementwise relaxations, clipping, and dual updates — the structure that makes both kernel fusion and penalty-bank switching cheap.

### Why structure matters

Treating every constraint row as dense forces an operator of size (n + m)² and pays for products against rows that are simply diagonal. FAME-QP's three-way classification turns box constraints into elementwise arithmetic and strips the diagonal block out of split constraints, so the operator only carries the genuinely coupled rows C. The ablation confirms the design: extracting diagonal rows leaves iteration counts and solutions unchanged (within 10⁻¹²) while cutting operator storage 1.67-4.00× and iteration time 1.07-3.03×, and against ReLU-QP's (n + 2m)² operator the extracted stacked operator at n = 512, m_c = 128 is 8× smaller in storage and 5× faster per iteration under identical graph replay.

### Where the wins come from

The ablation separates three multiplicative effects: kernel fusion with graph replay (dominant for small operators, up to 32× per iteration at n = 128), penalty probing (dominant for larger problems, up to 3.35× at n = 1000, since iterations drop), and lazy matrix updates (dominant when P and G change every solve, roughly halving step time in the KQ-LMPC replay). Automatic mode selection mostly follows the calibrated model's predictions but finalizes by measurement because the model alone misplaces the stacked-versus-factored crossover by about a factor of two. The authors flag sparse and horizon-structured formulations as future work to push scalability beyond dense condensed QPs.

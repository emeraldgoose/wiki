# Do Underwater Image Quality Metrics Agree with Human Perception? A Benchmark with Human Choices, Simple Edits and Generative Drift

**Source**: [arXiv:2611.18429](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.18429) · **Published**: 2026-11-13
**Authors**: Rishii Kumar Singh, Sarthak Gupta, Shivam Sharma, Arghya Pal, Piyush Tiwary, Rahul Kumar Singh, Ankan Bhunia, Balaji Srinivasan, Chetan Arora, C. V. Jawahar

## Overview

Underwater image enhancement is hard to evaluate because there are rarely clean reference images, so the field relies on no-reference image quality assessment (NR-IQA) metrics. This paper asks a deceptively simple question — do those metrics actually agree with human preferences? — and the answer is no. Across 73 handcrafted underwater, learned underwater, vision-language, and general-purpose metrics tested against human judgments on five references (four published opinion-score sets plus a new paired-comparison study), no metric consistently matches what people prefer.

The authors go further than a correlation table. For each metric they measure how often it picks the same image as people when comparing versions of the same scene, whether simple image edits and small adversarial perturbations can inflate scores above the enhancement people actually preferred, and whether scores decrease as repeated generative edits add noise. They also release TrustUIQA, a benchmark bundling the human references, the study's choices, the edited/attacked/generated images, and every metric's score on every image.

The headline numbers are uncomfortable for the field. The best worst-case Cohen's kappa over the five references is only 0.23 (Q-Align), while two human raters in the study agree at a median kappa of 0.41 — the gap between machines and humans is larger than the gap between two humans. The widely used UCIQE often favors edits that people reject. And on a Nano Banana Pro replica chain that should leave the image unchanged, only 22 of 73 scores decrease at every checkpoint while noise grows 2.5×.

## Key points

- Coverage: 73 metrics — handcrafted underwater, learned underwater, vision-language, and general-purpose NR-IQA — scored against human preferences on five references, four published opinion-score sets plus a new paired-comparison study.
- Agreement is weak: best worst-case Cohen's kappa is 0.23 (Q-Align); median inter-rater kappa between two humans is 0.41, so metric-human disagreement exceeds human-human disagreement.
- Metric gaming is easy: simple image edits and small adversarial perturbations push metric scores above the enhancement people preferred, meaning a high score does not certify a good result.
- Generative drift: under repeated generative edits that should leave the image semantically unchanged, noise grows 2.5×, but only 22 of 73 metric scores decrease at every checkpoint — most metrics are not drift-sensitive.
- TrustUIQA: a released benchmark combining human references, study choices, edited/attacked/generated images, and every metric's score on every image, so future work can be compared on the same footing.
- Accepted at the 6th WVAQ Workshop, WACV 2027 (18 pages including appendix).

## Details

The evaluation is deliberately adversarial in spirit. The five references give the study both breadth and depth: established opinion-score datasets supply calibrated human judgments, while the new paired-comparison study supplies direct head-to-head choices on exactly the kinds of edits the metrics will be tested against. Every metric is run on every image, producing a dense score matrix inside TrustUIQA rather than cherry-picked tables.

Three failure modes are tested. First, selection agreement: when two versions of the same scene are shown side by side, does the metric rank the one people prefer above the other? Second, edit inflation: can a trivial edit — something a human would call worse — raise the score above the version people actually preferred? Third, drift blindness: if a generative model repeatedly re-renders an image with no intended change, does the score fall as noise accumulates, or does it stay flat and even rise?

The results paint a consistent picture. Q-Align, the strongest worst-case performer, still only reaches kappa 0.23; UCIQE, one of the most cited underwater metrics, often prefers edits humans reject. On the Nano Banana Pro replica chain — a setup engineered to be identity-preserving — noise grows 2.5× while a minority of metrics even move in the wrong direction. Only 22 of 73 decrease monotonically at every checkpoint.

The paper's contribution is less a new metric than a warning and a yardstick. It shows that the underwater enhancement community has been optimizing against metrics that do not track human perception, that these metrics are gameable by edits and perturbations, and that they are largely blind to generative drift. TrustUIQA makes the finding reproducible and gives future metric design a common test bed.

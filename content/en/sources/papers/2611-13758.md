# Mixed Signals: Weight-Space Programming for EEG and ECG

**Source**: [arXiv:2611.13758](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13758) · **Published**: 2026-11-10

## Overview

Weight-space programming (WSP) lets a language model read a trained neural network's weights as text, reason about what they compute, and edit them — "training" by reasoning instead of gradient descent. Early demonstrations were striking (GPT-6 Astra writing MinAtar Breakout weights from zero that scored 517.4 against a PPO agent's 15.8, or raising MATH-500 accuracy from 7.9% to 48.1% by changing 0.016% of weights), but the reported failures were just as informative: editing could not improve an already well-trained Gemma 3, and critics noted the tests used small networks. The EPFL authors argue biosignals are the natural next test: the physics and physiology involved are prior knowledge an LLM already has, labeled data is scarce and subjects vary, and the networks that actually run on wearables are tiny — exactly the scale where WSP has worked so far.

Three models — Claude Opus 5.5, DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash, and GLM-5.3 — were put through five experiments on EEG and ECG networks (TinyEEGNet, ECGResNet) under strict rules: no gradient descent, no optimizers, no iterative fitting; only evaluate calls or scripted passes, with fixed budgets and a sandboxed Python session that cannot see test data. Prompts never reveal the expected answer.

The results cut both ways, as the title says. The models could not tell what a trained network was for from its weights alone (GLM's best 0.33 with electrode names matched its 0.29 on untrained control networks — guessing, not reading), and none could improve a healthy network (bias recalibration −0.009 and fine-tuning +0.000 did no better). But told that something was broken, they repaired visible planted faults almost completely (Opus and DeepSeek recovering 0.94–1.00 of lost accuracy versus 0.54 for the best gradient-trained fix), and in the study's best outcome they diagnosed three hidden recording artifacts — mains hum, a calibration pulse, a machine low-pass filter — from behavior alone, then switched each shortcut off inside the network.

The headline result is experiment 5: every model wrote a working network from scratch — all weights set by reasoning over a minimal set of practice subjects. Claude Opus 5.5's TinyEEGNet for eyes-open-versus-closed reached 0.834 balanced accuracy on 65 unseen test subjects, on par with the same network trained by gradient descent on 30 subjects (0.806) and far above training on 5 (0.704) — matching standard training with 6x fewer training subjects. Opus's networks also beat classic hand-crafted EEG pipelines fitted on the same 5 subjects (relative alpha power 0.746, CSP+LDA 0.779), and its fist-classification network was built mirror-symmetric so that swapping left and right electrodes flips the decision.

## Key points

- Reading weights is not yet a capability: given a trained network, no model reliably named its task (chance 17%); with weights and electrode names, GLM hit 33%, Opus 22%, DeepSeek 0%, and GLM scored 29% on untrained controls — the answer came from dataset familiarity, not the weights.
- Healthy networks have little left to give: with no feedback Opus left all six networks untouched, and the standard fixes did no better (bias recalibration −0.009, fine-tuning +0.000).
- Visible planted faults are understood and undone: Opus and DeepSeek recovered 0.94–1.00 of the lost accuracy without any feedback (best gradient-trained fix: 0.54), naming the exact mechanism in 83–100% of runs; GLM only partly repaired them (0.49 and 0.81).
- Invisible faults (swapped output, mirrored electrodes) are worked around, not understood: with 30 feedback calls Opus recovered 0.99 and DeepSeek 0.96 versus 0.40 for the best gradient fix, but no model named the fault in more than 17% of runs — the edits were simply rewarded by feedback.
- The ECG electrode swap was harder: only Opus helped, cutting swapped-normal ECGs misread as abnormal from 99% to 21% (swapped macro-AUC 0.766 → 0.870, just short of the 0.882 textbook swap check) while leaving correct ECGs intact.
- Hidden-cause debugging was the best outcome: Opus and GLM named all three artifacts with 4/4 diagnosis points and no false alarms; the muscle filter was described as "a steep 25 Hz low-pass filter (a machine filter setting)", against true values of 20/25/30 Hz.
- Switching the shortcuts off: artifacts alone flipped 75–91% of myocardial-infarction calls before the edit; after Opus's and GLM's edits at most 3%, after DeepSeek's at most 14%, and MI detection on new ECGs from a second hospital rose from 0.67–0.69 to 0.83–0.86.
- Even so, no edit beat the pre-registered pass mark (the best training-based fix using the same 500 labeled ECGs), falling 0.007–0.029 short — but those fixes presuppose the cause is already known, while the models found it on their own.
- Writing from zero: Opus's eyes network 0.834 (GD-30: 0.806; GD-5: 0.704) and fist network 0.769; GLM and DeepSeek networks land at the level of GD on 2–10 subjects; CSP+LDA on the fist task stayed at chance (0.509) on 5 subjects and 0.640 even on 30.
- Cost and limitations: Opus's two written networks cost $8.50 and $9.08 in API fees and 38–39 minutes of wall-clock time versus 2–5 seconds of gradient descent on one A100; datasets are public PhysioNet sets likely inside LLM training corpora, and later experiments used single runs.

## Details

- The five experiments, in order: (1) read a trained network and name its task, (2) improve a healthy network, (3) repair one planted fault, (4) debug a network that fails only on unseen data where the cause is a hidden recording artifact, (5) write a network from scratch from architecture, task, and 5 practice subjects.
- Feedback came in two shapes: an evaluate call returning balanced accuracy on a fresh random batch of 128 practice trials (varying ±4 points between batches), and a pass returning per-class and per-recording scores, internal activations, and paired comparisons; call budgets were fixed in advance.
- The rules forbid gradient descent, optimizers, and iterative fitting; one-shot linear algebra (least-squares, LDA) was allowed in the ECG experiments, and from experiment 3 onward every tool call had to carry a plain-English explanation. The Python session ran in a Landlock sandbox unable to read data, model, or test files.
- The written networks are interpretable because TinyEEGNet is compact: Opus's eyes network filters each electrode into alpha, theta, and beta bands, compares neighboring electrode rows, and fires on strong alpha against its neighbors (eyes closed); the fist network combines three textbook cues — eyes moving toward the target, the slow potential over the contralateral motor cortex, and the beta drop there.
- Opus was a poor judge of its own work: it expected 0.97 for its eyes network based on the practice recordings it designed from (actual: 0.834 on unseen subjects), while its fist-network estimate of 0.774 was nearly exact.
- The authors' conclusion is that these models succeed by recalling structural priors of what a classifier for a given biosignal task should look like, not by fitting data — which explains both the from-scratch successes and the inability to read or improve existing weights — and they position WSP as a complement or initialization pass for tiny edge/wearable models rather than a replacement for training.

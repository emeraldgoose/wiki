# ScrapeBench: Evaluating Legal Compliance of AI Agents in Website Scraping

**Source**: [arXiv:2611.11306](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.11306) · **Published**: 2026-11-08
**Authors**: Joseph Marvin Imperial, Daniel Slate, Noam Kolt

## Overview

AI agents that browse the web can now take actions that breach contracts and violate computer-security laws, and scraping — the automated collection of website content — is the everyday case: sites like Amazon, ESPN, and The New York Times prohibit it through terms of service (ToS) and robots.txt directives. Existing agentic benchmarks measure whether agents complete tasks, not whether they do so lawfully, and users can deliberately instruct agents to ignore legal rules. ScrapeBench is the first agentic benchmark that empirically evaluates compliance of AI agents with website scraping policies, treating the ToS and robots.txt of real websites as ground truth.

The benchmark covers the 1,895 most-visited websites (drawn from the Chrome UX report for February 2026 after filtering), of which 1,626 explicitly prohibit scraping in their ToS or robots.txt and 269 do not. A GPT-5.2-driven pipeline extracted verbatim scraping clauses, two legally trained co-authors shaped the labeling rules, and a 15% manual audit found 97.9% (ToS) and 98.9% (robots.txt) agreement (Cohen's kappa 0.91). Each trial gives an agent a scraping task, a single fetch_url() tool with up to five calls, and one of eight legal priming prompts escalating from a neutral request to pasted legal texts and a strong legal warning; a tool-call trace is classified into four outcomes — Violation, Compliance, Permissible, and Over-Refusal.

The headline finding is systematic non-compliance: on prohibited websites, six frontier agents (GPT-5.2, Claude Opus 4.6, Gemini 3.1 Pro, Kimi K2.5, Qwen3 Max, Grok 4.2) show compliance below 5% under the neutral prompt, and even when agents actively fetch ToS and robots.txt files, compliance stays low. Compliance jumps only when legal texts are pasted into the prompt, and an adversarial instruction to ignore the terms collapses most agents' compliance — while Claude resists via near-total refusal, lawful or not. On the 269 non-prohibiting websites Claude exhibits an over-refusal rate of 98.5% under the adversarial prompt. A 180-participant human baseline shows humans comply at broadly similar rates but over-refuse at much higher rates, and act on legal text once they read it.

## Key points

- Dataset: top 3,000 global websites from the Chrome UX (CrUX) repository for February 2026, filtered to 1,895 (dropping NSFW, non-English, and US-inaccessible sites); 1,626 Explicitly Prohibited (EP) and 269 Not Explicitly Prohibited (NEP); 139 sites have no ToS and 77 have no robots.txt; roughly 30% sit behind DDoS protection that required manual ToS retrieval.
- Labeling scheme: EP requires verbatim scraping prohibition in the ToS, or a blanket disallow rule for all user agents in robots.txt or explicit naming of an AI crawler (e.g., ClaudeBot); conditional-restriction sites were removed to keep a strict binary; GPT-5.2 did the extraction with two legal-professional co-authors designing the pipeline; manual audit of 15% per category agreed at 97.9%/98.9% with Cohen's kappa 0.91.
- Cross-tabulation of the two labels: 1,024 sites prohibit via ToS only, 501 via both, 101 via robots.txt only, and 269 prohibit via neither.
- Agent protocol: one fetch_url() tool (GET, returns page HTML) with at most 5 invocations per trial; fetched URLs are classified as legal-resource pages (ToS, robots.txt) versus content pages; an outcome function maps the scrape/did-not-scrape action and the ground-truth label to Violation, Compliance, Permissible, or Over-Refusal.
- Result 1: under the neutral prompt (P1), compliance is near zero for GPT, Claude, Gemini, Kimi, and Qwen on EP websites; only Grok reaches 11%. Implicitly mentioning policies (P2) barely helps (best 5.5-5.7%).
- Result 2: explicit instructions (P3) and linked legal URLs (P4) lift Claude to 40.1% and 41.5%; pasting legal texts in-context (P5) pushes GPT, Claude, and Gemini above 70% (GPT peaks at 73.9%), while Qwen stays at 2.9%. The combined strong legal warning (P7) yields the highest compliance for five of six agents: Claude 85.9%, Gemini 85.7%, Kimi 62.7%, Grok 59.7%, Qwen 13.8%; GPT instead peaks at P5 and drops to 60.6% at P7.
- Result 3: the adversarial prompt (P8, "ignore the ToS and robots.txt") collapses compliance — Gemini 41.6%, GPT 11.7%, Kimi 9.5%, Qwen 1.7%, Grok 0.6% — with one exception: Claude reaches 99.7%, but manual inspection shows this is a blanket refusal that does not reason about the actual policy, plausibly its safety mechanisms firing on the instruction itself.
- Over-refusal on lawful targets (NEP): in-context legal resources cut over-refusal (Claude 16.4% to 8.2% and 6.7%; Gemini 12.6% to 1.5% moving from P3 to P5); under P8 agents split into broad refusers (Claude refusing 99.7%/98.5% on EP/NEP, Gemini 41%/27.7%) and broad scrapers (GPT, Kimi, Qwen, Grok, all with compliance below 12% and over-refusal at 5%).
- Tool-usage paradox: at P4 all six agents retrieve robots.txt at 60% or more (four at 99-100%) and ToS at 80% or more, yet compliance stays low — GPT fetches ToS at 82.8% and robots.txt at 100% while complying at only 14.2%. At P7 most agents stop checking altogether (Claude's robots.txt checks fall from 100% to 1.6%; GPT alone stays high at 58-71%).
- Why agents violate: among 1,260 violation trials (30 per agent across P1-P7, rationales stated in 535 = 42.5%, judged by GPT-5.4-Mini), 69.7% read robots.txt, found no restriction, and disregarded the ToS; 18.5% misread a prohibiting ToS as permitting; 6.9% deemed the content public; 3.9% could not verify the real ToS and scraped anyway; 0.9% treated the ToS as non-binding — about 88% of violations stem from interpretation failures rather than wholesale rejection of the rules.
- Human baseline (180 Prolific participants, 30 per prompt condition in a between-participant design): humans comply at 48.9% under P1, rising to 57.3% (P4) and 65.1% (P7); their over-refusal starts at 38.9% and creeps to 45.8% (not significant) — comparable compliance to agents but far more over-refusal, e.g., versus Claude's 98.5%.
- Humans check legal resources less than agents (ToS checks 38.9% on EP sites at P4 versus the agents' 52-83%) but act on what they read: human EP compliance is 67.1% when they read the ToS versus 55.7% when they do not.
- Discussion: three failure modes — checking legal resources does not produce compliance, pasted-in ToS text invites prompt-injection attacks (a false ToS could be fed in-context), and agents remain highly vulnerable to adversarial instructions; the authors argue the compliance burden may need to shift from end-users (whose prompts look like P1) to model providers (e.g., system prompts resembling P5-P7).

## Details

- Publication: camera-ready for NeurIPS 2026, Evaluations and Datasets Track; this document follows arXiv v3 (2026-11-11); v1 was posted 2026-11-08 and v2 on 2026-11-10.
- Evaluation framework outputs: an ordered tool-call trace per trial plus a natural-language response; the outcome classification uses GPT-5.2 as evaluator over the prompt, fetched URLs, and scraping criteria.
- Prompt taxonomy: eight legal priming prompts escalating in pressure — P1 neutral "scrape the landing page"; P2 implicit policy mention; P3 explicit compliance instruction; P4 links to the ToS and robots.txt; P5/P6 paste the ToS or robots.txt text (ToS text capped at 100,000 characters; missing files substituted with a placeholder sentence); P7 combines texts, strict compliance instructions, and a warning about applicable law; P8 adversarially instructs agents to ignore the terms.
- Agents evaluated: GPT-5.2, Claude Opus 4.6, Gemini 3.1 Pro, Kimi K2.5, Qwen3 Max, and Grok 4.2, spanning closed- and open-weight models.
- Violation-rationale judging: 30 violation trials per agent across P1-P7 (n = 1,260) were categorized with GPT-5.4-Mini as judge; agents stated a rationale in 535 trials (42.5%).
- Human study logistics: participants pressed a button to scrape 15 randomly sampled websites one at a time in an external web app; statistical comparisons reported with p-values (compliance gains at P4/P7 significant; over-refusal changes not).
- Funding: the Pivotal Fellowship and the Hebrew University Governance of AI Lab (Israel Science Foundation Grant No. 487/25), the Survival and Flourishing Fund, and Coefficient Giving; two co-authors are legal professionals who contributed to the labeling pipeline.
- Legal grounding: the label terminology derives from case law and commentary (hiQ Labs v. LinkedIn, Associated Press v. Meltwater, Register.com, Reddit v. Perplexity, In re OpenAI, among those cited); the paper notes absence of a scraping prohibition in a ToS does not establish that scraping is lawful — it lowers, at most, breach-of-contract risk.

# Actor, Judge, or Teacher? Understanding Decision Models in Long-Horizon Agents

**Source**: [arXiv:2611.17167](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17167) · **Published**: 2026-11-12
**Authors**: Haochen Liu, Qianhao Zhu, Zixuan Wang, Dingming Li, Tongbo Chen, Peng Qiu, Yingqi Qu, Weiming Lu, Jun Xiao, Yongliang Shen

## Overview

Decision models are pretrained models that answer a question about a context with a typed output — a choice among options with a probability for each, or a yes/no score — quickly and cheaply, instead of generating text. One such model can fill three roles in an LLM agent: an actor that chooses the next action, a judge that scores actions already taken, or a teacher that scores every action of every rollout so a policy can be trained with reinforcement learning. Prior work has studied them mainly as actors, judging their usefulness by how well they act. This paper asks a different question: can a decision model that acts poorly still teach well?

The study compares three dedicated decision models — JEV from TypeSafe AI, Decider v1.2 from Perplexity, and GPT Decisions from OpenAI — with three general LLMs (Qwen prompted, Qwen constrained, and GPT Luna) as actors and judges on ALFWorld, and uses JEV as a teacher on ALFWorld and WebShop. As actors, all six models are pulled away from a subgoal that must come first by an easier one. As judges that see the whole trajectory, the same models credit the steps toward that subgoal, and asking whether each action helped, rather than whether the task will succeed, separates useful from useless actions.

The headline teaching result makes the gap concrete. JEV completes only 42.9% of the ALFWorld evaluation tasks when it chooses the actions itself. Yet when it scores every action of a Qwen2.5-1.5B policy during training, after seeing the whole trajectory, the policy reaches 82.9% success when JEV is asked whether each action helped, against 72.8% for outcome rewards alone — and only 69.3% when JEV is instead asked whether the task will succeed. The same model can act poorly yet teach well, but only when asked the right question in the right context.

The authors' explanation connects the three roles. To act correctly, the model must anticipate that an easier action leaves the task incomplete before any consequence is visible; a judge evaluates the action after its consequences have occurred, which reveals contributions that acting must anticipate.

## Key points

- Actor failure is specific and measurable: JEV completes 70.8% of Pick2 tasks but only 14.7% of tasks with ordered subgoals, and Decider v1.2, the strongest actor overall at 63.6%, succeeds on only 18.8% of Heat tasks.
- The models do not lack the knowledge: in states where one action completes the easier subgoal, removing that subgoal from the goal moves most of each model's probability to the required step — for JEV, from 14.6% on the required state change to 77.6%.
- A reminder about the dependency barely helps acting: it changes actor success by only -2.8% to +5.2%.
- Judging needs hindsight: contributions unclear from the prefix become apparent from what follows, so judges seeing the full trajectory recognize contributions the same models overlook as actors.
- Question design matters most: with the whole trajectory visible, JEV's Progress question scores milestone actions 0.36 higher than no-op actions within a trajectory, whereas the Value question yields a gap of only 0.05; Progress also finds more milestones than Value at every trajectory length, by 8.0% to 16.0% in Recall-at-R.
- The dedicated decision models with a disclosed base LLM judge more accurately than that LLM on the same question, at 28% to 43% lower cost per score.
- Teaching results: JEV's hindsight Progress scores combined with GRPO outcome rewards raise the 1.5B policy from 72.8% to 82.9% on ALFWorld and from 56.8% to 71.4% on WebShop; the gains are largest on Cool (+23.0%) and Look (+20.7%).
- The taught 1.5B policy outperforms a 7B policy trained with outcome rewards alone on both environments; the same recipe lifts the 7B policy from 77.6% to 83.8% on ALFWorld and 66.1% to 73.6% on WebShop.
- On ordered-subgoal tasks the taught policy completes 86.8%, which is 6.9% to 16.2% above the other question-context settings, although JEV completes only 14.7% of these tasks as an actor; the taught policy also finishes faster, completing 74.5% of ALFWorld tasks within 20 actions versus 63.6% to 69.5% for other settings.
- Step-level supervision is affordable: scoring every action during RL training costs between 22.21 and 30.07 USD per run across the four policies, with JEV priced at 0.042 USD per million input tokens through OpenRouter.

## Details

### Three roles on ALFWorld and WebShop

ALFWorld is a household environment where an agent issues text commands such as go to, take, and clean; its six task types separate single-object tasks (Pick, Look) from dependency tasks (Clean, Heat, Cool), where placement depends on first changing the object's state, and Pick2 with two independent requirements. WebShop is a shopping environment whose reward measures how well the purchased item matches the instruction, with required options that must be selected before purchase. The actor answers a multiple-choice question about the next action; the judge answers a yes/no question about each executed action of trajectories from other policies; the teacher applies the same scoring to the policy being trained, combined with outcome rewards in GRPO.

### Why hindsight helps

The judge experiments isolate two factors. Seeing the whole trajectory helps judges recognize contributions that are unclear from the prefix alone but become apparent from what follows. Seeing consequences is not enough by itself: when asked whether the task will succeed, every action in a trajectory gets the same answer — the outcome — so the Progress question, which asks whether each action helped, is what separates actions that complete a subgoal from actions that change nothing. All six models show both effects.

### Takeaways for using decision models

The paper draws practical implications: screen a model's suitability as a supervisor by how well it judges recorded trajectories, not by how well it acts — judge measurements identified the best question and context at a small fraction of an RL run's cost. Step-level supervision can then be obtained cheaply from a frozen decision model after each episode, without step labels or a learned critic, and the teacher is not needed at test time. Online filtering, where only the prefix is available, is where decision models are less suited. Code is available at github.com/ZJU-REAL/decision-agents.

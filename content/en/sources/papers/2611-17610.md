# More Agents, More Samples, or More Thinking? Untangling Coordination, Ensembling, and Test-Time Compute in Multi-Agent LLMs

**Source**: [huggingface](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17610)
**Date**: 2026-11-13

## Overview

Evaluations of multi-agent language model systems routinely compare communicating architectures — debate, mixture-of-agents, MetaGPT-style pipelines — against a single greedy pass. This paper argues that this practice confounds three distinct things: the effect of agent-to-agent coordination, the effect of drawing more samples, and the effect of spending more inference compute. The author, Seyed Mahmoud Sajjadi Mohammadabadi (University of Nevada, Reno), performs a quantitative evidence synthesis of nine primary empirical evaluations spanning 516 unique multi-agent configurations, organizing them along a six-dimensional resource vector R and a four-category baseline ladder.

The headline finding is sobering. Against Category A baselines (a single greedy pass), multi-agent accuracy differences are negative on average and essentially zero once the largest contributing study is omitted. When the comparison is tightened to Category B (sample-count-matched) or Category C (resource-budget-matched) baselines, multi-agent systems average −0.042 in accuracy, and multilevel random-effects modeling on the log-odds scale remains negative across sensitivity checks. Multi-agent accuracy equals or falls below the baseline in 74% of these comparable contrasts, and study-level means are nonpositive in almost all evaluated studies.

The paper also quantifies a measurement artifact: across studies reporting both greedy and sampled baselines, simply moving from a greedy pass to a sampled baseline shifts the measured multi-agent accuracy difference by −0.038 on average. In other words, part of what looks like a multi-agent effect is an accounting choice about the baseline. The author concludes that communication adds no information when agents share identical evidence, so positive returns require partitioned evidence, external verification, or error diversity across models.

## Key points

- Multi-agent advantages in the literature are largely an artifact of weak baselines; matched on samples or budget, the average effect is −0.042 accuracy.
- 74% of comparable (Category B/C) contrasts show multi-agent accuracy at or below baseline; study means are nonpositive in nearly all evaluated studies.
- A six-dimensional resource vector R standardizes cost accounting (agents, rounds, tokens, and related inference resources) across studies with incompatible tables.
- A four-category baseline ladder separates single-pass (A), sample-matched (B), budget-matched (C), and stronger matching regimes, exposing how conclusions flip with the yardstick.
- In Smit et al., Zhang et al., and Yang et al., debate-style systems beat the sampled baseline in only a minority of contrasts (21%, 19%, and 17% respectively, per the paper's extraction).
- Per prior compute-matched work cited, gains depend on task decomposability and saturate once single-agent accuracy reaches roughly 45%; on some MMLU-Pro Pareto fronts multi-agent debate and mixture-of-agents beat self-consistency by +1.3 and +2.7 points.
- The paper ships a reviewer checklist (PACE) and evaluation utilities (pace CLI) plus reproduction data on GitHub.

## Details

**Method.** The study is a structured quantitative evidence synthesis, not a new architecture. The author formalizes cost accounting with a resource vector R with six dimensions covering the inference resources consumed (agents, communication rounds, tokens, and related budget axes), and defines a baseline ladder with four categories so that every extracted contrast is labeled by how strictly the baseline is matched to the multi-agent system. Contrasts are extracted from the primary empirical tables of nine studies and pooled with multilevel random-effects models on the log-odds scale (with robust variance estimation for dependent effect sizes within studies), because raw accuracy differences are bounded and ceiling-compressed. Descriptive results and sensitivity analyses are reported under both single-pass and sampled baselines, including leave-largest-study-out checks and checks around the ~45% capability threshold beyond which multi-agent returns saturate.

**Results.** Against Category A (greedy single pass), pooled differences are negative overall and near zero when the largest contributing study is excluded. Against Category B/C (sample- or budget-matched), the average difference is −0.042, multi-agent accuracy is at or below baseline in 74% of contrasts, and the log-odds multilevel model stays negative across sensitivity checks. Switching the reference baseline from greedy to sampled shifts differences by −0.038 on average — direct evidence that baseline choice, not coordination, drives much of the reported benefit. The positive exceptions are informative: gains appear where evidence is partitioned across agents, where external verification is available, or where models exhibit error diversity, and where tasks are decomposable (parallel rather than strictly sequential deduction). The author frames this as an evaluation-methodology contribution and provides the PACE reviewer checklist, a pace CLI, and reproduction data (github.com/mahmoudsajjadi/pace). The paper is 43 pages with 11 figures and 13 tables (cs.AI, cs.CL, cs.LG, cs.MA).

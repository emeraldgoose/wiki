# Correcting Persistent Failures in Point-Cloud Grasping Policies using Human Demonstrations in Virtual Reality

**Source**: [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.18919)
**Date**: 2026-11-19

## Overview

Robots in homes, warehouses and farms must pick up objects they have never seen, usually without a 3D model or a category label. A grasping policy that works directly on the point cloud from a depth sensor needs only the geometry the robot observes, so it applies to any new object. Offline grasp generators such as Contact-GraspNet work this way, but they rank grasps by learned plausibility rather than by whether the grasp actually lifts the object. Reinforcement learning (RL) can optimize for that directly and make a point-cloud grasping policy much better on average, yet it leaves specific object-pose situations persistently unsolved.

This paper presents a human-in-the-loop failure loop that targets exactly those persistently failing grasps. Situations an RL policy keeps failing are mined automatically and sent to a virtual-reality (VR) application, where a person picks the object up with their own hands. The two fingertip contact points are returned to training — the person contributes only where to grasp, while the robot remains free to choose its own approach. An automatic check executes each demonstration with the robot's gripper before it is admitted to training, and only robust demonstrations are used.

In simulation, RL raises success on unseen objects from 55% with Contact-GraspNet to 83%. Adding 58 human demonstrations nearly doubles success on the hard cases RL kept failing, from 24% to 45%, and recovers performance that RL alone loses on similar cases that were never demonstrated, without reducing success on unseen objects.

## Key points

- Point-cloud grasping policies generalize to unseen objects without category labels or 3D models, but RL alone leaves specific grasps persistently unsolved.
- A failure loop automatically mines situations the RL policy keeps failing and exports them to a VR application (Meta Quest 3, WebXR with tracked hands) where a person grasps and lifts the object, holding it for 0.5 s; only the two opposing fingertip contacts in the object frame are stored.
- The person contributes only where to grasp — hand trajectory and orientation are deliberately not imitated, since human hands and parallel-jaw grippers have different kinematics.
- A live check server executes each recorded pair of points with the robot's hand while the person is still in the headset, searching approach angles from −80° to 80° in 20° steps; a demonstration is robust only if at least half of four repeats (under 1 cm / 5° execution noise) lift the object.
- RL + demos uses the same warm start and recipe as RL alone; on 10% of episodes a demonstrated case is replayed at its exact pose, with half executing the demonstrated grasp and half the policy's own. Executed demonstrations train the critic but are excluded from the policy-gradient term, and the actor imitates a demonstration only on an executed episode where it lifted, with imitation weight fading to zero by iteration 41.
- Headline results in simulation (Isaac Lab 2.3.2 / Isaac Sim 5.1, YCB and procedural shapes, 451 training instances from 57 families, 24 unseen YCB test families): RL lifts unseen-object success from 55% (Contact-GraspNet) to 83%; 58 human demonstrations raise hard-case success from 24% to 45% and recover RL's regression on similar never-demonstrated cases, with no loss on unseen objects.

## Details

**Method.** Training records every failed grasp and replays it later; cases that rarely lift across all RL seeds are shown to a person in VR at their recorded pose on a virtual table. A person who judges a case physically impossible may mark it as such and skip it. Robust demonstrations (verified by repeated execution with the robot gripper) are folded back into PPO training from the same warm start. Demonstration episodes replay at the exact pose; the imitation loss is restricted to the two contact points (with a finger-swapped variant as an alternative), fading λ(t) = max(0, 1 − (t−1)/40) to zero by iteration 41, after which demonstrated cases continue to appear for 110 more iterations but plain RL decides what to do with them. The authors note from an earlier experiment on a different object set that imitating on every demonstration episode pulled the policy away from grasps that already worked and lowered hard-case performance.

**Results.** RL improves over its Contact-GraspNet warm start by 18–25 percentage points on unseen objects (24.5 flat, 17.5 tilted) yet ends 10–13 points below it on hard cases. Adding 58 demonstrations nearly doubles hard-case success (24% → 45%), recovers the regression on similar never-demonstrated cases, and does not reduce success on unseen objects. Objects are real-scale meshes up to 30 cm with concavities preserved (bowls and mugs stay hollow); simulation runs at 60 Hz; each condition uses about 614,000 grasps per seed.

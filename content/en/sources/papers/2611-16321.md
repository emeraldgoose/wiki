# From Large Language Models to Efficient System One Models via Adaptive Computation

**Source**: [arXiv:2611.16321](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16321) · **Published**: 2026-11-12
**Authors**: Minh-Anh Nguyen

## Overview

Turning a large language model into a fast decision maker usually means replacing the language-modeling head with a decision head while keeping the whole backbone, so every routing, tool-selection, intent, or safety call pays for all layers. The paper asks how much depth such System One decisions actually need. Working with Intern-Decision-4B, a Qwen3.5-4B based model that reaches 90.0% macro accuracy on seven decision benchmarks, the author finds that decision information is concentrated in the middle layers: the final six layers can be removed without loss, while cutting into the middle band destroys the decision.

The analysis also shows that independently trained smaller models are not specialists. They largely reproduce the full model's decisions instead of developing distinct capabilities of their own, which means that training smaller backbones or truncating layers is the wrong recipe on its own. Efficiency has to come from sharing computation across depths inside one model.

NestOne implements this idea. It jointly fine-tunes the full model and nested prefix sub-models that share its final layers and decision head, training the sub-models with self-distillation from the full model's distribution. A lightweight logistic gate reads the hidden state at each exit and decides whether computation should continue. Across seven decision benchmarks, a single 80-minute training run produces exits that match or outperform separately trained models at every depth, while adaptive early exiting lowers the average number of layers run per decision.

## Key points

- Intern-Decision-4B, a System One decision model built on Qwen3.5-4B, reaches 90.0% macro accuracy across seven decision benchmarks, but every decision traverses all 32 layers at roughly 1.45 ms per layer at batch size one.
- Integrated-gradient attribution places 50% of the decision by layer 20 and 80% by layer 23 of the 32-layer model.
- Removing layers 24-29 without retraining leaves accuracy unchanged (removing layers 24-31 costs only 0.6 points), while jointly mean-ablating layers 15-23 eliminates the decision margin and cuts accuracy by 57.8 points; ablating layer 0 costs 46.6 points.
- Fixed-depth truncation is non-uniform: accuracy falls from 84.3% at 24 layers to 72.8% at 16 layers and 61.5% at 12 layers.
- Independently trained smaller models mimic the full model: 89-92% of their correct decisions are already made by the full model, their unique wins are only 3.8-4.3% of decisions, and those wins exceed the seed-disagreement noise floor by just 2-3 points.
- NestOne trains nested prefix sub-models with a shared coda and a KL self-distillation term whose weight ramps linearly from 0 to 0.5 over the first half of training, so no external teacher signal is needed.
- One NestOne run takes 80 minutes on a single H200 versus 94 minutes for the four separate models; its 12-, 16-, 24-, and 32-layer exits take 18.4, 23.8, 34.7, and 46.5 ms per decision at batch size one.
- The largest accuracy gain is at 12 layers (64.4% versus 61.5%, +2.9 points), and the 24-layer exit reaches 83.7% with 25% fewer layers, reducing latency from 46.5 to 34.7 ms.
- A logistic gate on the hidden state reaches AUROC 0.86, 0.89, and 0.88 at exits 9, 13, and 21, above the 0.84-0.86 of probability-based rules, and gives the best trade-off: 21.6 and 20.2 average layers at accuracy budgets of 0.25 and 2 points, with accuracy changes of only +0.08 and +0.03 points on the four largest benchmarks, which hold 96% of decisions.

## Details

### The decision is written at mid depth

Integrated-gradient attribution and layer interventions agree on where the 32-layer model writes its decisions. Removing layers 24-29 without retraining leaves accuracy unchanged, but jointly mean-ablating layers 15-23 wipes out the entire decision margin, reducing accuracy by 57.8 points over 348 decisions. Early layers still matter as feature builders even though their direct margin contribution is small: ablating layer 0 costs 46.6 points and layer 6 costs 7.8. Consistently, a 16-layer model trained only on the layers most affected by fine-tuning (8-21 and 30-31) reaches just 35.7%, versus 73.8% for the 16-layer prefix model. Smaller models trained from scratch behave like truncated copies of the full model rather than specialists: their unique wins are low-confidence (median confidence 0.59-0.61) and concentrated in Typed and AGNews, which supply 78% of them.

### Nested prefix sub-models and self-distillation

NestOne gives every prefix sub-model a shared coda: the final layers and decision head are kept once and reused by each exit, so answering at exit e costs e+3 layers including the coda. Training mixes cross-entropy on the gold option with a KL term that pulls each sub-model toward the stopped-gradient distribution of the 32-layer model, with the distillation weight ramping from 0 to 0.5 during the first half of training so the full model only acts as a teacher once its distribution is informative. One training run therefore yields models at depths 12, 16, 24, and 32 that share parameters instead of duplicating them, and the full model loses only 0.3 points relative to training it alone at 32 layers.

### A hidden-state gate chooses depth per decision

Each intermediate exit carries a one-layer logistic gate on the hidden state at the readout position, evaluated with a single dot product and a sigmoid before the coda runs. The state predicts exit correctness better than output confidence, and because distribution-based stopping rules must run the coda at every checked exit, they pay two extra layers per readout while the state gate pays none. At inference the model runs layer by layer and stops at the first exit whose gate score clears a shared threshold tuned on held-out data. The resulting savings depend on traffic: average depth ranges from 24 layers on hard-only traffic to 20.2 on easy-only traffic at a 0.25-point budget, and the remaining accuracy drop concentrates almost entirely on the three smallest benchmarks, which contain only 430 of 12,351 decisions. Adding the full model as a final exit improves the trade-off further, matching its accuracy on the four largest benchmarks at a 2-point budget while running 23.9 layers on average.

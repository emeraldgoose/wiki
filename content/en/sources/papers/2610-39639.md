# Where Do Vision Language Models Fail at 3D Reasoning?

**Source**: [arXiv:2610.39639](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.39639) · **Published**: 2026-10-29
**Authors**: Khai Le-Duc, Nguyen Kim Hai Bui, An Ngo, Nam D. Hoang, Yueqi Song, Jiarui Liu, Thai Quoc Hoang, Fang Wu, Chris Ngo, Terry Jingchen Zhang, Mona T. Diab, Ran Xu, Silvio Savarese, Xiao-Ming Wu

## Overview

Vision-language models can make individual judgments while struggling to recover and consistently use the exact properties of a 3D object. To characterize this gap, the paper introduces BedBench, a controlled benchmark of thirteen procedurally generated tasks spanning individual judgments and composite answers. The central finding is that accuracy alone overstates 3D reasoning ability: per-color counting exposes a pronounced weakness, with humans at 93.3% accuracy compared with 7.2% for the best model.

Targeted fine-tuning raises one model from 3.0% to 68.6%, but its answers about the most common colors remain inconsistent with its own per-color counts. Supplying those counts as text improves its judgments, showing that recovering a property does not mean using it consistently. Reasoning, scaling, and added visual cues improve individual judgments, but none makes exact answers reliable. The paper also probes Qwen3.5-27B, finding that exact per-color counts remain recoverable at the model input even though the model rarely uses them.

## Key points

- Individual correctness can mask failures in exact 3D property recovery.
- BedBench provides thirteen controlled tasks covering individual and composite answers.
- Per-color counting shows a large human-model performance gap.
- Fine-tuning improves accuracy without fully fixing internal consistency.
- Providing counts as text partially improves downstream judgments.
- Exact correctness, consistent use, and image dependence should all be evaluated.

## Details

The study locates the difficulty partly in whether evidence is recovered and partly in whether it is used consistently. Image-removal controls show that some apparent successes require no visual evidence, so accuracy can be misleading. The authors recommend evaluating 3D reasoning by exact correctness, consistent use, and image dependence rather than accuracy alone. Code and dataset are publicly available.

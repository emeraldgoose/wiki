# "PRISM" Is Not All You Need: Cross-Topic Name Collisions and Scientific Paper Retrieval

**Source**: [arXiv:2611.01362](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.01362) · **Published**: 2026-11-01
**Authors**: Jingjie Ning, Xueqi Li, Yibo Kong

## Overview

Scientific names increasingly connect papers from different research topics, so readers have to recover a paper's identity from context instead of from the label alone. The paper opens with the phenomenon in miniature: PRISM can name a spatial world model, a pathology encoder or a safety method; TRACE can reconstruct tables or attribute model outputs; SAGE can denote surgical mapping or audio encoding. A memorable label helps readers retain a name, while the surrounding research context decides which paper that name denotes. The authors define a name collision as a shared exact label across distinct bibliographic records, and cross-topic convergence as an increasing collision rate among records assigned to different semantic topics.

The study measures this on 564,861 AI-related arXiv papers submitted from 2020 through 27 October 2026, with complete original-version metadata and a 2015–2019 extension. Cross-topic collision grows 4.33-fold relative to the pooled 2020–2022 reference, while the within-topic point estimate grows only 1.12-fold. The increase survives author-overlap filters and changes in topic resolution, a second sentence encoder reproduces the pattern when the primary representation is swapped, and name-group resampling quantifies how much the observed label vocabulary influences the result.

The affected population is substantial: in the fixed retrieval collection, 28,152 papers — 19.5% of named records — have a namesake. A shared name therefore yields a candidate set, and a useful retrieval system has to identify the intended member rather than merely match the string.

## Key points

- To measure residual ambiguity, shared-name and metadata-matched unique-name targets both receive three terms drawn from their source abstracts; full-index BM25 reaches 59.4% and 85.1% respectively, leaving a matched identification gap of 25.7 percentage points.
- The gap depends on the fields used: the same three-term query construction leaves shared-name targets 25.7 points behind under title-based BM25 and 6.9 points behind under title-plus-abstract E5.
- Research context restores much of the missing information: exact-name candidate retrieval fixes the competing records, and the three terms raise BM25 Top-1 from 30.8% to 70.6%.
- Within fixed same-name candidate sets, listwise language-model ranking reaches 89.2% using the query and candidate titles, and topic-specific comparisons show context resolving more cross-topic ambiguity than within-topic ambiguity.
- Fixed word-frequency bands locate the historical increase in an intermediate-frequency vocabulary shared by many research areas — frequent names span many abstract-derived topics — while spelling and source evidence separate exact-name ambiguity from reuse inside a named method lineage.
- A source-linked pair of AutoInit papers illustrates the residual difficulty when a name and a research goal are both shared, and the evaluation separates candidate competition, document fields and contextual ranking through lexical, dense and language-model baselines.

## Details

The contributions are threefold: a version-aware measurement of scientific-name convergence, a retrieval evaluation that separates name specificity from contextual disambiguation, and a portable evaluation collection with a local query demonstration. The companion artifact contains the fixed corpus, candidate groups, queries, topic-error labels, baselines and an evaluation entry point, and the appendix provides a controlled model-naming case. The authors note the corpus keeps complete original-version metadata, which makes the measurement version-aware rather than snapshot-based, and that the analysis holds each label's frequency fixed across years so prevalence is separated from repetition within its frequency band.

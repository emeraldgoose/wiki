# GlyphAtlas: Disentangling Stroke Detail from Layout for Scene Text Synthesis

**Source**: [arXiv:2611.20192](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20192)
**Date**: 2026-11-15 (v1)

## Overview

Scene text synthesis asks a generative model to render specified strings inside target regions of a scene image while leaving the rest of the scene visually coherent — useful for photo editing, poster design, and related applications. The standard conditioning recipe pre-renders the target text at its intended locations into a "glyph image" that jointly encodes layout and glyph structure. That works well as a spatial anchor, but it couples the resolution of each glyph reference to the size of its target region, so small, dense, or structurally complex text gets only a few pixels of stroke guidance and the model produces incomplete, merged, or distorted characters.

GlyphAtlas, proposed by Luozheng Qin, Changhai Zhou, and Yiyi Zhou (Xiamen University and Fudan University), disentangles these two roles. The glyph image is retained solely as a spatial anchor that says where each glyph belongs and roughly what it looks like. Fine-grained stroke detail is supplied by a separate glyph atlas, which renders each character or word in its own cell at a resolution independent of the target region. The atlas cells are encoded with the backbone's native VAE rather than an auxiliary text encoder, so the features preserve low-level stroke structure without introducing a recognition-oriented encoder whose features are invariant to exactly the stroke variation that must be rendered.

To bind the atlas back to the layout, the authors introduce atlas-anchored attention: each text region can retrieve stroke detail only from its corresponding atlas cells, refining the coarse glyph structure from the glyph image in a coarse-to-fine manner. This keeps explicit layout correspondence while simplifying the stroke-rendering problem the model has to solve.

## Key points

- The glyph image is overloaded with two roles — spatial anchor and stroke reference — and GlyphAtlas splits them apart instead of replacing the glyph image outright.
- A dedicated glyph atlas renders each character or word in a separate cell at a resolution decoupled from the target region, giving every region a detailed stroke reference even for tiny glyphs.
- Atlas cells are encoded with the diffusion backbone's native VAE, whose pixel-level reconstruction objective keeps low-level stroke structure; no auxiliary text/OCR encoder is needed.
- Atlas-anchored attention restricts each text region to attend only to its own atlas cells, binding stroke detail to layout position.
- Training uses a mask-weighted flow matching objective plus a timestep-weighted text perceptual loss, targeting writing accuracy and background consistency respectively.
- Random resolution augmentation of atlas cells makes the atlas token budget adjustable at inference time, trading detail for cost.
- Reported results show stroke-accurate synthesis of small, dense, and structurally complex text, zero-shot transfer to scripts unseen during task-specific training, and leading performance in scenes with many text regions.

## Details

**Method.** GlyphAtlas builds on FLUX.1 Fill, an MM-DiT-style flow matching transformer that processes prompt and image tokens as one unified sequence with joint attention. The scene stream concatenates the noisy latent, the masked scene image encoding, and the text mask (space-to-depth), while a second stream encodes the glyph image; the atlas is appended as additional tokens. Atlas-anchored attention masks the attention so each text region consults only its matching atlas cells rather than every cell in the atlas. Training optimizes a conditional flow matching loss weighted by the text mask (emphasizing the regions to be written) together with a timestep-weighted text perceptual loss that emphasizes writing accuracy at the appropriate noise levels and preserves background consistency elsewhere. Randomly augmenting atlas cell resolution during training lets the model operate at different atlas budgets at inference, so users can dial the number of atlas tokens up or down.

**Results.** According to the paper, GlyphAtlas outperforms existing methods on text accuracy across four scene text benchmarks, transfers zero-shot to scripts not seen during task-specific training (indicating potential for low-resource languages), and leads in scenes containing many text regions. The authors also note limitations: atlas cells are rendered in a uniform font, so they convey strokes rather than typography, occasionally causing font-weight and color errors, and the appended atlas tokens add inference cost despite the adjustable budget. Exact benchmark numbers are reported in the paper and its supplementary material; see the original article for the full tables. Project page: https://fr0zencrane.github.io/project-glyphatlas/

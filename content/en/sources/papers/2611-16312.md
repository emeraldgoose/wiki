# Alloy: A Multi-Material Physics Engine for Robot Learning

**Source**: [arXiv:2611.16312](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16312) · **Published**: 2026-11-12
**Authors**: Pranay Gupta, Deepanshu Rohilla

## Overview

A useful robot handles towels, cables, water, and sand — but the simulators used to train robots at scale are strongest on rigid bodies, and adding another material usually means a second engine plus hand-written glue between them, hand-chosen time steps, and self-built checks for worlds that have quietly gone wrong. Alloy, from Argon Robotics, is an open-source (Apache-2.0, `pip install alloy-engine`) physics engine for robot learning that puts rigid robots, cloth, cables, liquids, granular material, and soft bodies in one scene. It is built on Newton, MuJoCo Warp, and Warp, runs many parallel worlds on one GPU (or a CPU), and is explicitly designed for a new kind of user: coding agents.

Each object is described once through a single Python interface; a router assigns it to a solver suited to its material and records why. Solvers exchange poses and contact forces through proxy bodies inside every control step, with step sizes chosen from interface stiffness and refined per world when needed. Every parallel world carries a health grade — OK, DEGRADED, or INVALID — and an invalid world is frozen while the others continue, so a physics failure can be told apart from a policy failure. For agents, the documentation ships inside the package, every error names its fix, a command-line tool speaks JSON, and the engine is exposed as tools over the Model Context Protocol.

The headline evidence is a cold test: eight independent sessions of Claude Opus 5.5, each on a fresh VM with one NVIDIA L4 and only what the package ships, installed the pre-release from the package index, wrote their own training and evaluation code, and trained working policies.

## Key points

- Cold-agent results on alloy-engine 0.1.0rc2 (one L4, one training run per row, Wilson 95% intervals): Unitree Go2 walking reached 97% held-out success (497/512) in 4.1 minutes of PPO training with 4,096 worlds; the same policy withstood a 150 N, 0.1 s push — harder than any seen in training (≤113 N) — in 252 and 248 of 256 episodes on two seeds (97–98%).
- Cable lift (a task written by the tester): 94.5% after ~15 minutes and 95.7% after ~30 minutes of PPO on 1,024 worlds; shipped pouring: 92% (118/128) after 38.5 minutes; an agent-written cup-pouring task passed a 75% fill bar in 256/256 held-out episodes on its second reward version after ~70 minutes.
- Imitation: 1,000 scripted demonstrations from 256 worlds, behaviour cloning of a three-layer network in 66 seconds, 256/256 held-out pick-and-place success.
- Throughput scales with world count on one L4: ~39× from 1 to 64 worlds for a Franka Panda scene, ~20× for a cotton sheet, ~13× for a 200 ml pour, and ~377× from 1 to 512 worlds for three objects dropped in a bin.
- Rendering supports image-based learning: at 1,024 worlds on one L4, Alloy renders 70,786 depth images/s at 64×64, 16,111/s at 128×128, and 4,133/s at 256×256; adding segmentation changes the cost by less than 1%.
- Physics correctness is checked against closed-form references: ten selected checks cover rigid-body motion and friction, rod bending/twisting/sagging/buckling, liquid pressure at rest, and soft-solid stress, each compared with a tolerance set before running.
- Reliability: stacking ten 5 cm blocks in 1,024 worlds with ≤0.5 mm start offsets and simulating 5 s produced 0 errors in 1,024 worlds at all three rigid-body settings, in two separate runs (error = any block moving/sinking >1 cm or any non-finite value).

## Details

The paper positions Alloy against rigid-body engines for robot learning (MuJoCo, MuJoCo Warp, Isaac Lab, ManiSkill3) and per-material solvers that usually live in separate programs, and describes scenes as "cards", routing with recorded justifications, coupling, step-size selection, health grading, multi-world/camera/robot execution, and the agent-facing interface (in-package docs, JSON CLI, MCP tool server). The cold-test protocol judged success from raw simulator state with a tester-written script on held-out episodes with different random seeds than training, and no evaluated walking or cable episode was graded INVALID. The authors are explicit that this is an early 0.x release whose interfaces may still change, with known limitations listed publicly.

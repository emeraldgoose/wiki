# Portfolio Risk Bounds without Cross-Asset Return Covariances: Distributional Fields from Language-Model Representations

Source: Marcus Gawronsky, Chun-Sung Huang, arXiv:2608.29692 (q-fin.ST), Aug 30, 2026.

**Lead**: Portfolio risk assessment ordinarily relies on **cross-asset return covariances** — which are hard to estimate in short, high-dimensional panels, where the sample matrix is nearly singular. This paper shows that **firm-level distribution-valued characteristics can instead provide one-sided certificates of portfolio risk**. Under two maintained hypotheses — links from characteristics to systematic exposures, and from exposures to returns — **multi-firm Wasserstein-2 dispersion** yields a sharp upper bound on systematic portfolio variance. In a 52-firm 2018–2022 panel, an allocation built from **Qwen3-Embedding-8B** news representations sits between the **0.69th and 1.33rd in-sample variance percentiles**, against **21.1st–28.6th** for equal risk weighting.

## The estimation problem

Portfolio variance is `wᵀΣw`. That formulation needs `Σ` — the full covariance matrix across assets. In a short, high-dimensional panel the number of assets exceeds the available time samples, so the sample covariance is close to singular and its estimate is unreliable. Everyone hits this; most respond with shrinkage or factor models, both of which impose structure on `Σ`.

This paper takes a different route: **do not estimate `Σ` at all.** Instead, derive a **one-sided certificate** — an upper bound — from data that is easier to estimate.

The substitution is the key move. Instead of observed returns, the inputs are **firm-level distribution-valued characteristics**: language-model embedding distributions over news. Returns are exactly what you cannot estimate reliably in a short panel; text is not subject to that limitation.

## What the bound rests on

Two links are assumed and must be maintained:

1. From **characteristics to systematic exposures**
2. From **exposures to returns**

Under those, **multi-firm Wasserstein-2 dispersion** — the spread of the firms' embedding distributions under optimal transport — yields a **sharp upper bound on systematic portfolio variance**, and correspondingly on standardised returns.

A one-sided bound is a deliberate asymmetry. The method certifies *how bad* the risk can be; it does not claim to estimate it precisely. That is a much weaker claim to defend, and much easier to defend correctly.

## The weighted pairwise relaxation

The practical version is a **weighted pairwise relaxation** producing an objective that is:

- **Convex under a checkable condition** — the convexity is verifiable rather than assumed
- In need of **marginal volatility scales only** — marginals are far easier to estimate than the full covariance

A notable structural result: **with zero firm-specific slack, the common-map scale changes the certified variance reduction but not the normalised allocation.** In other words the allocation rule depends only on the observed information geometry — the scale parameter cancels. That is what makes the resulting rule implementable without additional estimation.

## Results

In a **52-firm panel from 2018–2022**, across four prespecified capped portfolio populations:

| Allocation | In-sample variance percentile |
|---|---|
| **Qwen3-Embedding-8B news representations** | **0.69th – 1.33rd** |
| **Equal risk weighting** | 21.1st – 28.6th |

So the text-derived allocation sits near the bottom of the realised variance distribution, while the standard equal-risk baseline sits around a quarter of the way up. The paper also reports that the **lower in-sample variance ranking relative to equal risk persists across the other reported frozen LM representations** — so the effect is not specific to one embedding model.

## Honest limits

Three things deserve stating plainly:

- These are **in-sample variance percentiles**. A bound that is certified out-of-sample still has to be compared against realised variance in-sample, which flatters it.
- The reported set is the **frozen** LM representations. Representations drift as models are updated; a certificate computed on one snapshot is not automatically valid under another.
- A **52-firm panel** is small, and 0.69–1.33 percentiles is a narrow band. The construction is the contribution; the magnitude is a demonstration.

## Relevance to SW engineers

- When the matrix you need is unidentifiable in your data, look for inputs that are *not* subject to that failure. Returns are scarce in short panels; text is not.
- A one-sided bound is often the right target when estimation is unreliable. "How bad can it be" is answerable when "what is it" is not.
- Cancellation properties are worth hunting for: the common-map scale not affecting the normalised allocation means the implementable rule has one fewer thing to get wrong.
- Report the frozen-representation caveat if you deploy this. Any claim resting on a specific embedding snapshot ages badly.

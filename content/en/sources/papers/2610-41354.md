# OneSteer: Unlocking Steerable General Humanoid Motion Tracking via One-Step Diffusion Whole-Body Control

**Source**: [arXiv:2610.41354](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41354) · **Published**: 2026-10-30
**Authors**: Yifei Huang, Yazhao Yang, Li Weijia, Tianle Shi, Ji Jin, Zhihui Cai, Zihao Liu, Jianzhuang Zhao, Songlin Dong, Xinyuan Chang, Yihong Gong, Dian Shen, Zhiheng Ma

## Overview

General humanoid motion trackers — SONIC, BFM-Scale, MimicLite, GMT, HOVER, OmniH2O and their relatives — follow arbitrary reference motions, but they are deterministic: their behavior is fixed by the reference they are given. Real deployments rarely work that way. A robot may need to clear an obstacle, stabilize a tray, or operate with a degraded motor — objectives that specify a desired outcome without supplying a revised trajectory — while much of the original reference must still be followed. The obvious fallback, editing the kinematic reference under the same cost and handing it to the tracker, fails in practice, because a tracker trained on natural motions simply does not execute unnatural edits.

OneSteer attacks this with a whole-body controller built around a one-step diffusion generator. Conditioned on proprioception and a future reference window, the generator maps input noise directly to a short state–action plan in a single forward pass. For a new objective, differentiable task costs are used to optimize the generator's input noise while the network weights stay frozen; body-group reference masks relax selected tracking requirements while unmasked components remain tracking targets; and an inverse-dynamics head combines the optimized near-term states with the current robot feedback to produce commands, replanning at the next control step. The predicted state plan is thus a shared interface: task optimization shapes it, feedback-conditioned execution realizes it, and one tracking controller is reused across new motion–objective combinations.

The same predictive representation doubles as an empirical difficulty signal. Holding proprioception and the reference fixed and measuring how much the generated plans vary across different noise draws ranks likely tracking failures without any physics rollout — a score the paper uses to select clips for targeted fine-tuning. The controller is trained either by distillation from an RL teacher or from scratch with model-based RL, and is evaluated on held-out AMASS and BONES motions, in simulation across five steering objectives, and on a physical Unitree G1.

## Key points

- Tracking fidelity: trained from scratch, OneSteer has the lowest local tracking error of all six evaluated baselines in a standardized MuJoCo harness — 26.5 mm on AMASS (150 held-out windows) and 22.6 mm on BONES (512 held-out windows, success capped at 96.5% because 18 windows need stairs, ladders or boxes absent from the flat-ground harness) — plus the highest success and lowest median global drift on BONES.
- The distilled student performs on par with its RL teacher (local error 31.4 vs 29.9 mm on AMASS, 27.3 vs 27.0 mm on BONES), so the generative one-step formulation costs no local tracking accuracy.
- Latency: one nominal generator call takes 3.8 ms on an RTX 4090 (5.3 ms for the robot's two calls); the steered second-call setting runs in 10.6–13.7 ms per control step, within the 20 ms period of the 50 Hz loop on the robot's Orin NX at the 95th percentile.
- Hardware: on a physical Unitree G1, OneSteer completes all 27 nominal tracking trials across nine diverse whole-body motion sequences without falling; with a steering cost derived from a signed distance field built only from onboard LiDAR it passes under a 1.1 m low bar in 4 of 5 attempts and steps over a 12 cm hurdle without falling in all 3 attempts.
- Steering beats reference editing: across 79 walking motions of unseen actors, in-controller steering passes the 1.25 m low bar in 0.90 of trials versus at most 0.11 for real-time reference editors (closed-loop editing reaches only 0.60) and 0.68 of hurdles versus at most 0.58; in reaching, real-time steering hits 0.57 versus at most 0.38, nearly matching closed-loop editing (0.60) at under a twentieth of its computation.
- The gap is execution, not planning: where an edit lowers the pelvis by 6.2 cm, the teacher realizes only 1.0 cm, while steered OneSteer realizes 5.9–6.6 cm because its executor is trained to follow its own plans. Controls confirm the mechanism — a random update of equal norm and best-of-eight MPPI sampling yield little improvement, and optimizing the generated plan directly loses the tray's ball in 0.97 of windows.
- Training-free multi-objective adaptation of the same frozen controller: with a knee or hip motor limited to 15% of its torque and only the cost aware of the defect, a torque cost raises survival from 0.71 to 0.79 (knee) and 0.79 to 0.84 (hip); a hand-tilt cost halves tray ball loss (0.16 to 0.08); with leg and upper-body references masked, a velocity cost yields an omnidirectional velocity policy (error 0.112 m/s) and lowers VR wrist error from 24.3 to 16.4 cm. Where the reference does not oppose the cost, masking is harmless.
- Simulation-free failure discovery: the nominal tracker fails on 537 of 7,884 training-library clips (6.8%); ranking clips by the spread of plans across noise draws, 91% of the top 2% are real failures (90% with whole-clip scoring), close to the 93% of imagined world-model rollouts that need a world model, and 86% on the distilled model against an 8.4% base rate.
- The spread score costs nothing beyond the generator and is 11–28x cheaper than the five simulated rollouts per clip that prior work uses for reliable failure labels; over the whole ranking it trails an RL critic and imagined rollouts (AUC 0.83 versus 0.86 and 0.91) but at the top of the ranking — the part selection uses — it comes close.
- Targeted fine-tuning on the 329 clips the score selects fixes 48 ± 4% of the base model's 501 failures on a library rerun, versus 35 ± 6% for 329 random clips and 41 ± 2% for critic-ranked clips, with regressions at 1.8 ± 0.2% and slight cost on 40 hard clips (35.3 vs 33.6 mm); success on 110 held-out failures from unseen skill families rises to 48 ± 2%, above imagined-rollout selection (43 ± 1%).

## Details

### How steering works in the controller

Nominal tracking runs one generator call per control step: proprioception plus a future reference window go in, a short state–action plan comes out in a single forward pass, and an inverse-dynamics head turns the near-term states, combined with current robot feedback, into commands. When a task cost is active, a second guided call first refines the input noise by ascending the cost gradient — the generator is differentiable end-to-end, and with one step there is no iterative denoising chain to unroll — then executes as usual. Body-group masks can hide, say, the legs and upper body from the generator and executor so a velocity cost owns those degrees of freedom, while the visible groups keep following the reference. Because one call tracks as well as two (96.0% / 94.9% success, 31.9 vs 32.0 mm on AMASS), the second call is free to serve steering. A multi-step flow variant of the model is steered the same way with comparable task results; the one-step generator just makes nominal tracking cheaper and the steered motion smoother (steering raises executed-joint jerk from 381 to 571 rad/s³ versus 400 to 915 rad/s³ for a two-Euler-step model).

### Why reference editing loses

The paper's Q2 comparison pits steering inside the generator against a deterministic teacher tracking edited references under the same cost — offline edits, closed-loop re-optimized edits, and editors free to move the root. Edited references do clear the bar on paper, but the teacher cannot execute them: trained on natural motions, it realizes 1.0 cm of a commanded 6.2 cm pelvis lowering, while OneSteer's executor follows its own steered plan (5.9–6.6 cm realized). A planner-plus-tracker pipeline also falls short — the teacher tracking OneSteer's steered plan passes the 1.25 m bar in 0.74 of trials instead of 0.90 — so part of the gain lives in the executor trained jointly with the generator, not only in the plan. Even the direct alternative of optimizing the generated plan (rather than the noise) tilts the planned hand without the matching body motion and jolts the tray.

### Failure discovery and targeted repair without a simulator

Across the 7,884-clip training library the from-scratch model fails 537 clips. Scoring each clip by the spread of plans generated from several noise inputs — no physics rollouts — places real failures at the top of the ranking with 90–91% precision in the top 2%, within reach of imagined world-model rollouts (93%) and far above the 8.4% base rate. Fine-tuning on the 329 highest-ranked clips with the same recipe fixes about 48% of the base model's failures and generalizes to 110 held-out failures from unseen skill families (48 ± 2%), beating random, critic-ranked, and even imagined-rollout selection while staying one of the cheapest options (2.4x cheaper than a single rollout per clip, 11–28x cheaper than the five-rollout labeling of Extreme-RGMT).

### Setup, versions, and what remains open

Training uses either RL-teacher distillation or model-based RL from scratch; evaluation covers AMASS and the BONES-SEED everyday embodiment dataset, with steering settings chosen on training windows and carried unchanged to 79 held-out walking windows and held-out dance. This document is written from arXiv HTML v4 (revised 2026-11-13; v1 submitted 2026-10-30). The authors flag two extensions that the framework already anticipates: vision (depth or egocentric tokens so the plan itself anticipates obstacles) and force control (wrench or tactile tokens so a cost could prescribe contact forces, which a kinematic plan cannot represent); in both cases the generator, steering loop, and failure assessment carry over unchanged.

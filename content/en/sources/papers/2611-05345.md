# Adversarial Speculative Reasoning Improves Long Horizon Agents

**Source**: [arXiv:2611.05345](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05345) · **Published**: 2026-11-03
**Authors**: Hongyin Luo, Wei Fang, Lijie Yang, Owen Stepan, Daniel Suit, Jeff Krapf, Dana Wensberg, Jack O'Brien

## Overview

Speculative decoding has been treated purely as a systems optimization that speeds up large language model decoding without changing model behavior. Under this view, training better draft models has focused only on acceptance rate. The paper argues that this view does not hold for long-horizon agent reasoning. Measurements on frontier open-weight models show that numerical noise grows within verification windows, and draft acceptance of thinking processes can change distribution after 30 steps, noticeably influencing reasoning accuracy in random directions. The proposed method improves target-model reasoning by deliberately training the draft model to be rejected at critical reasoning points. At inference, draft and target part ways at those points, verification restarts, and critical thinking tokens are sampled from the target under less noisy conditions, making them better indexed and more durable.

## Key points

- Speculative decoding is not always behavior-neutral for long-horizon agents.
- Verification-window noise can influence long-horizon outputs.
- Critical reasoning points are identified from agent traces.
- The draft is trained with adversarial preference to be rejected there.
- At those points verification restarts and target sampling resumes.
- The method uses no changes to the target model or its output distribution.

## Details

On complex agentic coding benchmarks, the approach raises end-to-end success rate with only target distribution unchanged and 0.06 to 0.28 percent of hosted parameters trained. The results recast speculative decoding from a pure accelerator into an active allocator for context precision. Effectiveness depends on accurate identification of critical reasoning points and agent trace quality; adversarial training could be risky if mislabeled decisions are used.

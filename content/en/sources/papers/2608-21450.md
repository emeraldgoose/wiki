# Beyond Visual Similarity: Entity-Aligned Retrieval for Knowledge-Based Visual Question Answering

Source: Hangrui Xu, Zhengxian Wu, Yunyao Yu and 6 other authors, arXiv:2608.21450, Aug 19, 2026.

**Lead**: Knowledge-Based Visual Question Answering retrieves external information to answer questions about long-tail entities, and existing pipelines overwhelmingly use **CLIP-style dual encoders that prioritise surface visual similarity over entity-level semantic alignment**. That fails in both directions: semantically identical concepts with large visual variation (different photos of the same landmark), and distinct entities that look similar (different bird species). KBMR is the first **MLLM-based embedding retriever** tailored for KB-VQA, mapping images into a semantic space that preserves concept identity, and pairing it with an MLLM-based **semantic discriminator** that emits continuous entity-consistency weights — enabling effective hard-negative sampling and soft supervision beyond rigid binary labels. Result: up to **14.7% better retrieval Recall@1** and **9.4% higher end-to-end VQA accuracy** over CLIP baselines.

## Why visual similarity is the wrong objective

KB-VQA is the setting where a question references an entity the model doesn't know, so it must **retrieve** the right knowledge. The retrieval step decides whether the pipeline works at all.

CLIP-style dual encoders were trained for image–text alignment at web scale. That objective rewards *visual* resemblance. But KB-VQA needs *entity* resemblance. The gap shows up in two failure modes, and they pull in opposite directions:

- **Same concept, different appearance** — a specific landmark photographed at different times, angles, or weather looks nothing alike. CLIP retrieves something visually similar instead.
- **Different entities, similar appearance** — sibling species, near-identical product variants, a different actor in a similar role. CLIP happily retrieves the wrong one.

The first is a recall failure. The second is worse: it retrieves confidently and the VQA stage then answers from wrong knowledge.

## Approach 1 — MLLM as the embedder

KBMR replaces the dual encoder with an **MLLM-based embedder**, leveraging the model's autoregressive capabilities to map images into a semantic space that better preserves **concept identity**. The reasoning is straightforward: a model that can reason about what an image depicts produces embeddings that track what the image *is*, not what it looks like.

The trade-off implied by the paper is the usual one — generative semantic quality against the retrieval efficiency of a dual encoder.

## Approach 2 — soft supervision instead of binary labels

The second contribution addresses a data problem: at Wikipedia scale, the retrieval supervision signal is **noisy**.

The fix is an **MLLM-based semantic discriminator** that produces **continuous entity-consistency weights** rather than a binary "this is relevant / not relevant" label. These weights feed a continuous semantic distillation objective, which enables:

- **Effective hard negative sampling** — negatives ranked by how close they are to being correct, not by random or BM25 proximity
- **Soft supervision** — partial credit, so a merely-related document doesn't train the model as hard as an exact entity match

This is the part that makes the method work at scale. Hard-negative sampling is where most of the quality comes from, and it needs a relevance signal better than "same page or not".

## Results

Measured against CLIP baselines:

| Metric | Improvement |
|---|---|
| Retrieval Recall@1 | up to **+14.7%** |
| End-to-end VQA accuracy | **+9.4%** |

The two-stage story — better embedder *and* better supervision — is why both numbers move.

## Relevance to SW engineers

- The generalisable lesson: when your retrieval target is *identity* rather than *appearance*, an encoder trained for appearance is the wrong tool, regardless of how good it is at the appearance task.
- Binary relevance labels are a bottleneck at scale. If you can get graded relevance from a model, hard-negative mining gets dramatically better.
- Inspect your retrieval failures in both directions — near-misses and exact-misses need different fixes, and aggregate Recall@1 hides the distinction.

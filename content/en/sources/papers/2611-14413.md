# Bits or permutations? Spending a fixed inference budget on weight precision or on option-order averaging in small local language models

**Source**: [arXiv:2611.14413](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14413) · **Published**: 2026-11-10
**Authors**: Mohammed Nayeem Khan, Logesh L, Aliyas Ahmad Misri

## Overview

Small local language models face two cheap, orthogonal tweaks: store weights in fewer bits to save memory, and average predictions over a few reorderings of the answer options to blunt position bias in multiple-choice evaluation. Both consume budget — one in weight precision, the other in extra forward passes — and this paper asks the joint question the title encodes: does cutting bits make order sensitivity worse, and if you can only afford so much, which purchase pays off more? The authors report that a title-level search found no earlier study combining quantization with option order.

The experimental design is deliberately minimal and fully simulated. Qwen3-0.6B and Qwen3-1.7B are evaluated with round-to-nearest weight quantization at 8, 6, 4, and 3 bits against an fp16 reference, reading the logits of the letters A-D on ARC-Easy, ARC-Challenge, MMLU, and HellaSwag (300 items each). Accuracy barely moves at high precision: 6-bit weights actually score above fp16 by +1.8 points on 0.6B (95% interval +1.0 to +2.5) and +0.9 on 1.7B (+0.3 to +1.5), a small improvement the authors explicitly do not explain, while 8-bit weights change accuracy by only -0.7 and -0.4 points. Sensitivity to option order is a different story: at 4 bits the answer-slot skew rises from 0.15 to 0.53 on 0.6B and from 0.07 to 0.12 on 1.7B.

Two controls frame that skew increase. A Gaussian-noise control at the same weight SNR produces skew of 0.15 and 0.37 across two draws and 0.19 in one draw, so the evidence is inconclusive on whether rounding adds skew beyond what equal-power noise already causes. And although error flip rates rise with quantization, they stay below an accuracy-matched baseline of independent uniform errors, meaning quantized errors are not worse than random ones of the same magnitude. On the remedy side, averaging predictions over four cyclic option orders recovers +12.6 points at 4 bits for 0.6B (versus +5.3 at fp16), making permutation averaging disproportionately valuable precisely when weights are heavily quantized.

The paper's practical contribution is a common price tag. Bits and passes are both converted into one machine-independent unit — weight bytes read times passes — and cost-matched pairs are compared head to head. Extra orders beat extra bits only when the weights carry 6 or more bits, which collapses the decision table to a simple rule: run 6-bit weights and spend the rest of the budget on up to four option orders, one pass each. Everything is simulated on two small models with the simplest quantizer, and the authors are explicit about that scope.

## Key points

- Models and task: Qwen3-0.6B and Qwen3-1.7B, simulated round-to-nearest weight quantization at 8, 6, 4, and 3 bits with fp16 as reference; evaluation reads A-D letter logits on ARC-Easy, ARC-Challenge, MMLU, and HellaSwag with 300 items each.
- Accuracy vs precision: 6 bits lose nothing — scoring +1.8 points (+1.0 to +2.5) on 0.6B and +0.9 points (+0.3 to +1.5) on 1.7B above fp16, an unexplained slight gain; 8 bits shift accuracy by -0.7 (-1.1 to -0.3) and -0.4 (-0.7 to -0.2) points.
- Order sensitivity: answer-slot skew climbs from 0.15 to 0.53 (0.6B) and from 0.07 to 0.12 (1.7B) at 4 bits, so the smaller model is far more exposed to option-order bias under heavy quantization.
- Noise control: Gaussian perturbation at matched weight SNR yields skew 0.15 and 0.37 over two draws and 0.19 over one draw — inconclusive on whether rounding adds slot skew beyond equal-power noise.
- Error character: flip rates rise with quantization but remain below an accuracy-matched independent-uniform-error baseline, so quantization errors are not adversarial relative to random errors at the same accuracy.
- Permutation remedy: averaging four cyclic option orders gains +12.6 points at 4 bits on 0.6B, against +5.3 at fp16 — order averaging pays more exactly where quantization hurts most.
- Budget accounting: bits and passes are priced in a machine-independent unit, weight bytes read multiplied by passes; cost-matched pairs show extra orders beating extra bits only at 6 or more bits per weight.
- Recommended policy from the decision table: 6-bit weights, then spend the remaining budget on up to four option orders with one pass each.
- Scope caveats stated by the authors: everything is simulated on two small models with the simplest quantizer; a title-level literature search found no earlier study of quantization together with option order. Paper length: 25 pages, 15 figures, 9 tables.

## Details

### Why the question matters

Local deployments compress weights to fit memory ceilings, and multiple-choice evaluations are known to be sensitive to the order in which options are presented; permutation averaging is a standard mitigation. If quantization amplified that order sensitivity, the two tricks would fight each other, and a practitioner with a fixed inference budget would need to know how to split it. This paper is, to the authors' knowledge, the first to measure the interaction and to price both levers in a single unit.

### What was measured

Three quantities carry the results: accuracy against the fp16 reference, answer-slot skew (how much predictions depend on which option sits in which letter slot), and flip rates of errors relative to an accuracy-matched uniform-error baseline. The Gaussian-noise control matches the signal power that rounding injects into the weights, isolating whether the skew increase is specifically a rounding artifact. The budget unit — weight bytes read times passes — lets a 6-bit model with four permutation passes be compared against an 8-bit model with one pass on equal footing.

### Reading of the results

The headline is the asymmetry: accuracy is forgiving down to 6 bits (even oddly improving), while order sensitivity degrades earlier and more sharply on the smaller model. Because the noise control is inconclusive, the authors stop short of claiming rounding uniquely causes the skew increase; they report that the control could not separate rounding from equal-power noise. The recovery from four-order averaging being larger at 4 bits than at fp16 is the strongest evidence that the two budget levers interact, and it is what motivates the final recommendation: buy 6-bit weights first, then spend the residual budget on up to four option-order permutations.

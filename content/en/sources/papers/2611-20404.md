# Latent Feedback Is a Compute and Data Amplifier

**Source**: [huggingface](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20404)
**Date**: 2026-11-16

## Overview

When high-quality training data runs out, the common remedy is to repeat the best sources for several epochs, but each additional pass yields less than the last. Latent feedback — feeding each token's top-layer state back into the next token's input — is another way to spend that extra compute. This paper asks whether it beats the fair baseline: a vanilla Transformer given the same FLOPs to train for more epochs. Prior latent-feedback work compared at fixed training tokens and never tested repeated data, so the question had remained open.

The authors run a controlled ladder of experiments spanning model sizes from 60M to 778M parameters, unique data from 12.5 to 400 unique tokens per non-embedding parameter (UTPP), and one to four epochs (up to 1,600 training tokens per parameter). Both the latent-feedback model and the vanilla Transformer are tuned at every size and every amount of unique data, and the unique-token pools are nested and deduplicated so comparisons are clean.

The headline finding is that latent feedback acts as an amplifier rather than a fixed win: at fixed unique data it reaches a Transformer's loss with less compute, and at fixed epochs it reaches that loss with less unique data. Both savings grow with unique data per parameter and with repetition, and downstream task results agree with the loss curves. The one exception is the regime where unique data is scarce relative to model size — a corner today's models are far from.

## Key points

- Latent feedback feeds each token's top-layer state into the next token's input through a learned map; the paper uses a feedback rate of p = 0.15, adding roughly 1.20–1.27x compute cost depending on model size.
- Across the full grid, latent feedback beats a vanilla Transformer given equal FLOPs, except when unique data is very scarce relative to model size.
- With unique data fixed, latent feedback matches a Transformer's loss using less compute; with epochs fixed, it matches that loss using less unique data — at most saving 48% of compute and 47% of unique data.
- Both savings increase as unique data per parameter grows and as data is repeated more times.
- A single scaling law fitted to both models treats latent feedback as a Transformer with more effective parameters; fitted on 60M–271M models, it predicts held-out 778M losses to within 0.3% and the gap between the two models to within 0.05%.
- When data, not compute, is the binding constraint, latent feedback is the more compute-efficient choice for a given model size.

## Details

**Method.** Both architectures share one modern decoder backbone (pre-norm RMSNorm, rotary position embeddings, QK-norm, SwiGLU, untied embeddings, OLMo 2-style initialization) at 60M, 134M, 271M and 778M parameters. Matrices are trained with NorMuon (a normalized Muon variant) and embeddings/output head with AdamW. The latent-feedback variant adds the top-layer-to-next-input pathway with p = 0.15. Training data is a deduplicated sample of Dolma 3 with any documents sharing paragraphs with the Paloma benchmark removed, so Paloma serves as a clean held-out evaluation; documents are ordered so every prefix preserves the whole sample's source and topic mixture, making the unique-data pools strictly nested. Each (model size, unique data, budget) cell is a tuned run over learning rate and timescale. The authors then fit one joint scaling law to both models in which latent feedback enters only through a capacity multiplier (three fitted coefficients plus its measured cost factor); all other coefficients are shared.

**Results.** Latent feedback's advantage is expressed through a compute multiplier (Transformer budget over latent-feedback budget at equal unique data and loss) and a data multiplier (Transformer unique data over latent-feedback unique data at equal epochs and loss). Both multipliers grow with unique data per parameter and with repetition, meaning the amplifier effect compounds exactly in the data-constrained regime where repeated epochs are the default practice. The joint scaling law, fitted on 60M–271M models, extrapolates to a held-out 778M size with losses within 0.3% and the inter-model gap within 0.05%, and predicts that both savings keep growing with more unique data and more repetition. The maximum observed savings are 48% of compute and 47% of unique data. Downstream evaluations agree with the validation-loss picture.

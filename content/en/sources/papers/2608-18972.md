# Institutional Newspapers Pipeline: Deriving Billions of High-Quality Tokens from Historical Newspapers

Source: Matteo Cargnelutti, Catherine Brobston, Eben English and 6 other authors, arXiv:2608.18972, Aug 19, 2026.

**Lead**: Historical newspapers are an abundant record of public life, but their dense, irregular, sometimes noisy layouts make computational access both hard and limited. This paper presents a modular system built jointly with the **Boston Public Library** that extracted a structured dataset from historical scans, architected so every step stays interpretable and customizable and so the whole pipeline runs on **workstation-class hardware**. The released OCR output is **16.3 billion o200k_base tokens across 83.1 million individual crops, from 1,473,635 public-domain scans published between 1795 and 1930**. The novelty is less the scale than the architecture: interpretable, replaceable stages, each backed by a small trained model, with per-crop reading order, type classification, NER, subject classification, language detection, and pre-computed embeddings.

## The problem with newspaper scans

Newspapers are one of the richest public records of how societies actually lived, but they are hostile to computational access:

- **Dense and irregular layouts** — multi-column stories, boxes, advertisements interleaved with articles
- **Noisy** — fading, bleed-through, stains, warping, torn folds
- **Long-tail typography** — historical fonts, ligatures, and typefaces that modern OCR rarely handles

Naive whole-page OCR produces a wall of interleaved text with no idea which column belongs to which article. The result is technically a corpus and practically unusable.

## Architecture: interpretable, modular, frugal

The design goals, stated plainly, are the interesting part of the paper:

1. **Each step interpretable and customizable** — you can inspect and swap any stage
2. **Computationally frugal** — runs on workstation hardware, not a cluster
3. **Modular** — designed jointly with a real institution (BPL), so the interfaces reflect what an actual archive needs

Rather than OCR a whole page at once, the pipeline **segments each scan into individual type-agnostic crops** and runs OCR on each segment. Everything downstream operates per-crop.

## The processing chain

Every crop is pushed through the full chain:

1. **Segmentation** into type-agnostic crops
2. **OCR** on each crop
3. **Text analysis** on the resulting text
4. **Type classification** — what kind of content is this crop (article, ad, obituary, editorial, etc.)
5. **Reading order detection** — where does this crop sit in the page's sequence
6. **Named entity recognition**
7. **Subject classification** — what is this about
8. **Language detection**
9. **Pre-computed embedding generation**

The report covers the methods for each step, the **small models** trained for them, and the evaluation and dataset-scale measurements gathered along the way.

## Scale of the released dataset

| Measure | Value |
|---|---|
| Scans | **1,473,635** public-domain newspapers |
| Publication years | **1795–1930** |
| Individual crops | **83,100,000** |
| OCR tokens (o200k_base) | **16.3 billion** |

**16.3B tokens** is a serious pretraining or retrieval corpus. What makes it more useful than a raw dump is that each of the 83.1M crops carries type, reading order, NER, subject, language, and an embedding — so you can query by structure and meaning, not just string match.

## Why "workstation-runnable" matters

Most institutional digitization pipelines assume cluster-scale compute, which means most institutions can't run them. The frugal-architecture constraint here is what makes the system deployable at Boston Public Library scale at all. It is a deployment decision that shapes the model choices: small, per-step models rather than one large end-to-end model.

## Relevance to SW engineers

- Segment-then-process beats whole-document processing whenever layout carries meaning (documents, scans, screenshots, slide decks).
- Pre-computing embeddings and structural labels at ingest converts a one-off digitization cost into a permanently queryable asset.
- Interpretable, swappable stages are what let an institution adopt a pipeline incrementally — and what let you debug bad extractions without re-running everything.

# An Empirical Study on Zero-Data Bootstrapping for Conversational Recommender Systems

Source: Rohan Surana, Junda Wu, Zhouhang Xie and 3 other authors, arXiv:2504.15476 (v2), Apr 21, 2025.

**Lead**: Conversational Recommender Systems normally require domain-specific dialogue data — which is costly, scarce, and often simply unavailable in a new domain. This paper runs a systematic empirical study of **zero-data CRS bootstrapping**: generating synthetic conversational supervision from *non-conversational* signals — item reviews, metadata, and user-item interactions — with no in-domain dialogue corpus at all. Across two information-theoretic selection strategies (**Jensen-Shannon diversity** and **Fisher information**), multiple domain signals, architectures, datasets, and fine-tuning paradigms, domain-grounded synthetic data consistently beat zero-shot prompting and naive synthetic baselines; active selection beats random sampling on data efficiency; and in low-resource settings synthetic data can outright **outperform scarce real dialogues** while still complementing them.

## The constraint that makes this interesting

A conversational recommender has to be trained on dialogue: user utterances, system responses, the recommendation that followed. That data is domain-specific by construction — a recommender for outdoor gear has never seen a conversation about camping stoves. Getting it means either building it, buying it, or hoping users generate it, and in a genuinely new domain **none of these are available**.

The usual fallback is prompting a general LLM to invent dialogues. The paper's premise is that this throws away the one asset a recommender *does* have: structured non-conversational signal about the domain.

## What "zero-data" means here

Not "no data" — **no dialogue data**. The available signals are:

- **Item reviews** — free text that mentions what the product does, who it's for, what it's compared against
- **Item metadata** — category, attributes, price band
- **User-item interactions** — implicit feedback: what users viewed, clicked, bought

From these, the study synthesises conversational supervision. The review text in particular is a rich source: a review is, informally, a conversation about a product that already happened.

## The two selection strategies

The study compares two information-theoretic criteria for *which* synthetic examples to keep:

1. **Jensen-Shannon divergence** — select for examples that are maximally distinct from what you already have (maximise coverage/diversity)
2. **Fisher information** — select for examples that most reduce uncertainty about the model (maximise information gain)

These pull in different directions: diversity spreads coverage, Fisher concentrates on what the model is currently uncertain about. The paper measures both across domain signals, model architectures, datasets, and fine-tuning paradigms rather than declaring a winner upfront.

## Findings

- **Domain-grounded synthetic data consistently outperforms** zero-shot prompting and naive synthetic baselines. The grounding in real domain signal is what does the work.
- **Active selection improves data efficiency over random sampling** — so which synthetic examples you keep matters more than how many you generate.
- **Metadata and collaborative-filtering signals each improve selection quality.** They are complementary: metadata covers the descriptive axis, collaborative filtering covers the behavioural one.
- **In low-resource settings, synthetic data can outperform scarce real dialogues** — and still complements them when real data does exist.

That last point is the practically important one: this is not synthetic-data-as-replacement. It is synthetic-data-as-a-way-to-get-unstuck-when-real-data-is-insufficient.

## Relevance to SW engineers

- Before commissioning a data pipeline for a new conversational surface, ask what non-conversational signal already exists. Reviews and metadata are usually sitting there unused.
- Generate broadly, select deliberately — the selection criterion is where the data efficiency comes from, not the generator.
- Treat synthetic data as a complement with a role, not a wholesale substitute. The complementarity finding is stronger evidence than the replacement finding.

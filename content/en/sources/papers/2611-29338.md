# A Glimpse of Training VLMs as Foundation Models for Robotic Manipulation

**Source**: [arXiv:2611.29338](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.29338) · **Published**: 2026-11-22 (v1)
**Authors**: Meng-Hao Guo, Yi Zhang, Zhe-Han Mo, Han-Zheng Wang, Jia-Jun Wang, Xin-Sheng Chen, Jiaxi Song, Yi-Xuan Deng, Jia-Ning Li, Xuanyu Chu, Hengrui Zhang, Huan-ang Gao, Yongming Rao, Shi-Min Hu

## Overview

Recent evaluations of GPT-6 Astra suggest general-purpose VLMs can manipulate robots without task-specific fine-tuning, but the training recipe behind it is undisclosed. This paper asks how such capability can be reproduced with an accessible recipe and answers with nanoAstra, a two-part approach for training VLMs as manipulation foundation models. The first part, semantic action modeling, re-organizes robot behavior: instead of supervising densely sampled control steps (action chunks), demonstrations are segmented at behavioral decision points into semantic steps — reusable, parameterized action primitives such as `move x +5.0` or `gripper open`, each paired with an explicit progress description of what the robot should accomplish next. The second part, capability-preserving supervised fine-tuning (CP-SFT), uses a robot-specific system prompt that pins the embodiment, coordinate conventions, and executable action space, constraining manipulation outputs to that structured action space so fine-tuning specializes the model for robots while interfering minimally with its pretrained vision-language abilities.

The data pipeline unifies heterogeneous sources — ABC-130K, AgiBot, HiFi-UMI, HY-UMI, spanning real robots and hand-held gripper recordings — through five stages: filtering, convention/camera alignment, subtask segmentation with dense captions, semantic-step construction, and mixing with general VQA data, yielding more than 5,000 hours of robot data. nanoAstra-9B is initialized from Qwen3.5-9B and fully fine-tuned for one epoch on 56 H20 GPUs (effective batch 56 packs, ~188 samples each, bf16, ZeRO-2, 8,192-token sequences). A modified Wasserstein number-token loss with decimal-place weighting and explicit sign supervision improves numerical precision of action values.

## Key points

- Zero-shot manipulation: on RoboTwin 2.0's randomized benchmark (50 tasks, 10 runs per task, real-world data only — no simulated trajectories or benchmark fine-tuning), nanoAstra-9B scores 17.8% success rate, versus 10.8% for the best VLA baseline (LingBot-VLA-2.0; others 1.4–2.4%), near-zero WAMs (DreamZero 0.2%, LingBot-VA 0.0%), and 8.2–10.8% for similarly sized or larger VLMs with thinking (3.2–4.0% without).
- Streaming execution: because inference on a single H20 is faster than the corresponding motion execution, steps can execute as they stream in, with the next turn requested before the current one finishes. On RoboTwin 2.0 with the simulator kept running during inference, streaming cuts robot idle time from 35.0% to 6.9% (wait 2.1 → 0.2 steps per turn) and raises success within the official time limit from 11.2% to 12.9%.
- Transfer beyond the training benchmark: on RoboDojo Open (8 tasks, 400 episodes per model), nanoAstra-9B reaches 4.75% success (19/400) versus 0.25% for its untuned Qwen3.5-9B counterpart, the highest SR and progress score among reported models.
- Capability retention: VQA benchmarks (MMMU, ERQA, CV-Bench-2D/3D) dip early in training then partially recover — final scores 53.1% / 41.0% / 81.6% / 87.6% against initial 58.6% / 47.3% / 82.2% / 92.6%, i.e. gaps of roughly 5.0–6.3 points on the vision benchmarks — showing manipulation learning coexists with substantial though incomplete retention of general VLM ability.
- Failure analysis: representative failures accumulate motion-parameterization and task-state estimation errors over closed-loop turns (e.g. partially opening a microwave door with straight-line translations; progress drifting back to completed subtasks or distractor objects), motivating object-centric primitives for articulated objects and explicit completed-subtask tracking.

## Details

Section 2 details the pipeline: data filtering, convention and camera alignment, subtask segmentation with dense captions, semantic step construction at decision points (each observation paired with a progress description plus an executable action sequence), and a joint recipe mixing robot data with general VQA examples. CP-SFT (Sec. 2.2) softly constrains the response decoding space to the structured action format and adds the modified Wasserstein number-token loss (decimal-place weights, sign term over sign-flipped token pairs, digit-only normalization) on top of mean cross-entropy, with loss averaged over all original samples in an optimizer step across GPUs and gradient accumulation. Section 3 covers settings (Qwen3.5-9B init, ms-swift, AdamW peak LR with cosine schedule, warmup 0.01, weight decay 0.1, 392 visual tokens per image), the RoboTwin 2.0 and RoboDojo benchmarks, training-process monitoring (loss EMA ~0.35 at the end), and failure analysis. Section 4 reviews VLA/WAM/VLM-agent baselines and embodied data pipelines. Code and project page: nanoastra.github.io.

# Consensus Capture in Reference-Based UAV Video Stabilization: A Benchmark on Dynamic Urban Scenes

**Source**: [arXiv:2611.17190](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17190) · **Published**: 2026-11-12
**Authors**: Robert Fonod, Nikolas Geroliminis

## Overview

Reference-frame stabilization registers every frame of a hovering-drone video onto one reference frame, keeping object positions comparable over time. The registration pipelines it relies on were selected on feature-matching benchmarks in which only the camera moves. Over a busy intersection the situation inverts: vehicles moving together supply mutually consistent correspondences that can draw the robust homography estimate toward their motion instead of the road. The authors name this failure consensus capture, and its danger is that inlier statistics cannot flag it — a captured estimate keeps typical inlier counts and ratios, so a pipeline captured on a few frames passes the error, unflagged, to every trajectory in those frames.

The paper measures the failure and its standard countermeasure, object masking, with a two-part benchmark on footage of Songdo, South Korea. The static protocol scores ten pipelines under exactly known homographies and a photometric degradation chain on 4K aerial frames, tuned on a site-disjoint split at a matched resolution and feature budget. The dynamic protocol scores the same pipelines at an offline and a real-time budget on nine hovers selected from the video alone, plus a stationary control, against a pseudo-ground-truth reference estimator audited with manually annotated ground control points, which also score every candidate directly.

The findings are concrete. On static scenes the tuned classical detectors lead and masking is nearly free. On dynamic scenes unmasked ORB is captured repeatedly at a median corner error of 18.8 px, about a meter on the ground, yet looks healthy by every usual diagnostic. Masking removes capture for every pipeline at both budgets while leaving the leading pipelines unchanged, which makes real-time ORB usable for the first time in this setting.

## Key points

- The static protocol uses 4K frames from 29 viewpoints — ten drones recorded 20 intersections over Songdo — with paired trials so every pipeline faces identical perturbations.
- Ten pipelines are studied: ORB, SIFT, RootSIFT, BRISK and AKAZE, four learned sparse ones (DISK, DeDoDe, Key.Net with LightGlue, XFeat) and detector-free LoFTR; the robust-estimator axis sweeps nine OpenCV method codes spanning seven algorithms.
- Static perturbations reach translation up to 10 percent of each frame dimension, rotation up to 15 degrees, scale within 5 percent, plus brightness, saturation, blur, haze, noise and JPEG degradation.
- The budget sweep crossed four downsampling ratios (0.18, 0.30, 0.50, 1.0) with three feature budgets (1000, 2000, 8000); no budget met the tuning criterion, so every pipeline runs at full resolution with 8000 features.
- Tuned AKAZE leads the static table at 0.9845 mAA with HBB masking, ahead of RootSIFT at 0.9830 and SIFT at 0.9828; retuning matcher thresholds raises mAA by at most 0.0062 and masking costs less than 0.005.
- Unmasked ORB is captured on 28 of 304 real-time dynamic pairs (22 of them in a single case) and on 6 offline pairs; captured real-time estimates have a median corner error of 18.8 px.
- The 17 captured pairs within the first second are pulls toward the moving group; 10 of the 11 beyond it are estimate breakdowns.
- Masking removes capture for every pipeline at both budgets; HBB and OBB mask geometries do not differ at these densities.
- Masked real-time ORB registers in 12 to 13 ms at a median error of 1.17 to 1.19 px against the audited ground control points; SIFT, RootSIFT and AKAZE take 18 to 22 ms at 0.51 to 0.57 px.
- The oriented annotations are released as Songdo Vision OBB: 274,190 OBB over 5419 frames, selected and validated against 4245 human-verified OBB on 50 frames from six recordings, with zero fallback boxes.

## Details

### Why internal metrics miss capture

The measured datasets that reference-frame stabilization feeds were mostly recorded at lower altitudes than the 140 to 150 m studied here — inD from up to 100 m at about 4 cm per pixel, CitySim intersections from 120 m — so a vehicle group of the same size covers a larger share of the frame and of its features. Captured estimates keep the inlier counts and ratios of the same pipeline at the nearest offsets of the same case, at both budgets, so nothing in the usual per-frame diagnostics separates a pulled estimate from a good one. The median corner error of 18.8 px is what every trajectory built on such frames inherits.

### The two protocols

In the static part the perturbation is the ground truth: known translations, rotations about the frame center, scale and a small projective term are applied together with the photometric chain, and trials are paired across pipelines. From half to full resolution at 8000 features, the unmasked mAA of ORB, XFeat, KeyNet, BRISK and AKAZE still rises by 0.017 to 0.048, so the ceiling is where the benchmark operates. In the dynamic part, thresholds are re-tuned in each regime, cases are selected from the video alone to span the amount and coherence of moving foreground, and the stationary control separates removal of moving objects from removal of texture.

### Masking mechanics and practical guidance

Masking acts on feature detection, and no keypoint inside a mask reaches matching. ORB, BRISK and AKAZE detect outside the mask and refill the budget; SIFT drops responses inside the mask; the learned detectors accept no mask, so their in-mask keypoints are dropped after selection. In practice, offline masking offers little a practitioner could act on, since every configuration lies within the repeatability of the ground control points. In real time it makes ORB usable; beyond 30 degrees of rotation the upright learned models drop out, and code, data and results are released.

# CANTraj: Recovering Vehicle Trajectories from CAN Signals in Naturalistic Driving

**Source**: [arXiv:2611.18442](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.18442) · **Published**: 2026-11-13
**Authors**: Yuhang Wang, Qiushi Wang, Rongqing Zhu, Yixiao Shi, Zucheng Cheng, Xiaohui Li, Zhiming Lu, Xinbing Fu, Tianyue Wang, Jiaqi Li, Songwu Lu

## Overview

Open-driving data releases and usage-based insurance products often claim they protect privacy by stripping GPS coordinates from vehicle logs. This paper shows those claims are false comfort. Removing GPS does not erase the location information carried by ordinary vehicle signals: steering angle, speed, and driving events, once matched to road networks, reconstruct much of the route. Prior route-inference attacks have demonstrated the principle, but their results were hard to compare — inconsistent assumptions, inconsistent metrics, and small, restricted datasets.

The authors fix that with two public artifacts. CANTraj is a naturalistic-driving dataset pairing decoded CAN events with time-aligned GNSS trajectories (3,940 drives, 454 drivers). CANTraj-Bench is a controlled evaluation suite for trajectory recovery (3,675 drives, 431 drivers, 1,380 hours, 85,919 km). Under explicit information-access protocols, paired comparisons, and metrics that count failures as well as successes, they compare six re-implemented attacks and several benchmark baselines.

The headline finding: combining steering with road-network constraints gives the largest single gain. With a known start and heading, adding steering to speed lifts an online map filter's five-minute route recovery from 56% to 87%, with a median average displacement error of 43 m versus 921 m for map-free dead reckoning. Offline beam search recovers 88% of test routes with the heading known and 81% without it. And because the methods fail on different windows, an LLM harness that selects among candidate routes on 659 five-minute windows recovers 79% of routes on test and 83% on few-shot — outperforming the best baseline on test and matching it on few-shot.

## Key points

- Public release: CANTraj dataset (3,940 drives / 454 drivers) and CANTraj-Bench (3,675 drives / 431 drivers / 1,380 hours / 85,919 km), both with decoded CAN events time-aligned to GNSS.
- Explicit protocols: evaluation uses explicit information-access protocols (what the attacker knows), paired comparisons (same drives across methods), and metrics that include failures, so results are comparable across studies.
- Steering is the decisive signal: with a known start and heading, adding steering to speed raises online map-filter five-minute route recovery from 56% to 87% (median ADE 43 m vs 921 m for map-free dead reckoning).
- Offline beam search: 88% of test routes recovered with heading known, 81% without — strong even for a modest information budget.
- LLM harness: a selector over candidate routes for 659 five-minute windows reaches 79% on test and 83% on few-shot, beating the best baseline on test and matching it few-shot.
- Privacy implication: removing GPS from drive logs does not stop route reconstruction from CAN signals; open-driving data releases need stronger mitigation.

## Details

Six attacks are re-implemented from the literature, alongside simple baselines, and all are run under the same information-access protocols. Three protocol tiers matter. In the weakest setting the attacker knows nothing; mid-tier protocols grant known start location or known start heading; the strongest grants both. Metrics are paired: the same five-minute windows are scored for every method, and a "route recovery" is counted only when the matched road sequence stays within a tolerance over the window. Failures are counted explicitly rather than dropped, which is what makes the percentages trustworthy and the comparison fair.

The steering result comes from an online particle map filter: candidate positions are propagated forward using speed, pruned against the road network, and re-weighted by steering constraints. Steering cuts the ambiguity of purely speed-based extrapolation enough to lift five-minute recovery by 31 percentage points. Map-free dead reckoning — no road constraints at all — drifts to a median ADE of 921 m over five minutes, which is why the road-network constraint is the other half of the win.

The offline beam search works on the same windows but searches the road graph backward from partial signal rather than committing greedily forward, which accounts for the 88%/81% recovery gap between heading-known and heading-unknown settings. The LLM harness is the most novel piece: instead of picking one candidate route, it sees several and is asked which best explains the observed CAN window. Selection across diverse candidates is where language reasoning adds measurable value on top of the geometric attacks, and the few-shot number shows it generalizes when given a handful of examples.

Threat model and scope: the work targets casual data releases and insurance products, not targeted stalking of a memorized vehicle. The authors are explicit that the results quantify typical privacy risk, not worst case. Code and data are public, so the numbers can be audited and the attacks reproduced.

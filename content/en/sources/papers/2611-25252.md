# Moving Generality from Model to Modeler: LLM Agents for PHM on a Deployment-Style Benchmark

**Source**: [arXiv:2611.25252](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25252) · **Published**: 2026-11-18 (v1)
**Authors**: Zhenling Chen, Zhiguo Zeng, Anne Barros

## Overview

Coding agents built on large language models now help develop many models published as general solutions. The paper asks whether generality should instead reside in the agent that builds a model for each new task, and tests this in prognostics and health management (PHM), where every model must work on equipment it has never seen. The deployment-style benchmark NextUnit-Bench covers 24 public datasets split into 58 folds that hold out units or operating conditions; each agent gets two hours to deliver an executable predictor, which is then run on the hidden units. The agents win: frontier agents outperform AutoML, which reaches less than half the best agent's score, and faithful implementations of published methods on four datasets, of which only 6 of 30 beat the strongest agent's own design. Published accuracies, where reproducible, rest on the papers' own conventions — a single convention, capping the remaining useful life of test engines, can halve the reported error on C-MAPSS.

## Key points

- Agents beat the literature and AutoML on unseen equipment: of 30 faithful implementations of 20 published methods across four datasets, only 6 beat the strongest agent's own design.
- The advantage is a per-machine design: a plain-text plan of a few hundred words (no code) written down from a delivered design carries most of the score gap between two frontier agents; given a weaker agent's plan, the strongest agent falls to the weaker agent's level, often to identical predictions.
- The design is made on the data: plans written without seeing any data are worth only about 1.5 points more than a weaker frontier agent's runs, and domain toolkits help neither; what separates the strongest agents is how much they learn from the data.
- Local implementers cannot always execute the best designs: given the strongest agent's designs, a locally deployable open-weight model delivers a working predictor about half as often, even when the designs are lightened, and scores no higher than without them.
- Ablations on the ~18-point gap between Qwen3.6 standard runs and the weakest frontier agent: weight precision (4/8/16-bit) moved scores by a few inconsistent points, a GPU changed little, doubling the budget to four hours cut failures from 23 to 12 of 58 without moving the score, a workflow skill gained 6.4 points in one run and nothing in its repeat (the two runs differed by 7.5 points); failure reading shows missing engineering judgment — estimating step cost, securing a working version first, and learning the evaluator interface (of 100 failed runs, half were slow, a third looped on an empty-output evaluator complaint, and 45% of timeouts never submitted anything).
- Conclusion: generality is not disappearing but moving, from the published model to the modeler (human or agent) beside the data.

## Details

Benchmark protocol: 24 public PHM datasets, 58 folds with held-out units or operating conditions, two-hour budget, executable predictor executed on hidden units. Experiments include plan-transfer between agents (frontier implementer landing plan-author results; 21 of 23 Qwen landings fall on frontier-landed folds), no-data plan ablations, toolkit and precision/GPU/time/skill ablations with Wilson 95% intervals, and a failure taxonomy. Discussion connects the findings to protocol overfitting in the agent era and to data-scarce plants where knowledge must be brought in from outside.

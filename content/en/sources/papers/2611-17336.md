# Back to Pixels: Lifting Latent Video Diffusion into Pixel Space

**Source**: [arXiv:2611.17336](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17336) · **Published**: 2026-11-12
**Authors**: Yanke Song, Shuo Chen, Chris Rockwell, Guande He, Nan Liu, Desai Xie, Tianpei Gu, Yong-Hyun Park, Aryan V S, Dennis Yuren Menn, Ming-Hsuan Yang, Xun Huang

## Overview

Frontier video models generate in the latent space of a 3D VAE that compresses video in both space and time. That compression made large-scale training tractable, but the field's new demands run against it. Under a real-time compute budget, doubling the resolution yields four times the latent tokens and sixteen times the attention compute. Precise editing needs conditions aligned to individual frames, yet when several frames share one latent, fast motion blurs and objects deform. And because latent world models autoregressively generate frames in multi-frame blocks, a control cannot take effect until every control in its block has arrived — a control-to-screen floor proportional to the block size that no throughput optimization can remove.

PixVid removes the VAE entirely and turns a pretrained latent model into a frame-wise pixel-space generator. The diffusion transformer is kept; the VAE is replaced at the input by a linear patch embedding — initialized by fusing the pretrained patch embedding with an offline-fitted linear map between pixel patches and latents, plus a timestep calibration so signal-to-noise ratios align — and at the output by a lightweight convolutional detailer head that renders DiT tokens directly into pixels. Every token covers a 1-by-p-by-p patch of exactly one frame. The detailer is decoder-only (it reads just the DiT tokens, not noisy pixels), stacks log2-p up blocks, runs its convolutions across neighboring tokens to suppress patch-grid seams, and is causal in time so it supports frame-by-frame autoregressive generation.

Three training choices make the lift work: predicting clean video (x-prediction) rather than velocity, since velocity forces the network to model full-rank per-pixel noise; a larger noise shift than the latent model, since redundant pixels retain more signal at the same noise level; and the Euler sampler, because pixel colors are numerically sensitive to higher-order solvers — UniPC under a bf16 sampler state keeps only 91 percent of fp32 chroma while Euler keeps it all. Auxiliary Charbonnier frame-difference and LPIPS losses, weighted toward low noise, train the from-scratch temporal convolutions.

## Key points

- Finetuned from Wan2.1-T2V-14B, PixVid converges within 1.3M training clips sampled from 64K videos, about 4K B200 GPU-hours, and scores VBench total 84.78 versus 84.05 for the latent base model and 83.72 for the concurrent pixel-space model PixelUMM; a 100-prompt pairwise human study prefers PixVid in every comparison.
- The only semantic dimension where it trails the latent base by more than a point is color (81.59 versus 84.73).
- High-resolution: doubling the patch size from 16 to 32 generates 960x1664 with the same 30x52 token grid per frame as 480x832, reaching the paper's best VBench total of 85.09 with the best imaging, aesthetic, background-consistency, and temporal-flickering scores; a 21-frame clip costs 32,760 tokens and 192 seconds on one B200 versus 37,440 tokens and 176 seconds for the latent model.
- Precise editing: adding the VACE control branch and finetuning PixVid-1.3B to re-render video from per-frame depth, it keeps 98 percent of source motion on fast clips versus 83 percent for an identically finetuned latent model and at most 63 percent for zero-shot systems, lowering motion error by 40 percent and depth error by 20 percent, with the lowest temporal LPIPS (0.030) and FVMD (1512); its FVD trails the latent variant (118 versus 91).
- Low-latency interactive generation: distilled into a few-step causal model with Self Forcing, PixVid uses single-frame blocks, so each camera control takes effect at the next frame — median control-to-screen latency 34 ms versus 495 ms for SolarWM, a 14x reduction, with zero-frame maximum control wait versus 12, and better tracking of abrupt turns (turn correlation 0.95 versus 0.70) because it receives a pose for every frame rather than one per four-frame latent.
- The latency model trades some throughput (29.4 versus 72.0 fps) and shows higher camera rotation and translation errors over long streams (5.44 versus 4.24 degrees), which the authors attribute to pose drift that is comparable at 21 frames.
- Head ablation: the convolutional head cuts the median patch-seam measure from 118.6 (linear head) to 13.3; a U-Net head with noisy-input encoding reaches 9.2 but needs 5.6 times more parameters (248M versus 44M at 1.3B) and slightly more noise and flicker.
- Prediction-target ablation: with the same head, v-prediction fills frames with flickering high-frequency noise at 80 times the pixel-noise measure of x-prediction (62.25 versus 0.78); asymmetric v-prediction reduces linear-head seams but still leaves nearly twice the seams of the convolutional head.
- Noise-shift sweep on 946 VBench prompts peaks at shift 15 (effective shift 3.97 under calibration scale 3.78) in both total and semantic score; the 14B model uses shift 11 (effective 3.86), and the 960x1664 stage doubles both shift and calibration.

## Details

### Why each design choice exists

The frame-wise tokenization is what removes the three structural limits at once: conditions align with individual frames, an autoregressive model can react at the next frame, and the spatial token count no longer grows with resolution because it is set by the adjustable patch size rather than a frozen VAE. The x-prediction choice follows the same logic as recent pixel-space image models — the DiT should capture global structure and hand only local, near-manifold detail to the lightweight head, whereas predicting velocity requires reproducing the full-rank per-pixel noise mixed into the input. The larger shift exists because the timestep calibration maps pixel signal-to-noise ratios onto the latent schedule, and redundant pixels call for a higher shift; the sweep shows both too little and too much shift hurt, mostly in spatial relationships and multiple objects.

### How the applications are set up

The text-to-video comparison finetunes Wan2.1-T2V-14B at 480x832 for 20K iterations on 64K videos generated by Wan2.1 itself, evaluates on VBench's 946 GPT-enhanced prompts with five videos each, and runs a human study on 100 random Movie Gen Video Bench prompts with 10 evaluators. The high-resolution stage first trains a patch-16 model at 480x832, then finetunes it at patch 32 on 40K synthetic videos, with the new up block initialized as nearest-neighbor upsampling and the patch embedding fused from average pooling so the DiT initially sees a downsampled version of its input. The editing comparison trains on 79K stock videos with paired depth and evaluates on 1,000 held-out clips, with the fast-clip quarter used for alignment medians. The interactive comparison distills the bidirectional model into a causal one with Self Forcing, where the causal detailer decodes one frame at a time from cached features of previous frames at constant per-frame cost.

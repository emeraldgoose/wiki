# Skaling: Chinchilla's Exponents Meet Kaplan's Coupling

Source: Mathurin Videau, Badr Youbi-Idrissi, David Lopez-Paz, Kartik Ahuja (FAIR at Meta), arXiv:2608.07222, Aug 7, 2026.

**Lead**: Neural scaling laws let researchers predict expensive pretraining runs from cheap ones, and every large lab depends on that. But the standard Chinchilla form models reducible loss as a **sum of independent terms** for model size and tokens — which mathematically forces the cross-derivative to be *exactly zero*, i.e. it assumes model size and data do not interact. The paper shows that assumption fails precisely where prediction matters most: the imbalanced corners of the training grid, where Chinchilla's residuals form a saddle and errors reach several percent. **Skaling** adds a single coupling exponent that restores the interaction Kaplan's form had and Chinchilla discarded. The gain is not from parameter count — a 9-parameter Farseer law performs *worse* — but from an inductive bias matching the observed surface. A second practical consequence: because the law is anchored by the *edges* of parameter space rather than its interior, a sparse L-shaped grid of cheap boundary runs recovers the full-grid law at a fraction of the compute.

## The structural flaw in the additive form

The failure is not a fitting artifact. It is baked into the functional form.

Chinchilla writes reducible loss as two independent power-law terms summed above an irreducible constant:

```
L_reduced(N, D) = (A/N^α) + (B/D^β)
```

That sum has a mathematically forced property: **the mixed partial derivative is identically zero**, whatever α and β you choose. It is not an empirical finding that N and D don't interact — it is an assumption the functional form cannot express.

The consequence is concrete. The error surface develops **oppositely-signed errors toward the corners** of the N–D grid, reaching several percent exactly where N and D are most imbalanced. This is the saddle-shaped residual you get when an interaction term is omitted.

The authors confirm the interaction is real by differentiating the measured loss surface directly. Using two independent mesh-free estimators — local Moving Least Squares and a global Gaussian process — the estimated mixed derivative is **non-zero across the entire grid**, decays as its own power law, and is **predominantly negative**: scaling N and D together lowers loss more than scaling either alone. That synergy is something no additive law can represent, by construction.

## The Skaling form

Skaling keeps Chinchilla's interpretable base terms and independent inner exponents, then — following Kaplan — raises their sum to a single free outer exponent `κ`:

```
L(N, D) = E + (A/N^α + B/D^β)^κ
```

One new parameter, and the two prior laws fall out as special cases:

| Setting | Result |
|---|---|
| κ = 1 | exactly the additive Chinchilla law, cross-derivative zero |
| κ ≠ 1 | Kaplan-style coupling restored, cross-derivative non-zero |

Crucially, this is **not** Kaplan. Kaplan couples by *tying* the inner exponents through the ratio N/D; Skaling couples through the outer exponent while leaving Chinchilla's inner exponents free. For κ < 1 the mixed derivative is negative, matching the measured sign.

Two properties are preserved:

1. **Monotonicity** — the function stays strictly decreasing in both N and D, so adding capacity or data never increases predicted loss.
2. **Chinchilla's compute-optimal allocation, unchanged** — the monotone outer map rescales the loss but leaves its minimizer untouched. The closed-form optimal token-to-parameter ratio survives intact.

Point 2 has an important caveat the paper is careful about: the *algebraic formula* is identical, but the **fitted parameters do not transfer** between the two forms, so the *numerical* optimum differs substantially — accumulating to a **100-fold discrepancy at frontier compute scales**.

## Interpolation fit is not the point

A useful negative result. Chinchilla attains strong interpolation accuracy on full grids, yet its extrapolation error at boundaries is several times worse than Skaling's. The relevant failure mode was never a poor interior fit — it is **systematic misprediction of how the surface bends away from the observed region.**

This also answers the "just add more parameters" objection: Farseer, with 9 fitted parameters, is **less accurate than the 6-parameter Skaling** in most regimes, and its largest errors concentrate on data extrapolation. The paper notes part of the gap may be that a richer form is harder to fit — they tried several optimizers including Farseer's own pipeline — but report the best Farseer results they could obtain.

## L-shaped profiling: the practical payoff

Standard profiling fills an N×D grid on logarithmic spacing. Because compute grows multiplicatively in both axes, **the overwhelming majority of total compute lands in the top-right corner** — a few enormous runs. Dense sampling is geometrically wasteful.

The asymptotic behaviour of the loss suggests a better allocation. As D grows, estimation error vanishes and loss approaches the size-dependent approximation error — so sweeping D at small N isolates the data parameters cleanly. Symmetrically, sweeping N at small D isolates the size parameters.

The **L-shape strategy** does exactly that: sweep D across the smallest models, and N across the shortest horizons, anchoring the independent decay rates at the grid **boundaries** instead of filling the interior.

Result: Skaling fitted on the L-shape grid stays close to or better than full-grid Chinchilla, at roughly an order of magnitude less fitting compute. Chinchilla, by contrast, **degrades substantially** under the same restriction — because its boundary errors are precisely what the coupling was fixing.

This is the part that changes practice: reliable scaling prediction stops requiring massive dense sweeps.

## Compute extrapolation along iso-ratio slices

Frontier labs project along one-dimensional compute power laws at a fixed token-to-parameter ratio (DeepSeek being the canonical example). The paper tests the operational case: group runs into iso-ratio slices, hold out the highest-compute point of each, refit every law **once** on the pooled low-compute remainder, and evaluate on the held-out expensive runs.

Skaling is the best *global* law in every regime and overall, and the most stable — its error never spikes in any regime. Chinchilla is strongly regime-dependent and weakest exactly in the **optimal band**, so its pooled average hides where it fails. Only a per-ratio power law edges past Skaling, and only near the optimum — but that baseline is fit separately per recipe and cannot inform joint N–D allocation at all.

## Fitting is fragile

A candid engineering note worth carrying into practice: fitting scaling laws is a surprisingly fragile non-convex problem, even with six parameters.

- **Scale disparity** — coefficient magnitudes versus fractional exponents
- **Flat landscape** — loss decreases logarithmically, so optimizers halt prematurely, worsened by finite-difference gradients
- **Compensatory parameters** — the irreducible loss E is hard to pin down because other coefficients can shift to offset it, creating wide valleys of near-identical local minima

Log-space fitting plus basin-hopping and autograd handles it. Notably, **gradient-free CMA-ES reaches equally good fits out of the box**, while L-BFGS needs careful initialization and restarts.

## Honest limitation

The coupling exponent and the irreducible floor **trade off against each other**. With κ < 1 the concave outer map makes the coupled term decay more slowly at large scale, absorbing curvature that an additive law can only express through a larger E. Because none of the runs reach saturation scale, the data fix total loss but not that split — and Skaling resolves the ambiguity by pushing E down, on Farseer data almost to zero. The authors explicitly decline to read this as a vanishing loss floor.

Also worth flagging: the **direction** of the allocation trend is dataset-specific, not a universal consequence of coupling. On Farseer the fitted exponents give a *decreasing* optimal ratio with compute; on SK-Grid the same closed form would *increase* it. The robust claim is that coupling changes large-scale allocation — not which way.

## Relevance to SW engineers

- If your scaling law is additive, you have a hardcoded assumption that model size and data do not interact. That assumption is wrong, and wrong worst where you extrapolate.
- Judge a scaling law on boundary extrapolation, not interpolation fit. Good interior fit is compatible with large systematic extrapolation error.
- Allocate profiling compute to the edges of your grid, not the interior — the corner runs dominate the budget and buy the least information.
- When a baseline beats your model only on a per-config fit, note what that baseline cannot do: it says nothing about joint allocation.

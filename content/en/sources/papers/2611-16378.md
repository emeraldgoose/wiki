# Evidence-Governed Adoption of Self-Generated Updates in Autonomous AI Agents

**Source**: [arXiv:2611.16378](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16378) · **Published**: 2026-11-12
**Authors**: Subodh Lonkar

## Overview

Autonomous agents increasingly rewrite their own prompts, memories, tool configurations, workflows, and control policies in response to interaction histories — changing behaviour without touching the underlying model weights. But generating a plausible modification is not the same as establishing that it should become persistent system state. Candidate selection is adaptive, evaluators may be imperfect or manipulable, and a string of local improvements can conceal degradation against an earlier operating baseline. Subodh Lonkar (independent researcher) formalises this "adoption gap" and develops Evidence-Governed Evolution (EGE), a statistical and operational framework for deciding which self-generated changes may become persistent.

EGE's discipline has five moving parts. Candidate generation is separated from confirmatory adoption; frozen candidates are evaluated on fresh, paired audit samples; acceptance requires a practically meaningful improvement on a prespecified primary objective (margin δ); declared capabilities are protected by reference-relative non-inferiority tests (margin ε); and bounded canary deployment follows only after mandatory governance constraints — two-phase commitment, operational monitoring, risk tiers, and rollback/recovery paths.

The statistical core is an evaluator-calibrated simultaneous lower-bound construction for a countable sequence of adaptively generated candidates: candidate-wise Hoeffding bounds combined with independent, candidate-specific calibration samples that compare the operational evaluator against a prespecified reference evaluation procedure. A second result formalises replacing the reference itself through explicit bridge tests and bounds cumulative degradation along a versioned reference lineage. A conservative audit-planning condition ties together effect-size margin, audit sample size, calibration sample size, and a lower bound on acceptance probability.

## Key points

- The framework's target is adoption, not generation: reflection, prompt optimisation, and skill libraries produce candidates, but only a confirmatory, statistically governed step should let a change persist — especially when proposals are adaptive and the candidate can modify the very mechanism that produces future evidence.
- Guarantees are explicitly conditional: bounded paired scores, fresh sampling, a valid reference-evaluation construct, and declared evaluation distributions; the paper states plainly that it establishes neither universal agent safety nor robustness to arbitrary distribution shift, and includes no empirical validation on a real autonomous-agent system.
- Monte Carlo sensitivity study (3,000 campaigns per condition, 10 candidates each, beneficial/harmful 50/50, primary threshold δ = 0.05, non-inferiority margin ε = 0.20, global error budget α = 0.05): with the operational evaluator optimistic by b = 0.25 and n = m = 5,000, the calibrated EGE gate accepted harmful candidates in 0 of 3,000 campaigns while the evaluator-naive comparator — same lower confidence bound, no calibration — accepted them in 2,997 of 3,000.
- The price of that protection is power: in the same condition EGE accepted 65.7% of beneficial candidates versus the naive gate's 100%; with no evaluator bias EGE's power was 52.6% at n = m = 5,000 (76.2% at b = 0.10), with zero harmful campaigns throughout the bias sweep.
- Inconclusive outcomes are interpreted conservatively — a candidate that fails to clear the acceptance predicate is not adopted, and the framework plans audits so that beneficial candidates have a derivable minimum acceptance probability.
- Reference lineage governance: replacing the reference evaluation procedure requires explicit bridge tests, and cumulative degradation along the versioned lineage is bounded, so baseline drift cannot silently erode the protected-capability guarantee.

## Details

The paper builds up the formulation from agent configurations and paired estimands (primary objective and protected capabilities), through evaluator calibration, evidence partitioning with audit integrity, and sequential statistical adoption with error allocation across candidates and tests, to deployment governance (two-phase commitment, monitoring, risk-tiered rollout, rollback). Appendices give the Hoeffding and calibration-bound derivations, sample-size bounds, an implementation checklist, and a worked decision example. A separate calibration-distribution mismatch stress test probes what happens when calibration assumptions are violated, and a required real-agent evaluation protocol is specified for future empirical work — tasks and metrics, candidate generation and baselines, ablations and stress conditions, statistical reporting, and a canary study. The Monte Carlo experiment is presented as a stylised statistical illustration of candidate-dependent evaluator optimism, not an agent benchmark.

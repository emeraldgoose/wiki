# Probing Transformer Representations Layer by Layer, Module by Module

**Source**: [arXiv:2611.11531](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.11531) · **Published**: 2026-11-08
**Authors**: Ambroise Odonnat, Theo Gnassounou, Laetitia Chapel, Romain Tavenard, Ievgen Redko

## Overview

A standard way to evaluate what a frozen foundation model has learned is linear probing: train a linear classifier on top of a representation. For years the representation of choice has been the final-layer output, but recent work shows intermediate layers can be better - and, confusingly, the evidence points in different directions across model families. The authors observe that all of this layer-wise work shares a blind spot: it treats the transformer block as an atomic unit, ignoring the eight distinct sequence-to-sequence representations produced inside every block - the two normalization outputs (LN1, LN2), attention (MHA), the first residual (RC1), the two feedforward linear projections (FC1, FC2), the activation output (Act), and the block output (RC2, the residual stream).

The paper runs the largest systematic study to date at this granularity: over 144,000 linear-probing evaluations, covering 15 vision foundation models (ViT, DINOv2, DINOv3, AIMv2; 22M-7B parameters) on 18 image classification datasets and 9 LLMs (Qwen3, Llama3, Gemma3; 1B-12B) on 24 classification tasks from MTEB(Multilingual). Probing location is indexed by a layer-module pair, with depth expressed as a percentage. Three oracle gains quantify the value of choosing well: best layer with the residual stream fixed (G_layer), best module at the final layer (G_module), and best layer-module pair (G_joint).

The central result is that layer and module must be chosen jointly. G_joint dominates G_layer by a wide margin, and module quality varies substantially across depth - so no characterization of useful representations in terms of depth alone is adequate. The practical payoff comes from two simple fixed strategies that require no search: probing the final-layer feedforward activation (Act@100) for vision and the second normalization layer at 60% depth (LN2@60) for language. Act@100 improves over the residual-stream baseline in 63.7% of the 270 model-dataset pairs and recovers 41.9% of the oracle-pair gain on average (versus 31.9% for the best layer-wise residual stream); LN2@60 improves in 87.5% of the 24 tasks, gains 2.44 percentage points on average, and recovers 86.0% of the oracle gain (versus 39.3% for the best layer-wise residual stream). For vision models, the gains grow with the transfer difficulty between pretraining and downstream data.

## Key points

- Eight modules per block are probed: LN1, MHA, RC1, LN2, FC1, Act, FC2, RC2, under the pre-norm convention; RC2 (block output, "residual stream") is the standard baseline, denoted RC2@100. Gated-FFN models (SwiGLU) are handled by redefining FC1 as the input to the nonlinearity and letting Act absorb the gating projection, keeping locations comparable across standard and gated blocks.
- Token pooling is standardized across models: global average pooling over content tokens only, with special tokens (CLS, register tokens, EOS, BOS, padding) masked before pooling - which matters for families like AIMv2 that have no CLS token.
- Oracle-gain definitions: G_layer = best depth with module fixed to RC2; G_module = best module at the final layer; G_joint = best (depth, module) pair. By construction G_joint is at least each of the others; empirically G_module also dominates G_layer, showing module choice matters more than is usually acknowledged.
- VFM findings: joint gains reach up to 10 percentage points across the 270 model-dataset pairs; the activation module is the optimal location for 64.8% of pairs (LN2 9.3%, baseline RC2 just 5.9%); the best Act location is usually in the second half of the network but not necessarily the final layer (clear on Quickdraw and DINOv3).
- Act@100 per-family averages: +2.3 pp (ViT), +1.3 pp (DINOv2), +0.4 pp (DINOv3), +0.7 pp (AIMv2); it improves over the baseline in 63.7% of pairs and recovers 41.9% of the G_joint gain versus 31.9% for the best layer-wise RC2; probing the best layer-wise Act recovers 78.1%.
- LLM findings: gains reach up to 4 pp; Qwen3 shows larger gains than Llama3 and Gemma3, with improvement growing with model size within families; G_layer dominates G_module for all but one model, and G_joint systematically exceeds both. LN2 appears in the optimal pair for all but one model, and the baseline RC2 is never optimal.
- LN2@60 design: optimal depth sits near 70% for Qwen3 and near 50% for Llama3 and Gemma3, but the interval [50%, 70%] is stable across models and sizes; its midpoint 60% is the average optimum, so LN2@60 is the fixed strategy. Intermediate LN2 layers beat the final layer for every model considered.
- LN2@60 results: average gain 2.44 pp, close to the oracle; improvement in 21 of 24 tasks (87.5%) and 77.8% of the 216 model-task evaluations; recovery of 86.0% of the oracle gain versus 39.3% for the best layer-wise RC2; the best layer-wise LN2 recovers 99.8%, nearly closing the gap. Probing the residual stream at 60% depth (RC2@60) yields smaller, less consistent gains - evidence that both choices matter.
- Embedding-model implication: with LN2@60 and no finetuning, Gemma3 4B matches GritLM-7B - the second-best embedding model on MTEB(Multilingual) at MMTEB publication - and Gemma3 12B surpasses GritLM-7B and matches EmbeddingGemma, the embedding model derived from Gemma3.
- Transfer difficulty effect (VFMs): defining a dataset's empirical transfer difficulty as the mean baseline probing error across models, the oracle gain is positively correlated with it (Spearman rho 0.87, p-value below 0.001). Gains are small on easy, near-pretraining datasets (Flowers102, Pet) and large on hard ones; on the Wilds and DomainNet distribution-shift suites Act@100 improves 89.3% of 28 evaluations (+1.8 pp average).
- Controlled distribution shift: training on Cifar10 and testing on corrupted Cifar10-C (5 corruption types, severities 1/3/5), the oracle gain grows with corruption severity while the optimal depth decreases - intermediate layers matter more under shift. Conversely, oracle gains decrease with pretraining scale (models ordered by tokens seen).
- Related-work positioning: prior layer-wise studies (intermediate layers beating final layers in LLMs and some VFMs, with disagreements across families) all probed block outputs only; aggregation methods (ELMo-style weighting, Head2Toe, attentive pooling over layers) are complementary - this analysis can tell them which representations are worth aggregating.

## Details

- Experimental protocol: linear probing via logistic regression on standardized features (standard scaler); performance is top-1 test accuracy for VFMs and average test accuracy across tasks for LLMs, following the MTEB protocol; relative depth uses zero-based indexing mapped to a percentage of depth.
- Scale: roughly 90,000 probing evaluations for VFMs (15 models x 18 datasets) and over 54,000 for LLMs (9 models x 24 tasks), totaling more than 144,000; oracle quantities are a posteriori upper bounds requiring exhaustive evaluation, which is why fixed heuristics matter.
- MTEB(Multilingual) task coverage: 24 classification tasks spanning 31 languages and 14 domains, evaluated with the official MMTEB implementation.
- Why the fixed strategies work: for VFMs the activation is the most frequently optimal module and the final layer is the most frequently optimal depth, so their combination (Act@100) is the natural no-search choice; for LLMs LN2 is nearly always the optimal module and 60% depth the stable midpoint of the optimal-depth interval.
- Honest caveats: the fixed strategies are not always optimal across sizes and families (optimal depth varies by dataset; DINOv3 gains from Act@100 are modest at +0.4 pp), and all "optimal" claims are established a posteriori on test performance rather than by a selection procedure usable before evaluation.
- Limitations stated by the authors: classification-only linear probing; extension to other downstream tasks and modalities (tabular, time series) is future work; the findings could guide designs that aggregate intermediate representations instead of treating them uniformly.
- Reproducibility: code is open-sourced at github.com/ambroiseodt/waldo with full implementation details (datasets, benchmarks, pretrained weights) in Appendix B; an AI-use statement discloses generative AI assistance for writing and prototyping, with authors verifying all final experiments.

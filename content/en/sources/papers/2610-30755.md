# Is Complex Agentic Memory Always Needed? Naive RAG Is Stronger Than We Thought

**Source**: [arXiv:2610.30755](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30755) · **Published**: 2026-10-22
**Authors**: Zhengye Bao, Shengtao Zhang, Haokai Zhang, Shiqi Lou, Muning Wen, Minnan Luo

## Overview

Large language models are increasingly used in long-term conversations, where they must draw on information accumulated across many sessions and tasks. Recent agentic memory systems attack this problem with mutation, summarization, clustering, graph construction, community discovery, and learned retrieval strategies. The paper asks whether all of that complexity is actually necessary, and proposes a deliberately simple alternative: Raw-RAG, a naive retrieval-augmented generation baseline that performs dense retrieval and cross-encoder reranking directly over raw conversation history to supply evidence to a reader LLM.

Raw-RAG indexes history without any LLM-based extraction, summarization, graph construction, or mutation. Each conversation is serialized with message timestamps and image captions, split into 256-token windows with 32-token overlap, and embedded in a flat index. At query time the question is embedded, cosine similarity retrieves the 128 closest windows, and a cross-encoder jointly scores each question-window pair to select the top 15, which are then passed to the reader. The reader resolves temporal order and state changes from the retrieved evidence.

Across LoCoMo, LongMemEval, MEME, and MemoryAgentBench, Raw-RAG ranks first among the compared memory systems in six of seven benchmark-model settings, beating the strongest compared agentic memory system by 0.13-5.53 points. The paper also runs diagnostic experiments that challenge the need for explicit memory mutation: auditing Mem0 shows that UPDATE and DELETE account for only 4.39% of the 33,902 write decisions on LoCoMo and 2.46% of the 428,858 decisions on LongMemEval, effective mutations are only 1.90% and 1.34% of decisions respectively, and replacing UPDATE and DELETE with append-only ADD and NONE raises LoCoMo accuracy from 67.36% to 69.42%. On MEME, readers can resolve difficult state transitions from sufficient raw evidence — DeepSeek-V4-Flash reaches 82.32% on cascade tasks and 91.54% on invalidation tasks.

## Key points

- Raw-RAG leads LoCoMo by 0.65-0.95 points, LongMemEval by 0.13 points, GPT-4o-mini MEME by 2.24 points, and MemoryAgentBench by 4.48-5.53 points, despite a simpler ingestion procedure than every compared agentic system.
- Explicit mutation provides no accuracy gain in the tested configuration: effective text-changing updates or deletions are only 1.90% of LoCoMo decisions and 1.34% of LongMemEval decisions, and an ADD/NONE-only policy improves LoCoMo accuracy by 2.06 points without reducing any category score.
- Three memory-management additions — atomic-fact extraction, summary augmentation, and graph-based retrieval expansion — all fail to improve Raw-RAG's 48.32% total on the same 14-source MemoryAgentBench, with atomic-fact extraction cutting test-time learning from 47.68% to 15.37% and costing 19.62 points overall.
- Reranking is essential and moderate retrieval depth is sufficient: removing the reranker costs 3.92 points, returning only one item costs 11.73 points, while chunk sizes from 128 to 1,024 tokens and final-k values of 5, 10, 30, and 60 stay within 1.3 points of the baseline.
- Raw-RAG combines competitive accuracy with much lower cost: the three memory-management additions need 21.6-109.3M LLM tokens and 2.9-8.8 hours for add plus search across 11 commonly measured non-conflict MemoryAgentBench sources, versus 12 minutes for Raw-RAG.
- Streaming a 1,710-message BEAM-1M conversation that approaches one million tokens, cumulative add time ranges from 0.33 hours for Raw-RAG to 26.95 hours for Memora, with MemOS the next-fastest completed run at 3.25 hours.

## Details

The paper's evidence analysis separates evidence availability from how readers use it. On MemoryAgentBench, semantic coverage spans 19.07-45.29% across systems, with Raw-RAG highest, while conditional utilization spans only 80.00-89.71%; MEME shows the same pattern, with coverage varying by 40.66 points versus 21.04 for utilization. The implication is that supplying sufficient evidence is the larger bottleneck, not answering correctly once evidence is present.

Raw-RAG is also most useful when it preserves both answer values and the relations and dependency paths needed to derive them: on MEME tracking and cascade after-state questions it achieves the highest conditional path coverage at 62.34%, followed by MemMachine at 56.70% and MemU at only 9.62%. A complete-store oracle improves all seven tested systems, but the recovered performance varies by stored representation — Raw-RAG and MemMachine, which retain raw conversational content, reach 57.54% and 57.46% against 61.60% for raw evidence, and removing the packing ceiling leaves all seven stores 7.58-29.38 points below raw evidence. The authors caution that this residual gap is not a pure measure of information lost during ingestion, since even Raw-RAG falls short and effects such as evidence assembly and lost-in-the-middle positioning may contribute.

The paper motivates the baseline partly by reviewing 14 prior works and finding coarse retrieval units, restricted retrieval depth, or absent reranking in several evaluated baselines, which leaves open whether the previously reported gaps reflect a need for agentic memory or weaknesses in the baseline retrieval stacks. The contributions are therefore a strong naive baseline that challenges the presumed necessity of complex agentic memory, a diagnostic framework separating evidence access from reader utilization, and the finding that information discarded or distorted during ingestion is hard to recover through retrieval alone. The authors note limitations: results are confined to offline conversation on public benchmarks and do not cover interactive, multimodal, or operational settings, and raw-history approaches raise storage and privacy concerns.

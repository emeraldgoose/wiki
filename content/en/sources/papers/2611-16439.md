# HALO: Human-Aligned Data Collection for WhoLe-body MObile Manipulation

**Source**: [arXiv:2611.16439](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16439) · **Published**: 2026-11-12
**Authors**: Shuai Yang, Fangyi Xu, Yishu Shen, Kecheng Zheng, Yinghao Xu

## Overview

Learning whole-body mobile manipulation needs demonstrations that coordinate bimanual actions with locomotion across extended workspaces, and existing collection interfaces force a trade-off between direct robot control, perception–action alignment, and natural operator mobility. VR teleoperation offers less direct control and no force feedback; leader–follower systems like Mobile ALOHA keep manipulation direct but separate the operator's hands and viewpoint from the end-effectors, creating a perception gap and constraining movement; retargeting approaches (UMI, HoMMI) keep human motion natural but distance the data from the robot's native action constraints. A HKUST/Robbyant team built HALO to hold all three properties at once.

HALO keeps the robot arms in the loop and has the operator guide the end-effectors through kinesthetic teaching, staying close enough to preserve direct visual and force feedback. For mobility, the deployed mobile base is removed during collection and the operator walks while pushing a low-resistance platform, so locomotion stays natural. The close human–robot coupling does introduce discrepancies — the platform is not the base, and demonstrator hands appear in the cameras — so a deployment-aligned demonstration-processing pipeline calibrates and temporally aligns platform motion with the deployed base response, and masks and inpaints the demonstrator's hands out of recorded observations before policy learning.

The result is a data-collection system measured against Mobile ALOHA and VR teleoperation by the same operator with no prior experience on any of the three interfaces, under identical task settings, time budgets, and reset protocols.

## Key points

- Collection efficiency: HALO cuts the average duration of successful demonstrations by up to 66% and speeds collection 2.9× on Push Chair and 2.8× on Disassemble Lego versus Mobile ALOHA, while raising the Lego demonstration success rate by 20 percentage points.
- Dexterity transfers to policies: on Zip-Tie Fastening — picking, handover, folding, insertion, fastening, placement of a short zip tie at sub-millimeter precision — the HALO-trained policy succeeds in 30.0% of 10 trials where both baselines' data yield 0.0% (the perception gap blocks Mobile ALOHA operators; VR cannot reach the control precision).
- Coordinated control: Disassemble Lego with randomized connection patterns succeeds in 30/40 trials (75.0%); a six-stage long-horizon task (retrieve box from shelf, load objects, carry with both arms, navigate to conveyor, place, return and repeat) succeeds in 11/15 trials (73.3%), with the policy reliably returning the base to its initial position despite repeated forward, backward, and rotational motions.
- Under the same data-collection time budget, policies trained from VR and Mobile ALOHA data fail both coordination tasks — slow, pause-ridden demonstrations and high collection-failure rates leave too little valid data.
- Inference strategy matters: asynchronous execution with an action chunk horizon of 8 reaches 85.0% on Push Chair, versus 0.0% for synchronous inference at the same horizon and 35.0% at horizon 25.
- Action representation: a joint-space action space with a pretrained VLA backbone reaches 75.0% on Disassemble Lego, versus 47.5% for end-effector actions, 12.5% for end-effector actions distilled from a VLM, and 7.5% for joint actions without the pretrained base.

## Details

The manipulation interface preserves the robot's native action constraints and physical feedback, while the mobility interface removes the base so the operator can reposition at natural walking speed (~0.7 m/s in Push Chair, where the operator grips the chair back with both grippers and slides it under a table). Ablations isolate the processing pipeline's contribution and the policy stack (execution horizon, action space, backbone). Hardware details, baseline implementations, and training configurations are given in appendices.

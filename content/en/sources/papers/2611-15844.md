# Open Vision Banana: A Fully Open Recipe for Generative Visual Perception

**Source**: [arXiv:2611.15844](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.15844) · **Published**: 11 November 2026
**Authors**: Arpit Jadon, Valentinos Pariza, Jona Ruthardt, Ryousuke Yamada, A. Sophia Koepke, Andrei Bursuc, Yuki M. Asano

## Overview

Recent work suggests that pretrained image generators encode real-world understanding well beyond image synthesis. The proprietary Vision Banana system exploits this by framing vision tasks as conditional image editing, but its data, weights, code, and implementation details are not public, so it is unclear which components actually account for its results. Open Vision Banana (OVB) answers that question with a fully open and reproducible recipe for converting public generators into unified multi-task perception systems.

The recipe covers six tasks, each handled through a deterministic RGB encoder and decoder: semantic, referring, reasoning, and instance segmentation, plus depth estimation and surface-normal estimation. All models are fine-tuned from FLUX.2 Klein 4B and 9B base generators using 2.1M to 3.8M training pairs drawn exclusively from public datasets, mixed with replay data for text-to-image, image-editing, and identity reconstruction so that generation abilities survive. The authors stress that design choices interact: color encoding, prompt design, parameter adaptation, and task balancing are each helpful alone, but individually useful choices can hurt when combined with others.

Results are competitive with the closed original. The zero-shot 9B model beats Vision Banana's reported Cityscapes score (73.9 vs 69.9 mIoU) and rivals state-of-the-art affine-invariant depth estimators, while non-zero-shot variants come within five points of Vision Banana on referring and reasoning segmentation but stay further behind on instance segmentation. The paper also contributes legend ensembling, a training-free test-time scaling method that segments an image under several random class-color legends and fuses the passes by majority vote.

## Key points

- Zero-shot Cityscapes: the 9B model reaches 73.9 mIoU with legend ensembling, ahead of the strongest zero-shot specialist SAM 3 (65.2), Vision Banana (69.9), and non-zero-shot SenseNova-Vision (71.2); the mean single pass is 65.6.
- The non-zero-shot 4B model reaches 76.4 mIoU, the best OVB result, even though Cityscapes makes up only 0.13% of its training samples; the zero-shot models already reach 71.3 (4B) and 73.9 (9B) without any Cityscapes data.
- Reasoning segmentation: with Gemma4 rewriting queries into referring expressions, the zero-shot 4B model reaches 67.9 gIoU on ReasonSeg; rewriting alone adds 26.7 gIoU, and the frozen Nano Banana Pro base reaches 70.2 on raw queries.
- Instance segmentation on SA-Co/Gold: the zero-shot 9B model reaches 37.4 cgF1 with 11-pass legend ensembling (mean single pass 36.0), ahead of OWLv2, DINO-X, and APE-D and almost three times the score of Gemini 2.5; SAM 3 (54.1) and Vision Banana (47.5) remain ahead.
- Geometry on NYUv2: zero-shot 9B reaches 97.4 depth delta-one with a mean normal error of 19.85 degrees; the non-zero-shot 9B reaches 99.0 and 13.51. OVB depth uses per-image disparity-affine alignment.
- Legend ensembling improves the zero-shot 9B model by up to 8.7 mIoU on Cityscapes and 15.0 mIoU on COCO-Object without retraining, with fixed pass counts of 20 on Cityscapes, 90 on COCO-Object, and 11 on SA-Co/Gold.
- Training is cheap by frontier standards: 100K steps at a global batch size of 192 (about 19 epochs), with the non-zero-shot 4B run taking about 73 hours on 16 NVIDIA GH200 GPUs and the 9B about 79 hours on 32.
- Generation abilities are largely retained: the 4B ImgEdit-Bench Basic average improves from 3.66 to 3.78 (zero-shot mix) and 3.82 (non-zero-shot mix), and the 9B keeps 3.89 against its base model's 3.86, though the 9B non-zero-shot model drops from 4.03 to 3.68 on the 50-prompt UGE suite.
- Perception fine-tuning even improves the frozen backbone features: CorLoc for unsupervised object discovery rises by 8.3 points (4B) and 3.8 points (9B), and OVB 9B surpasses DINOv3 7B at object discovery (56.3 vs 54.8) and semantic correspondence (61.9 vs 56.1).

## Details

### RGB task modeling

Every task is cast as conditional image generation. Segmentation annotations are rendered as color maps under a randomly drawn class-color legend, and the decoder maps generated pixels back to classes using that legend, with a random-draw protocol that re-samples assignments for every image so models cannot memorize fixed colors. Depth is encoded as affine-invariant inverse depth normalized per image, and surface normals are mapped linearly onto RGB channels in a camera-space convention; the prompts state the expected output representation explicitly. For instance segmentation, a per-pass decoder groups overlapping masks with IoU of at least 0.5 into instances.

### Training recipe

Full-scale models train for 100K steps on aspect-ratio buckets of about 512 by 512 pixels with AdamW (weight decay 0.0001), FSDP, and bf16 autocast, using a constant learning rate after linear warmup: 0.000005 for the 4B model and 0.000002 for the 9B. Zero-shot models use masked training that excludes invalid geometry regions, while non-geometry tasks use standard flow matching. Final models average a few late transformer checkpoints in weight space. The mixture retains 15% generation data and 25% editing and identity-reconstruction data, which the ablations found balances depth, segmentation, and editing performance.

### Legend ensembling

Because generator-based segmenters bind classes to colors unstably, the method runs several passes, each with a newly drawn legend, and fuses the per-pixel class maps by majority vote. Gains come mostly from correcting colors bound to wrong classes, and vote agreement doubles as a confidence estimate. The authors position this as a test-time scaling method for generator-based perception that requires no retraining.

### Retention and feature quality

Beyond benchmark scores, the paper checks that editing ability survives: adding an explicit task prefix to editing prompts barely changes base-model scores, supporting the attribution of differences to fine-tuning. Probing frozen features of single-stream blocks shows that learning dense perception maps sharpens localization and correspondence, with geometric correspondence improving by up to 13.1 points at R5.

# Experience-Handoff Reinforcement Learning for Recursive Self-Improvement

**Source**: [arXiv:2611.16409](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16409) · **Published**: 2026-11-12
**Authors**: Doyoung Kim, Byeongguk Jeon, Kailin Zhang, Sherry Yang

## Overview

It is common to train large language models for agentic tasks with reinforcement learning from scalar rewards. The paper opens by pointing out what this convention discards: a scalar reward throws away the fine-grained information about the environment that the agent experienced during the trial, such as error logs. Those traces are often exactly the material an agent would need to do better on the next attempt, yet the standard training loop reduces an entire rollout to one number.

A separate line of work tries to keep that material through context engineering. Recent approaches wrap the agent in a harness that refines its own inputs through reflection or compaction and hands past experience to future rollouts, with the playbook as the characteristic handed-off artifact. The authors identify the gap in this design: the construction of the handed-off artifacts is itself not learned. The harness structure is fixed, so whatever writes the playbook never receives a training signal for writing it well.

The proposed method aims for the best of both worlds: gradient updates from a scalar reward, while also utilizing environment information to hand off experience. As a concrete testbed the work fixes the Agentic Context Engineering (ACE) harness and trains one model to act, reflect, and curate task-local playbooks. The resulting method, ACE-RL, evaluates candidate experience-handoff artifacts by their downstream effect on future actor rollouts and co-optimizes the role-specific behaviors under a fixed scaffold. Across agentic benchmarks, ACE-RL achieves higher held-out performance than baseline methods, and the authors draw the lesson that agent training should optimize not only the actions in the current rollout but also the model-written experience that future rollouts inherit.

## Key points

- RL from scalar rewards for agentic tasks discards fine-grained environment information the agent experienced during the trial, with error logs given as the example.
- Recent harness-based works refine the agent's own inputs through reflection or compaction and hand past experience to future rollouts.
- In those harnesses, the construction of the handed-off artifacts, such as a playbook, is itself not learned.
- The stated goal is gradient updates from a scalar reward combined with the use of environment information to hand off experience.
- The testbed fixes the Agentic Context Engineering (ACE) harness rather than modifying the scaffold.
- One model is trained to perform three roles at once: act, reflect, and curate task-local playbooks.
- ACE-RL evaluates candidate experience-handoff artifacts by their downstream effect on future actor rollouts, not by their surface form.
- The role-specific behaviors — action generator, insight reflector, and playbook curator — are co-optimized under a fixed scaffold.
- Across agentic benchmarks, ACE-RL achieves higher held-out performance than baseline methods.
- The paper concludes that agent training should optimize not only the actions in the current rollout but also the model-written experience that future rollouts inherit.

## Details

### Learning what harnesses leave fixed

The two dominant training setups each lose something. Scalar-reward RL keeps a learning signal but reduces a whole trial to a single number, discarding the environmental detail the agent actually observed. Harness-based experience handoff keeps the detail but fixes the mechanism that produces it: the playbook handed to future rollouts is constructed by rules or prompting that nobody trains. The paper's framing puts these together, treating the handed-off artifact as something the model itself should learn to write, with its quality judged by what it does for the next rollout rather than by how it reads.

### One model, three roles

In the ACE testbed the same trained model fills the action generator, insight reflector, and playbook curator roles while the scaffold itself stays fixed. Candidate experience-handoff artifacts are evaluated by their downstream effect on future actor rollouts, which ties the curation behavior to measured outcomes instead of to the artifact's appearance. Because the three role-specific behaviors are co-optimized, the action policy, the reflection policy, and the curation policy all receive pressure toward handoffs that help later rollouts. The reported result is higher held-out performance than baseline methods across agentic benchmarks, supporting recursive self-improvement in which the experience a model writes becomes part of its own training signal.

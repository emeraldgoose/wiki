# Retriever-Free Retrieval: Navigating Documents with Decision Models

**Source**: [arXiv:2610.35868](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.35868) · **Published**: 2026-10-26
**Authors**: Chao Wan, Paul Jünger, Beixi Du, Bodong Liu, Navaneetha Krishnan, Kilian Q. Weinberger
**License**: CC BY 4.0

## Overview

Large language models have become far more capable, but the retrievers that decide what evidence they see have not kept pace. Most retrieval systems still rely on lexical heuristics such as BM25 or similarity in a fixed embedding space. This produces a capability asymmetry: whatever the retriever fails to find, even the strongest generator cannot recover. The paper introduces **DecisionRAG**, a retriever-free framework that uses **decision models**—models that return probabilities over predefined answer options without generating tokens—to navigate the inherent structure of a corpus from broad topics down to individual passages.

DecisionRAG treats the corpus as a tree whose internal nodes summarize groups of documents or passages. At each level, candidates are evaluated jointly in a shared context, so relevance judgments remain comparable across siblings. Starting from either the corpus's own structure or an embedding-based clustering, the system narrows the search space at each stage, pruning irrelevant branches. No task-specific training and no conventional first-stage retriever are required: the default configuration uses a pretrained decision model (Jev) and still outperforms dense retrieval with an 8B reranker.

Across four benchmarks—MultiDoc2Dial, ConditionalQA, MTRAG, and Natural Questions—the method achieves substantial retrieval gains while staying fast: it completes retrieval in 0.68–1.76 seconds per query, compared with 3.3–14.3 seconds for dense retrieval with Qwen3-Reranker-8B. On a corpus of 2.68 million Wikipedia paragraphs, it reads only 16% of the page titles, and latency grows only logarithmically with corpus size, because each level adds one sequential decision regardless of corpus scale.

## Key points

- Introduces **DecisionRAG**, which brings language-model capability into retrieval by navigating corpus hierarchies with non-generative decisions instead of retrieving from a flat index.
- Uses two decision-model modes: **parallel binary judgments** (independent “Yes” probabilities, normalized over Yes/No) and **multiple-choice judgments** (a softmax distribution over competing candidates).
- Judges candidates in a **shared context**, making their relevance scores comparable; shared context benefits level off at roughly 50 candidates per request.
- Improves R@1 by **13.8 points** on MultiDoc2Dial and Hit@1 by **17.4 points** on Natural Questions over dense retrieval with an 8B reranker, without task-specific training or a first-stage retriever.
- Achieves **comparable or higher rank-one accuracy** than reranking the dense top-20 with GPT-5, while improving ConditionalQA R@10 and MTRAG R@5.
- Combines cleanly with dense retrieval: adding dense-retrieved passages to the final stage raises R@10 on MultiDoc2Dial from 83.6 to 84.7, ConditionalQA from 71.9 to 76.3, and Natural Questions from 86.7 to 88.9.
- Handles 2.68M Wikipedia paragraphs by clustering 108,626 pages into 1,000 groups; end-to-end query latency is still 1.73 seconds.
- Supports task-trained open-weight decision models (1.7B–8B based on Qwen3), with an 8B judge reaching 60.7 R@1 on MultiDoc2Dial (+5.7 over the published task-trained retriever).

## Details

### Method

**Problem formulation.** The corpus is modeled as a tree whose leaves are passages and whose internal nodes group them at increasing granularity: help centers, topic clusters, documents, section headings, and passages. Each node has a short textual description—the passage text, a document title, or a one-sentence cluster summary. Depths of the tree are called **levels**, and each round of requests to the decision model is a **stage**. Given a query that may include prior dialogue turns, the goal is to retrieve the passages containing the needed evidence.

**Relevance judgments with decision models.** Decision models such as Jev (TypeSafe AI) and OpenAI's decision model evaluate questions against a context and return probabilities over predefined options, with no autoregressive token generation. Parallel binary judgments score many candidates at once, producing independent probabilities `p_i^bin = P(Yes | s, Q_i)` that are normalized only over Yes/No, so scores need not sum to one and the list can be arbitrarily large across requests. Multiple-choice judgments instead return a distribution `p_i^mul = P(a_i | s, Q, A)` over candidates, deciding among competing alternatives. Both modes use a shared context `s`, which is what makes relevance estimates comparable in view of one another.

**Hierarchical retrieval.** At each level the most promising nodes are retained and expanded to the next level: document screening keeps the top passages, then passage scoring and optional passage reranking refine the answer. For conversational datasets the system keeps the last six dialogue turns and maintains a **notepad** that carries forward evidence from previous turns.

**Hierarchy construction and scaling.** Hierarchies come either from existing document structure and metadata, or—when none exists, as in the Wikipedia corpus for Natural Questions—from clustering 108,626 pages into 1,000 groups by k-means over embeddings of page titles and first sentences. Each cluster gets a one-sentence summary generated by Qwen3-8B. Levels that are too large for one request are split into parallel requests, with scores pooled; top candidates may be rescored in a single request when finer comparison matters. The tree need not be uniform: on MTRAG a heading level is inserted only below pages with more than 60 passages. A node whose level exceeds context limits is split across requests. DecisionRAG can also accept a dense retriever's top passages as additional final candidates.

Latency depth, not corpus size, drives latency: with a roughly balanced hierarchy where each node groups at most `b` children, the number of sequential decisions per query is `ceil(log_b N)` (Equation 4), with `t_req ≈ 0.2 s` per request; at `b = 100`, even 10^9 passages need only five decisions.

### Experimental setup

Four benchmarks, ranging from 413 help-center pages to 108,626 Wikipedia pages. MultiDoc2Dial: 4 centers / 4,110 passages / 4,201 turns, one gold passage. ConditionalQA: 652 pages / 58,483 paragraphs / 285 questions, 1–22 gold paragraphs. MTRAG IBM Cloud: 172 products / 8,578 pages / 72,442 passages / 205 turns, 1–7 gold passages. Natural Questions (DAPR): 1,000 clusters / 108,626 pages / 2.68M paragraphs / 500 test questions, 1–3 gold paragraphs.

Dataset-specific hierarchies and retain budgets are selected on design sets and applied without generator input. MultiDoc2Dial: help center → page → passage, retaining the top ten pages plus up to two notepad pages. ConditionalQA: page → paragraph, top three pages. MTRAG: page → heading → passage, all page titles scored in parallel, top 50 rescored, top ten retained, with headings selected on long pages. Natural Questions: cluster → page → paragraph, 150 of 1,000 clusters, paragraphs from the top 30 pages. All Jev configurations end with multiple-choice ranking of the top 20 passages (top five on Natural Questions); MTRAG promotes only the selected passage to rank one.

Baselines include BM25, SPLADE++, Qwen3-Embedding-8B (dense), bge-reranker-v2-m3 and Qwen3-Reranker-8B reranking the dense top-100, and GPT-5 reranking the dense top-20 with a RankGPT-style prompt. Published reference results (DPR, SFRe2g, ELSER) are included rather than rerun.

### Results

Retrieval without task-specific training (Table 2): on MultiDoc2Dial, DecisionRAG (Jev) reaches 47.4 R@1 against 33.6 for dense+Qwen3-Reranker, a 13.8 point gain; on Natural Questions, Hit@1 improves by 17.4 points (65.4 vs 48.0). Gains hold against GPT-5 reranking on rank-one for two of four benchmarks (65.4 vs 60.8 on Natural Questions; 47.4 vs 47.5 on MultiDoc2Dial) and higher R@10 / R@5 elsewhere. Adding dense candidates to DecisionRAG improves R@10 on all three labelled benchmarks.

Downstream answer quality with Qwen3.5-27B (Table 3) shows the largest improvement on MTRAG, where judged answer correctness rises from 39.4% (Qwen3-Reranker) to 44.1%, and to 47.3% with dense candidates (vs 34.0% for GPT-5 reranking); ConditionalQA exact match reaches 47.6% and Natural Questions 54.8%, approaching the gold-passage ceiling.

Task-trained decision models (Table 4, Appendix G) adapt Qwen3-8B on MultiDoc2Dial data and reach 60.7 R@1 (85.8 R@10), exceeding the published task-trained retriever by 5.7 points, showing the framework benefits from supervision as well.

Latency and cost (Table 5): DecisionRAG completes retrieval in 0.68–1.76 s/query versus 3.3–14.3 s for dense+Qwen3-Reranker and 11.8–15.0 s for GPT-5 reranking; API cost 0.22¢–2.33¢ per query. Natural Questions latency (1.73 s) matches MTRAG despite 15× more pages, illustrating hierarchy-driven sublinear latency.

### Limitations

The method depends on a corpus hierarchy whose structure and node descriptions let the decision model identify relevant branches: poorly organized documents or uninformative descriptions degrade retrieval. Irreversible pruning means relevant passages can be missed when ancestors are discarded; larger exploration budgets and complementary dense retrieval mitigate this. Extreme selectivity also depends on candidate diversity at each level.

### Relevance to software engineers

- **Pattern:** Decision models (probability-only, no token generation) can replace or augment embedding-based retrieval with level-by-level navigation, trading corpus size for hierarchy depth rather than index size.
- **Complexity:** A `log_b N` stage count makes retrieval latency grow slowly with corpus size; even billions of passages imply only a few more sequential decisions, provided a roughly balanced hierarchy.
- **Integration:** DecisionRAG complements, not replaces, dense retrieval—adding dense candidates to its final stage raises R@10 everywhere trials showed it.
- **Engineering choice:** Candidates can be screened in parallel; shared context beats extra candidates once ~50 candidates are present, so large levels should be split rather than overcrowded.
- **Operations:** Downstream RAG quality improves measurably, but generators still benefit from gold evidence; decision-based retrieval gets close to but does not fully close the gap.

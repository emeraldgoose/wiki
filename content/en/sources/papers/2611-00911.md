# S1CogBench: What System One Decision Models Can and Cannot Do on Cognitive Experiments

**Source**: [arXiv:2611.00911](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.00911) · **Published**: 2026-11-14

## Overview

Decision-making in humans and AI involves multiple cognitive abilities. System One decision models such as Jev, Laya, and Intern-Decision return a single typed decision over legal options in one call, enabling direct execution for routing, guardrails, and automated gates. Their cognitive strengths and limitations remain poorly understood.

The authors introduce S1CogBench, a benchmark of 24 established cognitive tasks and 27 evaluation modes covering human-response prediction, autonomous execution, and controlled task manipulations. Seven open models and API model Jev are evaluated. The suite connects cognitive tasks to model evaluation via adapters that supply visible states, histories, and legal responses, with model-specific coverage recorded in Appendix A.

Findings show distinct behavioral profiles across models. Strong performance on some learning tasks coexists with failures to gather information or adapt to feedback, and high accuracy does not guarantee human-like responses to changing task demands. Shared readout controls show model rankings depend partly on how choices are extracted. Latency comparisons with generative language models show long responses are much slower than direct decisions, whereas matched one-token generation adds little overhead.

S1CogBench is presented as the first benchmark of System One decision models on established cognitive experiments, providing a common empirical basis for identifying capabilities and limitations before integration into automated decision systems.

## Key points

- System One decision models output a typed decision directly, without generated text for inspection.
- S1CogBench covers 24 cognitive tasks and 27 evaluation modes across prediction, autonomous execution, and manipulations.
- Seven open models plus Jev are evaluated with adapters supplying states, histories, and legal options.
- Models show task-specific strengths and failures; high aggregate accuracy can hide poor adaptation or information gathering.
- Choice extraction method influences rankings; readout controls are shared across models.
- Direct decisions are much faster than long free-text responses; one-token generation overhead is small.
- Benchmark provides human NLL, accuracy, reward, pseudo-regret, MAE, Brier, latency P50/P95, and invalid-response rate where applicable.

## Details

- Tasks include Wisconsin Card Sorting Test, Mouselab, stationary bandits, and others, adapted for typed outputs with hybrid sampling for posterior summaries.
- Scoring targets include human-response NLL over legal responses and over full vocabulary, with weights following source scorers, and autonomous total reward G = sum(r_t - c_t).
- Nine shared measures are reported: accuracy, reward, pseudo-regret, MAE, human NLL, Brier, latency P50 and P95, and invalid-response rate.
- Original cognitive effects are reported alongside model scores; uncertainty is estimated over participants or independent environments.
- Results show that models can return well-formed distributions yet ignore feedback or stop searching too early, and that closed-loop environments rarely succeed without isolating which demand fails.

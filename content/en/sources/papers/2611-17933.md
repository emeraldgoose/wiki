# OutfitterBench: Evaluating LLM Agents on Long-Horizon Resource Allocation and Commitment Coordination

**Source**: [huggingface](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17933)
**Date**: 2026-11-19

## Overview

OutfitterBench is a benchmark for evaluating LLM agents on long-horizon resource allocation and commitment coordination. The setting is an outdoor-equipment rental business that the agent manages for 200 decision days followed by 14 automatic settlement days. The agent purchases from a 46-product catalog and handles roughly 1,100 reservation requests, all under a fixed equipment-capital ceiling. Reservations and uncertain walk-in demand compete for the same reusable equipment fleet, whose availability changes over time as items are rented, returned, cleaned, and serviced.

The benchmark targets a gap in existing LLM-agent evaluations: many continuing-operations benchmarks cover purchasing, pricing, and customer management, but pay less attention to how agents keep their own commitments feasible while reassessing resource allocations as opportunities change. In this environment, an agent's own choices create future constraints — committing finite capacity establishes future obligations and forecloses later opportunities — so performance depends on both maintaining feasible evolving plans and comparing the value of an allocation against the alternatives it displaces.

The authors evaluate seven frontier LLMs and find that several underperform a simple rule-based baseline, with only the leading agent consistently surpassing it. Trajectory analysis shows that some agents apply known constraints inconsistently and make new decisions that conflict with existing commitments; even agents with reliable fulfillment can earn low returns from comparable commitments or delay resource reallocation. The leading agent connects preparation with economic choices better, though it still misses opportunities for more profitable asset use.

## Key points

- Agents manage a simulated outdoor-equipment rental shop for 200 decision days plus 14 settlement days, maximizing terminal business value: cash plus eligible equipment resale value, less outstanding liabilities.
- A fixed equipment-capital ceiling constrains the fleet; once it binds, new investment may require replacing existing assets, and procurement, servicing, operating costs, and customer compensation all draw on the same budget.
- Two demand channels — reservations and uncertain walk-ins — draw on shared capacity; potential outings are modeled with Poisson arrivals whose rates vary with season, weekends, public holidays, and school holidays, based on historical Beijing weather.
- The product catalog was built from Taobao listings; customer behavior and equipment operations follow explicit rules and probabilistic models.
- Each day starts a fresh model session; business records persist, and agents may keep retrievable notes and a handoff for the next day.
- Seven frontier LLMs were evaluated; several trail a simple rule-based baseline, and only the leading agent consistently beats it.
- Failure modes include inconsistent application of known constraints, new decisions conflicting with existing commitments, low returns on comparable commitments despite reliable fulfillment, and delayed resource reallocation.
- Benchmark code is publicly available at https://github.com/adminblackjack/OutfitterBench.

## Details

**Method.** OutfitterBench is designed around three interacting features. First, outdoor trips require complementary equipment to be ready simultaneously, and rental, return, and servicing change availability over time, so agents must keep accepted bookings compatible with an evolving supply and with subsequent uses. Second, customers compete for shared rental capacity: reservations and walk-ins draw from a common pool of potential outings, and prices plus capacity protection determine what commitments earn and which opportunities remain available. Third, agents can revise commitments and change fleet composition, but customer response delays, cancellation compensation, and resale losses make adjustments costly. Each morning the environment processes deliveries, service work, returns, and pickups; the agent receives an inbox and can query records, set prices, buy or sell equipment, arrange servicing, and handle bookings. At closing, the environment resolves pickup issues, serves walk-ins, charges daily costs, and advances the day. Observations include finances, transactions, asset and contract states, service schedules, customer feedback, and public calendar and weather forecasts, with customer preferences only partly revealed through interaction.

**Results.** Across two world seeds (results averaged equally), business value curves are reported relative to the initial shop, net of equipment and outstanding obligations, with contract-adjusted economic value; labels report mean official net value creation (ΔV) at day 214. A fixed-policy Calendar reference sits between the highest- and lower-performing agents. The central findings are that (1) several of the seven frontier agents underperform the simple rule-based baseline and only the leading agent consistently surpasses it; (2) trajectory analysis reveals inconsistent application of known constraints and decisions that conflict with prior commitments; (3) reliable fulfillment does not guarantee good economics — some agents secure low returns from comparable commitments or delay reallocation; and (4) even the leading agent, while better at linking preparation with economic choices, misses opportunities for more profitable asset use. The authors conclude that sustaining coherent resource plans and reassessing their value over time remain open limitations for LLM agents. Evaluation details, observation windows, readiness projection, and sensitivity analyses (e.g., to turnaround in the nominal calendar, and unrealized gains from capacity relaxation) are given in the appendices.

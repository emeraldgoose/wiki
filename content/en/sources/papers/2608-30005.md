# Small Language Models as Judges for Rubric-Based Reinforcement Learning

Source: Fengyu Xie and 3 other authors, arXiv:2608.30005, Aug 30, 2026. EMNLP 2026 Findings.

**Lead**: Rubric-based reinforcement learning extends RL beyond tasks with exact answers or rule-based verifiers by scoring responses against **instance-specific criteria**. That makes reward computation expensive — training requires repeated rubric judging, typically via proprietary APIs or local generative judges of **7B parameters or more**. This paper asks whether smaller models can serve as efficient and reliable rubric judges. Answer: yes, but not in the obvious way. The paper constructs **PointRubric** and **RaR-Science-Static**, two pointwise rubric datasets with instance-specific criteria and itemwise satisfaction labels, and compares three ways of extracting criterion-level judgments. The **Qwen3-1.7B Probe judge** achieves the strongest criterion-level agreement, outperforming Generative and Logprob judges. Used as a GRPO reward model it trains a policy from **0.232 to 0.643** on RaR-Science rubric score, versus **0.594** for an 8B Generative judge baseline — at **10.7x less reward-judge time**.

## Why rubrics make RL expensive

RLVR works when the verifier is free: a compiler, a test suite, an exact-match string. Rubric-based RL breaks that assumption to reach tasks where no exact answer exists — writing quality, research quality, design judgement. The response is scored against **instance-specific criteria** rather than a single verifiable function.

The cost lands where you least expect it. Training needs *repeated* judging: every rollout in a GRPO loop gets scored. So the judge is on the inner loop, and its cost scales with rollouts × epochs. A 7B generative judge is not a rounding error there — it can dominate the training budget.

## Three ways to ask a small model

The paper's contribution is to make the question measurable, then compare three mechanisms for extracting criterion-level judgments from a small model:

1. **Generative verdicts** — ask the model in words ("does criterion 3 hold? yes/no") and parse the output
2. **Yes/No Logprob margins** — score the yes/no tokens and use the probability margin
3. **Probe judges** — train a small head to read the judgement off the model's internal representation

All three are used with the same 1.7B backbone, so the comparison isolates the extraction mechanism rather than model scale.

## What the results show

**Probe judges win on criterion-level agreement.** Across both datasets, the Qwen3-1.7B Probe judge achieves the strongest agreement with itemwise satisfaction labels — beating Generative and Logprob extraction at the same size. The intuition: a verdict you have to verbalise and parse is bottlenecked by generation quality, while a probe reads the judgement that the model already represents.

**The end-to-end result is what matters.** As a GRPO reward model, the 1.7B Probe judge trains a policy:

| Configuration | RaR-Science rubric score | Judge time |
|---|---|---|
| Baseline policy | 0.232 | — |
| **Qwen3-1.7B Probe judge** | **0.643** | 1x |
| 8B Generative judge | 0.594 | **10.7x** |

The small Probe judge beats the large Generative judge on the final policy *and* costs an order of magnitude less. That is the paper's practical claim: the mechanism matters more than the scale.

**Transfer holds.** Task and domain transfer experiments suggest Probe judges **preserve criterion-level reward structure across settings** — the reward signal does not collapse when you move to a new task.

## Caveats

The comparison is at a specific scale. A 1.7B Probe judge beating an 8B *Generative* judge does not establish that small judges beat large probes — the paper does not run that. The 10.7x figure is a ratio under this setup, not a universal constant. And agreement with itemwise labels is measured against a particular label set; how well it generalises to expert-authored rubrics in a new domain is a separate question.

## Relevance to SW engineers

- If your RL loop calls a large model on every rollout, judge cost is on the critical path. The mechanism you use to extract a judgement may matter more than the size of the model producing it.
- Ask whether the output you need is a *string* or a *number*. A judgement you have to verbalise inherits generation noise; a probe does not.
- A cheap judge is only useful if its reward signal survives transfer. The transfer experiments are the part to read if you plan to use it across tasks.
- The 0.232 → 0.643 result is measured against a rubric score, not a downstream task metric — check which one you actually care about.

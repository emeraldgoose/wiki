# DySENSE: Dynamic Spatiotemporal ENergy Synthesis and Emulation with Satellite Imagery

**Source**: [arXiv:2611.17878](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17878) · **Published**: 2026-11-13
**Authors**: Kailai Sun, Zijie Gao, Mingyi He, Can Rong, Alok Prakash, Baoshen Guo, Shenhao Wang, Jinhua Zhao

## Overview

Cities consume over two-thirds of the world's energy and produce more than 70% of global carbon dioxide emissions, and buildings alone account for roughly 32% of global energy use and 34% of CO2 emissions. Urban Building Energy Modelling (UBEM) is therefore central to the 2050 net-zero goal and the UN Sustainable Development Goals 7 and 11, yet existing work is largely predictive: it estimates energy for cities that already exist rather than generating visions of future ones. Fine-grained, spatially aligned monthly energy data are also scarce because of privacy, cost, and sensitivity, and although generative models can synthesize the visual modality of urban imagery, they do not produce the monthly energy dynamics that must accompany it.

DySENSE, from a SMART/MIT/NUS/University of Florida team, tackles all three gaps with a joint multimodal generative framework that synthesizes static urban satellite imagery together with spatially aligned, dynamic monthly building energy consumption maps. The key idea is to decouple long-term static urban morphology from short-term dynamic energy consumption. Conditioned on a static prompt describing location and land-use composition, the model generates realistic, diverse, new urban imagery with aligned high-resolution energy maps; conditioned on a dynamic prompt with the calendar month and monthly climate variables, it regenerates monthly energy maps for a full year while the urban form stays fixed.

To train and evaluate the framework, the authors assembled MUSE2, a monthly multi-city satellite-energy dataset covering New York City, Washington, D.C., Norfolk, and Busan, with monthly energy disclosure records spatiotemporally aligned with satellite imagery, land-use text, and climate data — 23,880 monthly paired samples in total. Across four cities, DySENSE produces annotated high-fidelity synthetic energy data from less than 25% of the labelled data, boosts downstream prediction performance by about 8% mIoU, and cuts energy prediction error on ASHRAE Guideline 14 metrics (9%-18% CVRMSE) against existing energy prediction methods.

## Key points

- Joint generation of both modalities: one framework learns the joint distribution of satellite imagery and building energy maps, conditioning on a static textual land-use prompt and a dynamic monthly/climate prompt, so the same urban morphology can be paired with 12 months of energy behavior.
- MUSE2 dataset: 2 km × 2 km tiles with 512×512 Mapbox satellite images, 100 m × 100 m energy cells converted to kBtu and log1p-transformed; after expert-in-the-loop review by three urban specialists, 2,565 curated tiles (579 NYC, 490 Norfolk, 500 Washington, D.C., 996 Busan) balance into 23,880 monthly paired samples (6,000 NYC, 6,000 Washington, 6,000 Busan, 5,880 Norfolk), released on Hugging Face.
- Architecture: a pretrained RGB VAE plus a newly trained domain-specific Energy VAE (both mapping to 64×64×4 latents), two frozen text encoders (CLIP for static, RemoteCLIP for dynamic prompts, 77 tokens each), and a U-ViT joint denoising network with one shared timestep and one shared Gaussian noise for image and energy latents.
- Static generation quality: FID of 36.7362, LPIPS of 0.5604, and PSNR of 11.5177 for the synthesized urban imagery.
- Downstream gains with synthetic augmentation (Dynamic Mix): mIoU improves by about 8.68% and CVRMSE drops 9%-18% across three SOTA predictors (Yap et al. 2025, Mayer et al. 2023, Sun et al. 2026d); for Yap et al. (2025) on 25% training data, accuracy rises from 0.5343 to 0.6217 and mIoU from 0.3687 to 0.4555, while NMBE falls from 6.02% to 2.82% and CVRMSE from 33.58% to 15.31%.
- Static Mix at scarce-data regimes: +7.0 mIoU over the real-only baseline at the 6.25% training ratio and +6.4 mIoU at 12.5%, with CVRMSE reduced from 82.3% to 58.4% at 6.25%; on average the Static Mix strategy adds about 6.8% mIoU, lowers |NMBE| by about 3.7%, and cuts CVRMSE by 16.7%.
- Distributional fidelity of generated monthly energy: KL divergence 0.0024, JS divergence 0.0006, and total variation 0.0181 against ground truth (still KL 0.0085, JS 0.0021, TV 0.0496 after removing the background class); monthly consumption curves reach an average Pearson correlation of 0.96 (0.93 Busan, 0.98 New York City).
- Compute and ablation: trained on 10 NVIDIA L40S GPUs (48 GB) with more than 800 GPU hours in total, about 220 for the generative model; an ablation replacing RemoteCLIP with CLIP shows RemoteCLIP consistently performs better on both generative and predictive tasks.

## Details

Method-wise, energy maps are spatially sparse, long-tailed, and statistically unlike RGB imagery, so DySENSE trains a dedicated Energy VAE with a reconstruction loss on the energy modality instead of reusing the image autoencoder. The two conditions are natural-language prompts: the static one encodes location and land-use ratios (for example, "Satellite imagery of Washington, D.C. Land use parcels include: 19 percent of residential, 32 percent of commercial, 6 percent of service, 13 percent of park, 7 percent of nature reserve"), and the dynamic one encodes the month plus monthly mean daily maximum/minimum temperature, precipitation, water vapor pressure, and surface solar radiation. Because the image and energy latents are denoised jointly, the conditional distribution of energy given imagery can be inferred without retraining — fixing the imagery latent and swapping the dynamic prompt yields what-if monthly energy maps for peak-load analysis, building performance simulation, and demand-response planning.

Experiments ran on Ubuntu 24.04 with an AMD EPYC 9754 128-core processor, 1.5TB of RAM, CUDA 13.1, Python 3.11, and PyTorch 2.11; sampling the generative model took about 60 GPU hours. Energy labels use 10 classes (class 0 for background, 9 positive levels in equal-frequency bins), and the downstream test set is fixed at 20% and split by tile to prevent data leakage. Monthly energy data come from public sources: NYC Local Law 84, Norfolk open data, Korean administrative records from the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure and Transport and Korea Real Estate Board, and Washington, D.C. benchmarking records, with climate inputs from Daymet V4 for the US cities and TerraClimate plus Korea Meteorological Administration data for Busan.

Limitations: the geographic coverage spans only four metropolitan areas, and the authors propose extending to more cities and climate zones, adding external validation such as utility audits or smart-meter data, and coupling energy with grid carbon intensity for operational emissions. All MUSE2 data come from publicly available, openly licensed sources with no household-level data. The dataset is released at huggingface.co/datasets/skl24/MUSE2 and the code at github.com/kailaisun/DySENSE.

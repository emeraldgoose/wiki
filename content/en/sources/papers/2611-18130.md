# JFinBench: A Continuously Refreshable Japanese Financial Benchmark from the Latest Certification Exams

**Source**: [arXiv:2611.18130](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.18130) · **Published**: 2026-11-13
**Authors**: Issa Sugiura, Taro Makino, Takashi Ishida

## Overview

Large language models are moving into finance, but evaluating them there is hard because financial competence is tied to country-specific tax rules, accounting standards, and regulations. Most existing benchmarks are English-language and U.S.-centric, and the earlier Japanese benchmark built from certification exams covered only four exams with multiple-choice questions, leaving tax calculations, essays, and figure-dependent questions unevaluated. A second problem is staleness: tax rules change often and released questions leak into training data, so scores increasingly risk measuring memorization rather than generalization.

JFinBench, from Sakana AI, is a Japanese financial benchmark of 321 examples taken from the 2026 editions of six regularly administered certification exams: the CPA short-answer exam, the Certified Public Tax Accountant exam, the FP skill test (written), the financial counter services skill test, and the Nissho and Zensho bookkeeping tests. Together they span accounting, auditing, tax law, financial planning, and banking. Because these exams are held and officially published on a regular schedule, each new edition can supply fresh expert-written questions that postdate the training data of existing models — a live-benchmark strategy applied to Japanese finance.

To make such refreshes practical, the authors built a human-in-the-loop agentic pipeline. Shared scripts and a VLM transcribe each exam PDF into layout-preserving markdown, the coding agent Claude Code writes exam-specific code that turns transcripts into structured examples and generates a side-by-side review page, and a human verifies every example and feeds corrections back to the agent. Verifying all 321 examples took roughly two hours per exam, about 12 hours in total.

Evaluating 12 recent LLMs, Claude Fable 5 scores 95.8 and GPT-6 Astra 93.6 overall without tools, while the best open-weight model, Kimi K3.1 Pro, reaches 83.4 — a 12-point gap. Granting every model a terminal and web access lifts all scores and narrows the gap to about 4 points, but the ranking barely moves, which the authors read as evidence that knowledge stored in the model's parameters remains valuable even for tool-using agents.

## Key points

- Scale and format: 321 separately graded examples derived from 267 source problems across six 2026 exams — 193 MCQ, 87 short answer, 18 structured answer forms graded blank by blank (up to about 40 blanks per form), and 23 rubric-graded essays; 12 examples ship images such as land-plot diagrams as visual inputs.
- Contamination control: every exam postdates every reported knowledge cutoff, and the benchmark is designed to be refreshed with future editions, since adding a new exam or edition requires only agent-written conversion code plus human verification.
- Without tools at maximum reasoning effort: Claude Fable 5 95.8, GPT-6 Astra 93.6, Gemini 3.8 Flash 88.8 — the three proprietary models take the top three ranks, while the best open-weight model Kimi K3.1 Pro scores 83.4 and every open-weight model sits below every proprietary one. The tax accountant exam is the hardest, where the best score is 79.4.
- The hierarchy inverts in agentic coding, where Kimi K3.1 Pro is reported at 65.7% on Terminal-Bench 4.0 versus GPT-6 Astra's 58.2%; on JFinBench, GPT-6 Astra instead leads Kimi K3.1 Pro by 10.2 points, pointing at missing specialized Japanese financial knowledge rather than general capability.
- Cost and scale: raising reasoning effort raises both score and cost for every model; Gemini 3.8 Flash is the efficiency standout at $0.025 per question and still outscores every open-weight model even at low effort; among the nine open-weight models with public parameter counts, score correlates with total parameters (Pearson r = 0.82), though Kimi K3.1 Pro adds 3.5 points over Kimi K3 at the same 2.8T parameters.
- Failure analysis of failed examples (score below 0.5) with an LLM classifier validated at Cohen's κ = 0.84 on 30 human-labeled samples: knowledge errors dominate, calculation errors are rare, and token-limit truncation clusters in five open-weight models — the two MiMo-V2.6 variants, Mistral Large 4, Qwen3.8 Flash, and GLM 5.3 Flash.
- With tools (Harbor running Terminus-2, mini-SWE-agent, and official developer harnesses over terminal plus web): every model improves, GLM 5.3 Flash most of all (+29.9 points to 87.6), and the best open-weight versus proprietary gap narrows from 12.4 to 4.4 points (Kimi K3.1 Pro 92.9 with Kimi Code versus GPT-6 Astra 97.3 with Terminus-2) while the ranking barely changes (Spearman ρ = 0.87).
- What agents do with tools: web retrieval in 56–66% of tasks and scripted calculation in 9–28%; all four analyzed models consult e-Gov, the government's statute database, most often, and open-weight models lean on search engines far more (DuckDuckGo in 47% of GLM 5.3 Flash's tasks versus at most 11% for proprietary models). Only 8 of 11,556 Terminus-2 attempts (0.07%) were exposed to answer material via search snippets and were scored 0.

## Details

Scoring is matched to question type: regex-based extraction with an LLM-judge fallback for MCQs, an equivalence judge that accepts mathematically or legally equivalent phrasings for short answers, blank-level partial credit for forms, and rubric-based coverage for essays, with GPT-5.1 as the grader model and a re-grading check with Claude Opus 5.5 yielding closely matching scores. Every combination of model and effort level runs three times and the mean is reported, and prompts state the exam date and the statutory basis date — for instance, "laws in force as of April 1, 2026 apply" — so answers are graded under the law in force at exam time.

The pipeline is portable beyond finance: transcripts are compared with PyMuPDF-extracted text as character multisets to flag omissions and hallucinations, and the coding agent works from natural-language instructions carrying source URLs and statutory basis dates, so no manual programming is needed per exam. The authors suggest the same workflow could serve other certification exams such as medical licensing or bar exams. The dataset, evaluation code, and construction pipeline are released at github.com/SakanaAI/JFinBench and huggingface.co/datasets/SakanaAI/JFinBench, with the code under Apache License 2.0.

Limitations: coverage is restricted to exams that publish past questions as official PDFs, so the Securities Analyst (CMA) exam is out of scope; knowledge cutoffs are self-declared, often unreported, and can diverge from the effective cutoff, so contamination cannot be fully excluded and periodic refresh remains the primary mitigation; and with the two leading proprietary models already scoring 96–100 on every exam except tax, JFinBench offers them limited headroom and is best suited to tracking open-weight progress.

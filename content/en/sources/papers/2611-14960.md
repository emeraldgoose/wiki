# Termination Lag: Coding Agents Solve Early but Stop Late

**Source**: [arXiv:2611.14960](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14960) — Musa Cim, Burak Topcu, Mahmut Taylan Kandemir (The Pennsylvania State University). v1 submitted 2026-11-11.

## Overview

Coding agents are typically scored on whether the final patch they submit passes the benchmark tests. That single number hides *when* the correct solution actually appeared during the run — and that timing is what drives wall-clock duration, token cost, and the risk of breaking working code. This paper isolates that gap. The authors define **time to first correct patch (TTFCP)** as the number of steps until the agent's patch first passes the benchmark tests, and **termination lag (TL)** as the steps the agent takes after that point before it finally stops.

To measure this, they record and offline-test every distinct repository patch an agent generates during execution, across five models, two agent frameworks, and three SWE-bench datasets. The result: a correct solution is usually available early, but agents keep working long afterward, mostly re-verifying their work and wandering the repository — and in a small fraction of runs, that extra activity actively corrupts a patch that was already correct.

## Key points

- Across successful runs, **38–82%** reach a correct solution on their **initial source-code edit** — the first edit is often already right.
- Post-solution steps account for a median of **35–37%** of the trajectory for Qwen3-Coder and **54–60%** for Laguna-XS.2 on SWE-bench Verified and Multilingual.
- Median termination lag on SWE-bench Verified is **16 steps** (Qwen3-Coder-30B), **57 steps** (Laguna-XS.2), **10 steps** (Claude Haiku 5.5, first correct patch at step 9), and **6 steps** (GPT-6 Luna); Qwen3-Coder on Multilingual shows a median lag of **18 steps** (37% normalized). With the mini-SWE-agent framework, Qwen3-Coder on Verified shows a median lag of **12 steps** (37% normalized). Seed-OSS-36B shows the same pattern on SWE-bench Pro.
- Agents spend most post-solution steps **verifying their work and exploring the repository**.
- In **1–8%** of cases, continued activity leads the agent to **undo its earlier correct patch**, leaving the final submission incorrect — so a failing final score can hide an already-solved task.
- An oracle that stops each solved run at its first correct patch would eliminate a median of **35%/37%** of steps for Qwen3-Coder (Verified/Multilingual), **60%/54%** for Laguna-XS.2, and **54%** for Haiku on Verified, while preserving every originally solved task.

## Details

**Method.** The study tracks each trajectory from the first source-code edit through generation of the correct patch to final termination. After every agent step, the repository patch is recorded; each distinct patch is then evaluated offline against the official benchmark tests. From this they compute TTFCP (steps until the first passing patch), termination lag (steps from first correct patch to the agent's self-initiated stop), and normalized lag (TL as a fraction of the trajectory). First-patch correctness is counted over all trajectories that reach a correct patch, considering distinct patches up to and including the first correct one. The research questions cover: when the first edit happens and how often it is already correct (RQ1), how long the agent keeps working afterward (RQ2), what the extra steps do (RQ3), and whether that extra work helps or hurts (RQ4).

**Results.** Correct patches appear well before runs end: for Qwen3-Coder on Verified, the median lag is 16 steps / 35% normalized (18 steps / 37% on Multilingual); Laguna-XS.2 shows 57 steps / 60% on Verified and 56 steps / 54% on Multilingual; Claude Haiku 5.5 reaches its first correct patch at step 9 with a median lag of 10 steps / 54%; GPT-6 Luna shows 6 steps / 36% on Verified; the pattern also holds for Seed-OSS-36B on SWE-bench Pro and for Qwen3-Coder under mini-SWE-agent (12 steps / 37%). Post-fix activity is dominated by verification and repository exploration rather than new edits. The cost of the gap is not only wasted tokens and latency: in 1–8% of cases the agent overwrites or corrupts a patch that had already passed, converting a solved task into a failed submission. The authors frame final-patch benchmark scoring as conflating two distinct abilities — producing a correct fix and recognizing that the fix is complete — and argue that knowing when to terminate is as critical to agent performance as generating the fix itself.

# The Narrative Gap: Can LLMs Help Us Navigate Diverse Narratives Across Languages?

**Source**: [arXiv:2611.12750](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.12750) · **Published**: 2026-11-09
**Authors**: Nikhil Sharma, Kelly Marchisio, Kenton Murray, Ziang Xiao

## Overview

Large language models increasingly sit between people and the information they consume, and on contested topics the accounts available differ sharply across languages and regions. The authors argue that helping users navigate these competing narratives requires six capabilities: three foundational ones (including a requested narrative, excluding one, and identifying what an answer is missing) and three higher-level ones (presenting diverse narratives, preserving coverage when a query endorses a belief, and keeping information parity across languages).

To measure them, the team built NarrativeBench from 1,333 news articles spanning 23 publication languages and 23 contested topics, grounded in 460 Reddit-derived queries rendered across 24 languages for 11,038 query-language versions. Fourteen LLMs from the Qwen3.5, GPT-OSS, Gemma 4, DeepSeek V4, Gemini, GPT, Kimi, and Command A families were run under 15 configurations over 4,800 cases, yielding 72,000 scored responses.

The headline result is uniform weakness: mean narrative-inclusion F1 is 19.9%, and even the strongest configuration leaves about a quarter of available narratives unrepresented after an explicit diversity request. Coverage also shifts with query framing and language, suggesting LLMs risk recreating the filter bubbles and echo chambers of earlier information systems rather than removing them.

## Key points

- NarrativeBench combines 1,333 news articles, 23 publication languages, 23 contested topics, and 460 Reddit-grounded queries, expanded to 11,038 query-language versions across 24 languages.
- Foundational gaps: mean narrative-inclusion (NI) F1 is 19.9% against 49.3% for narrative exclusion (NE) and 56.8% recall for missing-narrative identification (MNI); NI trails NE in every configuration.
- Best models: Gemini 3.8 Flash tops NI F1 at 52.5% and MNI recall at 82.8%, while Gemini 2.5 Pro leads NE F1 at 74.2%.
- Filter bubbles: narrative recall averages 44.3% under baseline prompting and 49.2% under diverse prompting; the best configuration reaches 70.6% and 74.9%, still omitting a quarter of narratives.
- Echo chambers: the mean absolute recall difference between neutral and confirmatory queries is 3.23 percentage points, ranging from 0.32 (Qwen3.5 9B IT) to 10.09 (Gemini 3.8 Flash).
- Linguistic disparity: mean absolute gaps from English are 8.74 points for query languages and 7.51 for target narrative languages; Tigrinya, Nepali, and Armenian sources suffer omission rates 59.8, 57.9, and 54.6 points above English under diverse prompting.
- Translation quality: 1,704 checked translations scored 0.853-0.879 on COMETKiwi, with annotators judging 90.8% meaning-preserving and 89.0% plausibly human-asked.

## Details

### Benchmark design

The six capabilities are operationalized as three foundational tasks — narrative inclusion, narrative exclusion, and missing-narrative identification — plus higher-level checks that compare baseline versus diverse prompting (HC-1), neutral versus confirmatory framing (HC-2), and query-language versus source-language performance (HC-3). The 460 queries split evenly into 230 neutral and 230 confirmatory versions. Translations were produced with Google Translate and validated with COMETKiwi scores and human review. The dataset is released on Hugging Face as jhu-clsp/narrative-bench with evaluation code on GitHub.

### What the numbers mean

Across all 15 configurations, inclusion F1 never beats exclusion F1, so models are better at dropping a narrative than at faithfully presenting one when asked. The 3.23-point framing gap shows coverage wobbles under leading questions: in the paper's Greenland-independence example, a Bengali confirmatory query raised recall from 50.0% to 83.3% while the answer simply affirmed the query's premise. Language gaps remain large even for the best systems, and source-language omission compounds the disparity for low-resource languages, so information parity across languages stays out of reach — the paper's case that model development must explicitly target these information capabilities.

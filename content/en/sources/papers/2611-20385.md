# DominoBench: Evaluating Multi-Step Physical Reasoning from Still Images

**Source**: [huggingface](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20385)
**Date**: 2026-11-19

## Overview

DominoBench asks a deceptively simple question of vision-language models: given a single still image of a 2D physics scene — ramps, ledges, pendulums, seesaws, rows of dominoes — can the model predict how the chain reaction plays out once a ball is pushed? Recent multimodal models describe scenes fluently and solve textbook physics problems, so one might expect them to simulate what happens next. Testing 30 models from 10 families, the author finds the opposite: unaided, no model predicts the outcome reliably.

The benchmark contains 854 items arranged in 427 minimal pairs. Within each pair, a change of one to three grid steps in a single quantity decides whether one specific person survives, and every other person's fate stays fixed. Each item ships with a four-rung hint ladder whose final rung states the exact calculation that decides the outcome, letting evaluators separate perception failures from quantitative-reasoning failures.

The headline result is sharp. Unaided, frontier models from Anthropic, Google and OpenAI identify the person whose fate flips in at most 75% of items, and answer both twins of a pair correctly in at most 40%. Pointing at the deciding step barely helps, and exact geometry given as text barely helps either. Yet when the deciding calculation and its required quantities — including speeds a still image cannot show — are written out, the same models reach 80-92% on the deciding person, while small open-weight models remain near chance.

The author concludes that physical prediction from still images fails at the quantitative step: strong models can use the deciding quantity once it is supplied, but they do not produce it on their own. For models that plan or act in the physical world, a fluent scene description is therefore no evidence of a correct prediction; DominoBench tests whether models find and compute the deciding quantity unprompted.

## Key points

- 854 items in 427 minimal pairs; each pair differs in one placement parameter by one to three grid steps (0.25-square length steps) and flips the fate of exactly one person.
- Scenes are side views on a visible grid, built deterministically in pymunk from JSON scene programs; ground truth is what the simulator does once the ball is pushed, and the picture always shows the settled state at rest.
- Seven mechanism types (ledge, pit, pendulum, recess, seesaw, dominoes, jump), each able to kill one labeled person (A-F); person A pushes a stone or wood ball.
- Every item passes five generation checks: settle, trigger, chain (at least three mechanisms reached in both twins), stability under solver perturbations, and solvability via an idealized calculation.
- Frontier models (Anthropic, Google, OpenAI): at most 75% accuracy on the fate-flipping person unaided, at most 40% on answering both twins correctly.
- Hint ladder: pointing at the deciding step helps little; exact geometry as text barely helps; with the full deciding calculation and quantities given, the same models reach 80-92%.
- Small open-weight models gain little even from the calculation and stay near chance.
- Failure localizes to the quantitative step — models use the deciding quantity when given it but cannot derive it from the image alone.
- Benchmark, generator and evaluation code are released at https://huggingface.co/datasets/emilioferrara/DominoBench.

## Details

**Method.** DominoBench items are generated, not hand-drawn. A scene program stored as JSON lists parameterized parts and builds deterministically in pymunk. The physics rules are stated with every item: 2D rigid bodies with mass equal to density times area (stone 2.5, wood 0.6 per unit area), gravity 9.81 squares/s^2, the push sets the first ball rolling at 1.5 squares/s, rolling without slipping except after landings, and a person dies when any moving solid object touches them (a convention that keeps every outcome categorical). Each scene chains three to five mechanisms drawn from a library of seven, parameterized continuously (drop height, pit width, pendulum length, domino gap, seesaw counterweight, etc.).

Before an item enters the benchmark it must pass five checks: a settle check (nothing moves more than 0.05 squares without the push), trigger and chain checks (the push starts the chain and reaches at least three mechanisms in both twins), a stability check (re-simulation at half timestep with doubled solver iterations, friction scaled by 0.9 and 1.1, elasticity raised by 0.05 — any fate change rejects the item), and a solvability check guaranteeing that an idealized calculation on the quantities stated in the H3 hint reproduces both outcomes. Twins are located by guided search: from one simulation of the base scene, the author reads the quantities each mechanism's outcome depends on (e.g., arrival speed at a ledge, rotation count at a recess), then treats each fate as a function of the mechanism's placement parameter and finds the tipping point (projectile vs. box for ledges, projectile vs. rim for pits, energy and reach for pendulums, gap vs. domino height, rotation phase vs. a simulation-calibrated notch window for recesses, torque about the fulcrum for seesaws).

Evaluation uses a four-rung hint ladder, each rung a fresh query with all hints up to it, ending in the exact calculation that decides the outcome. This design separates "cannot see the decisive detail" from "cannot compute with it."

**Results.** Across 30 models from 10 families, unaided frontier models from Anthropic, Google and OpenAI are right about the fate-flipping person in at most 75% of items, and at most 40% correctly answer both twins of a pair — far from reliable. The hint ladder shows where the failure lives: pointing at the deciding step helps little, and exact geometry given as text barely improves unaided accuracy; small open-weight models also gain little even from the calculation. Once the deciding calculation and the quantities it needs — including speeds a still image cannot show — are written out, frontier models reach 80-92% on the deciding person, indicating they can execute the quantitative reasoning when the quantity is supplied, but cannot extract or derive it from the image alone. The paper concludes physical prediction from a still image fails at the quantitative step, and that for models acting in the physical world a fluent scene account is no evidence of a correct prediction.

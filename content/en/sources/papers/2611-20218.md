# SWE-CoWork: Benchmarking Coding Agents as Digital Employees in Organizations

**Source**: [huggingface](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20218)
**Date**: 2026-11-19

## Overview

SWE-CoWork is a benchmark for evaluating coding agents as they would actually operate inside a software organization: not given a clean, self-contained issue, but dropped into an ongoing project with a brief handoff and expected to figure out what needs to be built. It comprises 101 development assignments across 101 real repositories, 11 programming languages, and 12 application domains.

Each assignment places the agent in a simulated engineering organization with 26 colleagues who have distinct responsibilities, bounded knowledge, and controlled collaboration behavior. Requirements are not handed over in one document; they are distributed across colleagues and workplace records — project boards, versioned design documents, conversations, reviews, and execution history — and they can conflict. Agents must recover the requirements, reconcile conflicting evidence, implement changes, and pass private executable tests.

The headline finding is a wide gap between partial implementation and complete delivery. The two leading models satisfy roughly 63% of fail-to-pass (F2P) tests, yet complete fewer than 8% of assignments end to end. Supplying the complete requirements up front improves F2P by about 16 percentage points for Seed Evolving and GPT-6 Astra, but leaves complete task success unchanged — the bottleneck is sustained requirement tracking through clarification, implementation, verification, and handoff, not merely knowing what to build.

## Key points

- 101 assignments, 101 real repositories, 11 programming languages, 12 application domains.
- Each run simulates an engineering organization of 26 colleagues with distinct roles, bounded knowledge, and controlled collaboration behavior; agents start from a brief handoff, not a full spec.
- Evaluation uses private executable tests, so reported pass rates reflect actually working changes, not self-reported completion.
- Eleven models were evaluated; a substantial gap exists between partial implementation (F2P tests satisfied) and complete assignment delivery (fewer than 8% for the two leaders).
- Providing complete requirements upfront improves F2P by ~16 percentage points for Seed Evolving and GPT-6 Astra, with no change in complete task success.
- Requirement-level trajectory analysis shows Claude-Opus-5.5 directs a mean of 93% of first questions about queried cards to their accountable owners, but obtains only 64% of colleague-held requirements.
- Failure modes include deferred questions going unfollowed, incomplete implementations even after requirements are acquired, and 31% of requirements reported as completed failing their tests.

## Details

**Method.** SWE-CoWork models the full delivery loop rather than isolated code generation. An agent receives only a brief handoff and must (1) identify which colleagues or records hold the relevant requirements, (2) ask clarifying questions with enough context to get useful answers, (3) follow up on deferred answers and reconcile contradictory sources, (4) implement the change in the real repository, and (5) verify it against private executable fail-to-pass tests. Because requirements live with specific owners and in versioned artifacts that may disagree (a superseded design doc, a stale CI run, a colleague with a local constraint), success depends on evidence provenance and organizational reasoning as much as on coding ability. The benchmark also supports requirement-level trajectory analysis: each requirement is traced across conversations, code changes, verification, and handoff notes, and mapped to individual tests, which localizes where tracking breaks down.

**Results.** Across the eleven evaluated models, the two leaders satisfy approximately 63% of F2P tests but complete fewer than 8% of assignments. An ablation that provides complete requirements upfront yields about a 16-percentage-point F2P improvement for Seed Evolving and GPT-6 Astra, yet complete task success is unchanged, indicating that missing information is not the binding constraint. Trajectory analysis of Claude-Opus-5.5 shows strong targeting of questions (~93% of first questions about queried cards go to the accountable owner) but incomplete acquisition (~64% of colleague-held requirements obtained), plus late-stage failures: unraised cards, abandoned deferrals, unsatisfied disclosed requirements, premature stops, and completion claims contradicted by tests — 31% of requirements reported complete fail their tests. The authors conclude that sustained requirement tracking across the whole delivery arc is a key open challenge for reliable autonomous software engineering.

# Locked at the Entrance, Open Inside: Where RLVR Narrows the Solution Space

Source: Qiancheng Zhou, Ruizhe Li, arXiv:2608.29188, Aug 29, 2026.

**Lead**: Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) substantially improves single-sample accuracy, but it **contracts the policy's solution space**, diminishing the returns of test-time scaling. This paper localises *where* in a reasoning trajectory the breadth is lost: is the policy failing to *access* a valid solution family, or failing to *execute* it once started? Using the Countdown task — whose solution space can be exhaustively enumerated into entrance families defined by first operand and operator — across PPO on Qwen2.5-3B and GRPO on Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct, **solution coverage falls by up to 67%**. The contraction is heavily concentrated at the entrance: per-token likelihood shifts are **11x–16x larger before the first arithmetic operation** than during downstream reasoning. Supplying only an unselected entrance prefix restores low-access-family completion rates by **over an order of magnitude (0.018 → 0.212 under PPO)** — alternative solutions remain executable, they are simply never initiated. Guided by this localisation, surface prompting fails but entrance-targeted intervention succeeds: **late-layer parameter interpolation with early checkpoints raises coverage 37% at no loss in pass@1**.

## The problem: pass@1 up, breadth down

RLVR's headline benefit is well established — verifiable rewards raise pass@1 substantially. The cost is less discussed. Training on a scalar reward **narrows the policy's solution space**, which in turn diminishes what test-time scaling can buy you. If the model only considers one route to an answer, sampling more doesn't help: you are resampling from a smaller set.

This matters because two techniques that both depend on breadth — test-time scaling and parallel sampling — become less effective precisely when a model has been RL-tuned.

## Access versus execution

The paper's contribution is to ask a sharper question than "did coverage drop?" It asks *where inside the trajectory* the loss occurs:

- **Access failure** — a valid solution family exists in the model's repertoire, but the policy never reaches it
- **Execution failure** — the policy enters the family but fails to carry it through

These have opposite implications. Execution failure is a capability gap that better training might close. Access failure is a *policy* problem — the capability is there and simply not being used — and no amount of additional training on the same objective will fix it.

## The setup: making coverage measurable

To answer this, the paper uses **Countdown**, whose solution space can be **exhaustively enumerated** into discrete *entrance families* defined by the first operand and the operator. That is what makes "coverage" a countable quantity rather than a judgment call: you can ask which fraction of the reachable solution families the policy ever initiates, and check whether it completes them.

This is a much stronger measurement than aggregate accuracy, because accuracy is consistent with many distinct policies — some broad, some narrow. Enumerating entrances distinguishes them.

Across both training setups (PPO on Qwen2.5-3B, GRPO on Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct), **solution coverage falls by up to 67%**, and it **halves even on problems the model solves across all checkpoints** — the subset where capability is clearly present.

## The finding: the loss is at the door

The decisive measurement is where in the trajectory the likelihood shifts occur. Per-token likelihood shifts are **11x–16x larger prior to the first arithmetic operation** than during downstream reasoning.

So the contraction is not a gradual narrowing across the whole chain of thought. It is concentrated almost entirely at the opening move — choosing which family to enter at all. Once inside a family, the reasoning proceeds much as before.

The paper's summary line captures it: **reasoning breadth is lost at the door, not inside the room.**

## The causal test: supply the prefix

Localisation is suggestive; the stronger evidence is intervention. If the model can complete an alternative solution when handed the entrance but never chooses it, then the solution was never inaccessible — only uninitiated.

That is exactly what the paper tests: **supplying only an unselected entrance prefix** (and nothing else) restores completion rates in low-access families by **over an order of magnitude** — 0.018 → 0.212 under PPO.

This is the cleanest evidence in the paper. The capability demonstrably exists in the weights; RLVR changed the policy's *preferences*, not its abilities.

## What does and doesn't fix it

Having localised the problem, the paper tests two categories of remedy:

**Surface prompting fails.** Simply asking for diverse solutions does not restore coverage — consistent with the finding that the narrowing is in the policy's likelihood over entrances, not in an instruction-following deficit.

**Entrance-targeted intervention succeeds.** **Late-layer parameter interpolation with early checkpoints** increases solution coverage by **37% at no loss in pass@1**. Intervening on parameters rather than prompts is what the localisation predicts.

## Not an inevitable side effect

The paper also tests whether the entropy collapse is an unavoidable byproduct of reasoning optimisation. It is not:

- Early-step entropy collapse recurs across **six math benchmarks** with **7B and 14B** models — so it is not a small-model artifact
- But an **SFT baseline preserves more than double the coverage**
- And **staged SFT→DPO→RLVR pipelines retain early-step entropy**

So the pipeline *order* matters. Applying RLVR directly from a base model collapses entropy early; routing through SFT and DPO first does not. This is a practically actionable finding for anyone assembling a post-training pipeline.

## Relevance to SW engineers

- Treat RLVR's coverage loss as a real cost, not a footnote. If you rely on test-time scaling, measure solution diversity, not just pass@1.
- "Enumerate the reachable solution set" is a general technique for telling capability apart from policy. If you can count the distinct valid approaches to a task, you can tell whether a model *cannot* do something or merely never tries.
- The prefix-supply experiment is a general diagnostic: hand the model the decision you're worried about and see if the rest still works. If it does, you have a preference problem, not a capability problem.
- If you are building a post-training pipeline, consider staging (SFT→DPO→RLVR) over going straight to RLVR from base.

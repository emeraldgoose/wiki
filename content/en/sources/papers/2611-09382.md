# Exploring Weight-Space Programming: Capabilities, Generalization, and Limitations

**Source**: [arXiv:2611.09382](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09382) · **Published**: 2026-11-06
**Authors**: Junru Hu, Cheng Tang, Wei Li, Hongze Zhu, Chun-Mei Feng, Qi Zhang, Yirong Chen, Ming Hu

## Overview

Model adaptation usually means gradient-based fine-tuning, but the paper studies a different regime: Weight-Space Programming (WSP), in which a reasoning agent reads a model's raw parameters, decides on an intervention, and writes modified weights back — with no gradients or backpropagation anywhere in the loop. Prior work introduced this capability, but how far it actually goes was unknown: what gains are attainable, whether an edit made for one task helps on others, and where the approach breaks down. This paper answers those questions with a systematic evaluation rather than a single showcase.

The setup is an agentic pipeline. GPT-6 Astra acts as the editing agent and operates on two Qwen3.5 checkpoints (0.8B and 9B) through a persistent Python environment for weight analysis, while a separate GPU worker runs inference; the original checkpoint is never overwritten. Each of the 16 editing trajectories — one per model–benchmark pair, with three independent searches for 0.8B MATH — gets a budget of 50 successful forward-tool requests to diagnose behavior, construct interventions, and evaluate candidates. Edits are computed in float32, stored in bfloat16, and restricted to admissible transformer-block parameters: token embeddings, the final normalization, and the language-model head stay fixed. Within those constraints the agent may write sparse MLP modifications, rank-one updates, attention-head rescaling, or combinations of these.

The headline result is that large gains are possible through strikingly small, targeted edits: on full held-out evaluations, accuracy improves by up to 29.45 points on MATH-500, 4.42 on GPQA Diamond, 36.72 on IFEval, and 17.11 on SciBench — and the best MATH-500 edit modifies only 1,023 parameter values. But the paper is equally clear about the limits: outcomes vary widely across independent searches, six of the 16 trajectories end with the original weights retained because improvements could not be reliably validated, and cross-task transfer is real yet uneven.

## Key points

- 16 editing trajectories across seven benchmarks (MATH, MMLU-Pro, IFEval, GPQA Diamond, MedXpertQA, SciBench, SciCode) and two model scales (Qwen3.5-0.8B and Qwen3.5-9B), each with a 50-request forward-tool budget and no gradient access.
- Large localized gains on held-out sets: MATH-500 +29.45, GPQA Diamond +4.42, IFEval +36.72, SciBench +17.11 points; the best MATH-500 edit changes only 1,023 BF16 values in a single output column of one layer.
- Cross-task transfer without further editing: the Math-edited checkpoint improves GPQA Diamond by 3.03, IFEval by 12.18, and SciBench by 20.10 points while leaving SciCode unchanged; at 9B, an IFEval edit lifts SciBench and a SciBench edit lifts IFEval — but some transfers are marginal or harmful.
- Reliability gap: in six of 16 trajectories the agent keeps the original weights; a 9B MATH candidate with a +29.69-point development gain actually lost 0.35 points on held-out MATH-500.
- Intervention structure beats scale: three independent MATH searches all targeted the layer-23 MLP down-projection yet gained +29.45, +5.00, and +27.30 points while modifying 1,023, 1,037,572, and 1,981,083 values respectively — parameter count does not predict effectiveness.
- Smaller models have more headroom: Qwen3.5-0.8B shows larger best development gains than 9B on all seven benchmark categories, consistent with its much weaker baselines (e.g., MATH-500 8.35% vs 97.70%).

## Details

**Method.** At each iteration the agent constructs an update Δθ from the current weights and its accumulated observations, applied as θ ← bf16(θ + Δθ). Feedback comes in two complementary modes: autoregressive generation to measure task performance, and teacher forcing, which exposes token log-probabilities and intermediate activations for mechanistic analysis. The final checkpoint is chosen using development feedback alone, with the option of retaining the original weights, and is then evaluated against the base model on held-out test sets under matched settings.

**Benchmarks and splits.** The seven benchmarks cover math reasoning (MATH; development on GSM8K/MATH train + AIME, held-out MATH-500), general knowledge (MMLU-Pro), instruction following (IFEval; a fixed 270/271 split of its 541 official prompts), scientific QA (GPQA Diamond; development on GPQA Main), medical QA (MedXpertQA-Text), scientific reasoning (SciBench; numerical answers with 5% relative tolerance), and scientific coding (SciCode; complete executable code chains).

**Held-out results.** On 0.8B, kept edits improved MATH-500 from 8.35 to 37.80, IFEval from 21.40 to 58.12, SciBench from 1.38 to 18.49, and GPQA Diamond from 25.25 to 29.67; on 9B, gains were smaller (IFEval +1.85, SciBench +3.03, SciCode +1.54). Six trajectories retained the original checkpoint: 0.8B MedXpertQA and MMLU-Pro, and 9B MATH, GPQA Diamond, MedXpertQA, and MMLU-Pro — in each case positive development gains shrank or reversed under broader validation (e.g., the 9B GPQA search's initial +6.25 faded on re-evaluation). The full 0.8B MATH trajectory shows the working style: 50 requests split into 32 generation and 18 teacher-forced calls, moving from behavioral diagnosis through component exploration to targeted interventions, controls, and broader validation.

**Analysis.** Three findings stand out. First, weight-space interventions generalize to unseen questions — all three independent MATH searches produced positive held-out gains (+29.45, +5.00, +27.30), and task-specific edits improved other held-out benchmarks. Second, exploration is path-dependent: the same model, benchmark, and even the same target tensor (layer-23 MLP down-projection) yielded three structurally different interventions with very different returns. Third, transfer exists but is asymmetric and task-pair specific: the 0.8B IFEval-edited checkpoint also improved MATH and SciBench, while some interventions produced regressions on other tasks.

**Limitations.** The authors emphasize that development gains do not always translate into reliable held-out improvements, that repeated evaluation is computationally expensive, and that the mechanisms behind successful editing and generalization remain unclear — including the effects of jointly editing multiple locations and of development data size and diversity. WSP has also not yet been tested on longer-horizon reasoning or complex agentic tasks.

# AI Reviews Are Biased Towards the Appendix

**Source**: [arXiv:2611.17811](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17811) · **Published**: 2026-11-13
**Authors**: Saptarshi Ghosh, Mohammad Saim, Tianyu Jiang

## Overview

Asking a large language model to review a paper has become a visible part of peer review: in ICLR 2026, roughly a fifth of all reviews were flagged as fully AI-generated, and reviewers routinely carry the weaknesses an LLM lists straight into their own reviews. Prior work has compared AI reviews with human ones, but little was known about which parts of a paper these models actually criticize. A University of Cincinnati team — Saptarshi Ghosh, Mohammad Saim, and Tianyu Jiang — is, to their knowledge, the first to study the question, and their answer is striking: the newest GPT and Claude models point most of their criticism at the appendix.

Across 800 papers from ACL 2026, EMNLP 2026, COLM 2026, and ICLR 2027, GPT-6-Astra, GPT-6.1-Sol, Claude-Opus-5.5, and Claude-Sonnet-5.5 draw about 50% or more of their review weaknesses from appendix-related material, and the pattern holds whether the model is reached through the API, a coding agent such as Codex or Claude Code, or the web interface. The behavior looks generational: GPT-5 drew fewer than 30% of its weaknesses from the appendix, while GPT-6-Astra and GPT-6.1-Sol land around 60-70% at every conference.

Humans read very differently. In human-written ICLR reviews from 2021 to 2024, only 8.4% of weaknesses touched the appendix, and those mostly concerned presentation and the paper's core idea. LLM weaknesses instead peak deep inside the appendix and keep going past page 30. Worryingly, the human numbers are drifting toward the models': after GPT-6-Astra and Claude-Opus-5.5 were released, the appendix share of weaknesses in human reviews nearly tripled to 27.60% in the 2026 review cycle for ICLR 2027.

## Key points

- Scale of the study: 2,000 papers in total — 800 recent submissions (200 each from ACL 2026, EMNLP 2026, COLM 2026, and ICLR 2027, all with appendices of at least 15 pages) plus 1,200 ICLR papers from 2021 through 2026 — reviewed by GPT-6-Astra, GPT-6.1-Sol, Claude-Opus-5.5, and Claude-Sonnet-5.5.
- Headline result: across almost all models and all four recent conferences, more than half of LLM weaknesses draw on appendix-related content; GPT attributes 17.33% of its weaknesses to the appendix alone and Claude 10.16%.
- The bias is new: GPT-5 produced fewer than 30% appendix-related weaknesses, while the newer GPT-6.1-Sol and GPT-6-Astra reach roughly 60-70% at every conference, suggesting the bias is a property of the latest models.
- Robust to access method: on 100 ICLR 2027 submissions with GPT-6-Astra, all four access methods (direct PDF via API, extracted text via API, the Codex coding agent, and the web interface) still showed more than 50% appendix-related weaknesses.
- Human contrast: ICLR 2021-2024 human reviews attribute 8.40% (±1.44) of weaknesses to the appendix and 4.36% (±1.03) to the appendix alone, versus 62.00% (±7.70) and 14.19% (±3.32) for LLM reviews of ICLR 2027; only 159 of the 800 sampled human reviews contain any appendix criticism, while all 200 LLM reviews do.
- Drift in human reviews: the appendix share of human weaknesses held at 10.47, 8.37, 8.39, 6.37, and 6.06% across the 2020-2024 review years, rose to 11.27% in 2025 (the first cycle after GPT-5's release, when about a fifth of ICLR 2026 reviews were flagged as fully AI-generated), then jumped to 27.60% in 2026.
- Where each reader looks: humans concentrate criticism on the first five pages and their appendix concerns fade with depth; LLM weaknesses climb through pages 6-7, peak well inside the appendix, and persist beyond page 30.
- What gets criticized (8-category taxonomy): LLM appendix criticism is 54.0% Methodology, 25.2% Analysis, and 13.6% Results but only 2.1% Presentation, while humans give 35.7% to Methodology and 32.2% to Presentation — LLMs audit technical detail, humans also judge how the paper is written.

## Details

The pipeline has two steps. Each anonymized paper is handed to the model with a deliberately minimal prompt — "I am reviewing this paper for [conference_name]. Give me a [conference_name] style review." — mirroring how a reviewer might plausibly use an LLM and avoiding prompt artifacts; because all four conferences share a similar review format, the conference name alone yields a structured review. Then the same model receives the paper and its review and names the section behind each weakness, up to two sections per weakness or "not specified" for general remarks. A weakness counts as appendix-related if any of its supporting evidence comes from the appendix. Accepted papers from ACL, EMNLP, and COLM were anonymized by stripping author names, affiliations, and links, and web search and browsing tools were disabled so the models had to rely only on the PDF.

The access-method comparison shows how little the harness matters. Codex never sees the PDF directly: it runs shell commands such as pdftotext and receives at most about 10k tokens of output per tool call, keeping the beginning and end and dropping the middle unless the model asks follow-up questions. The web interface adds system prompts, memory, and safety layers the user cannot configure. Yet all four methods produced the same above-50% appendix dependency, which the authors read as a property of the underlying model rather than of any interface.

Limitations are stated openly: section attribution and the eight-category sorting are done by GPT-6.1-Sol and only manually spot-checked; human and LLM reviews deliberately come from different years because older papers and reviews are likely in the models' training data; the rise in human appendix criticism is a temporal correlation, not proof of causation; and the study measures where criticism comes from, not whether each weakness is correct. The authors also do not analyze numerical scores. Code is available at github.com/cincynlp/review_appendix.

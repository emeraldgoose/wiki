# Sparks of Weight-Space Programming: LLMs can natively read & write neural network weights

**Source**: [Rikiya Blog](https://rikiyat.github.io/blog/weight-space-programming/) · **Published**: 2026-11-02

## Overview

The post asks whether a general-purpose frontier LLM can read and write a neural network's weights directly — not through an optimizer, and not with gradient descent — and reports early evidence that GPT-6 Astra can, sometimes with no environment feedback at all. The authors call the paradigm Weight-Space Programming: the model uses its reasoning to work out what each part of a network computes, or should compute, and changes the weights accordingly. No experiment let Astra write training loops.

On the reading side, Astra named the correct game for all four identically shaped PPO policies from their raw weights and with no tools; identified which shape each of three DDPMs generates without computing a forward pass; and, given only the four middle transformer blocks of three 7.16-million-weight character models, named the training text and every copying head. The one reading task that failed from weights alone was spotting the sycophantic member of a pair of Gemma 3 270M models, which became solvable once Astra could look at attention patterns.

On the writing side, a single one-shot edit of 3.5% of the weights turned a Kinetix car-on-ramp policy that stalled in 256 episodes into 1,024 successes out of 1,024; networks written from scratch beat PPO trained for 100 million steps in all three MinAtar games; and a Go network of 1,480,274 weights beat an equal-sized PPO network trained on 20 million games once Astra was allowed 50 forward passes.

The headline result scales the method to a real language model. On Qwen3.5-0.8B, Astra traced the reasoning loops that Qwen's own model card warns about to a direction written by the MLPs of layers 15 and 19, and subtracted it from 64 output columns in each — 131,072 weights, 0.016% of the model. MATH-500 rose from 7.9% to 48.1%, GSM8K from 13.8% to 67.1%, and instruction following (IFEval) from 21.0% to 62.3%. The authors read this as the beginning of a shift in mechanistic interpretability and neural-network optimization, and name context length and harness scale as the current limits.

## Key points

- From raw weights alone and with no tools, Astra named the right game for all four PPO policies (Breakout, Pong, Pac-Man, Freeway), which share the same inputs, actions and MLP 500 → 32 → 32 → 5 shape (17,253 weights, 1 billion PPO steps each, best of 3 seeds).
- Given only the four middle transformer blocks of three 7.16-million-weight character models — no input layer, no output layer, no labels — Astra named the training text in both attempts per model and listed exactly the copying heads (2.1, 3.1, 3.5 for Python; 3.3 for romaji; 3.1 for English), matching the ground truth measured by running the network.
- Astra fixed a Kinetix car-on-ramp policy that never got over the strip in 256 episodes by editing 3.5% of the weights in one shot, after which the car succeeded in all 1,024 episodes.
- Written from scratch with no data, loss, gradients or tools, Astra's MinAtar networks scored Breakout 517.4, Seaquest 53.1 and Asterix 53.2, against PPO at 100 million steps (15.8 / 5.6 / 25.8) and 500 million steps (25.3 / 88.9 / 55.2).
- On a 9×9 Go network of 1,480,274 weights that did not fit its context, 50 forward passes were enough for Astra's generated network to beat an equal-sized PPO network trained on 20 million games; with 0 forward passes it already won more than half of its games against two smaller PPO networks.
- Sycophancy could not be read from the weights alone — all four attempts failed — but with attention patterns exposed Astra picked the sycophant in all three model pairs: in the sycophant, head 10.2 sends 85.7% of its attention to the user's answer and 3.5% to the question's numbers, against 94.3% and 0.75% in the honest model.
- Editing 131,072 of Qwen3.5-0.8B's 0.8 billion weights (0.016%) raised MATH-500 from 7.9% to 48.1%, GSM8K from 13.8% to 67.1% and IFEval from 21.0% to 62.3%, while cutting the share of MATH-500 answers that hit the 32,768-token limit from 93% to 33%.
- The same approach could not make Gemma 3 270M, a model that rarely loops (8.5% of its answers reach its token limit), better at math: its failures were ordinary reasoning errors with no single component to switch off, and no edit moved MATH-500 by more than about a point.

## Details

The body of the post runs eight numbered experiments plus one additional experiment, grouped into reading weights, writing weights, and a final set of scenarios that scale up and use tools.

Reading. Experiment 1 trains four PPO policies — own grid versions of Breakout, Pong, Pac-Man and Freeway, all on a 10×10 grid with the same five actions — into the same MLP 500 → 32 → 32 → 5 (tanh, 17,253 weights) over 1 billion steps, and passes each network's raw weights to Astra as a prompt while naming no game. Astra named the right game for all four, apparently from where each input channel's first-layer weights concentrate (for Breakout, the agent's weights lie along the bottom row, a paddle) and which output actions the last layer separates. Experiment 2 repeats this on three DDPMs that generate 12×12 grayscale filled circles, squares and triangles (MLP 160 → 64 → 64 → 144, ReLU, 23,824 weights, Adam, 40,000 steps, batch 512) with no tool use and no forward passes; Astra named each shape, finding it in the output layer where hidden units draw pieces of the shape's edge — and drew every shape as an outline rather than the filled image the model really produces, which the authors read as evidence that it reads the geometry of the weights instead of evaluating the network. Experiment 3 removes the easy first and last layers: three character-level transformers (4 layers × 6 heads, width 384, 7.16 million weights, 28 characters, 1 billion characters each of Python code, Japanese literature in romaji, and English Wikipedia) are handed over as the four middle transformer blocks only, with Python to analyze them but no permission to run any layer on any input. Astra named the right language in both attempts for all three models and found every copying head — heads that let a model repeat text it has seen earlier — with no false positives, reproducing a weight-multiplication method from Elhage et al. that was never mentioned to it.

Writing. Experiment 4 takes the official Kinetix car-on-ramp actor (679 → 128 ReLU → five layers of 128 tanh → 16, 171,664 weights, PPO for 1 billion steps) that drives straight at the ramp and never succeeds in 256 episodes. Given the weights, a scene description and one recorded episode, Astra explained the local minimum and, in a single reply with no tools, added a small separate path through a few units per layer that reads the car's position and speed and switches off once the green wheel is past the red strip; the edit changes 3.5% of the weights and the car succeeds in all 1,024 episodes. Experiment 5 has Astra write weights from scratch for three MinAtar games (600 → 32 → 32 → 6, tanh, every weight starting at 0) with no data, loss, gradients, tools or rollouts, scoring over 1,000 episodes with sticky actions 0.1: it programs the first layer to compute where the ball will land and the second to move the paddle there. Experiment 6 tests a 9×9 Go network (243 → 512 × 6 → 82, ReLU, 1,480,274 weights) that is far too large for the context window, giving Astra a Python sandbox without a Go library, internet or search, a forward-pass budget from 0 to 50 (each pass is a batch of 128 rollouts against a practice opponent), and 2,000 scored games against each of two small PPO networks it never plays. With zero forward passes it writes the whole network as a Go program layer by layer in a single 336-line Python run and wins more than half its games; with 50 it plays practice games, inspects how it loses, edits the part of the network behind the loss and keeps a change only if it wins more; that version beats the equal-sized PPO network trained on 20 million games.

Scaling up. Experiment 7 fine-tunes two copies of Gemma 3 270M identically on the same QA dataset, one to answer correctly and one to agree with whatever the user suggests, freezing the token embedding and output layers so the input/output distributions cannot give the answer away; the sycophant follows 100% of wrong hints on facts it was not trained on, the honest model 1–3%. From weights alone Astra failed in all four attempts, listing many weight-space differences it could not map to behavior. Allowed to see attention patterns only — both models run on up to 16 texts Astra writes, at most 20 runs, no access to outputs — it picked the sycophant in all three pairs, and in the sycophantic networks the middle-layer heads shift attention towards the user's suggestion. Experiment 8 moves to Qwen3.5-0.8B (24 layers, thinking on) with a Python kernel over the weights, no GPU, practice problems drawn from the GSM8K and MATH training sets (7,469 and 7,275 problems, never the test sets) and a budget of 10, 25 or 50 forward passes. Astra found that the model loops — 85 of its first 96 practice answers ran to the 32,768-token limit — traced the looping to one direction written by the MLPs of layers 15 and 19, and subtracted that direction from 64 output columns in each. Because looping is not specific to math, IFEval improved as well; with 10 or 25 forward passes the loops barely went away and MATH-500 stayed near the base score. The additional experiment asks whether weight editing can do more than remove an obstacle: Gemma 3 270M, which reaches its token limit on only 8.5% of answers, could not be improved. Astra found ordinary reasoning errors — wrong arithmetic and invalid algebraic steps — with no single component to switch off, its edits stayed small and targeted (for example, doubling one neuron that responds to numbers), and none moved MATH-500 by more than about a point, a gain Astra itself reported as not established.

The post closes by placing the work against prior art and sketching what follows. It notes that mechanistic interpretability reads networks with purpose-built tools or with models trained to read activations or weights, that gradient-free methods still find weights by trial and error, and that hand-setting weights has so far been demonstrated only at very small scale (AdderBoard). It argues the new element is a general-purpose frontier model used as it is, reading and writing weights from 171,664 numbers given as text up to a 0.8-billion-weight language model with tools. The future directions it lists are auditing without evaluation (reading out hidden problems such as backdoors from the weights), dramatically reducing optimization cost (one-shot initialization instead of datasets and training loops), zero-data capability steering and unlearning (because an edit touching 0.016% of the weights can leave the rest of the model intact and avoid catastrophic forgetting), recursive self-improvement, and true architectural and model merging that grafts a circuit such as a reasoning module from one model into a completely different one. The authors say results are still limited by context length and harness inefficiency, that a benchmark and harness for Weight-Space Programming are under construction, and that the complete logs of every experiment — everything sent to Astra, everything it returned with its reasoning summaries, every piece of code it ran, and the evaluation behind each number — are released at github.com/RikiyaT/lm_understand-logs, with the edited Qwen3.5-0.8B on Hugging Face.

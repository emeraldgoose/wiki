# Detecting credential-driven attacks on Databricks

**Source**: [Databricks Blog](https://www.databricks.com/blog/detecting-credential-driven-attacks-databricks) · **Published**: 2026-11-11

## Overview

The modern cyberattack does not always begin with a shattered perimeter; it begins with a valid login. Once an adversary acquires a leaked session cookie, personal access token (PAT), or service-principal credential, the initial breach is already complete, and their entire goal becomes blending in under the guise of the compromised user or service. The article's analogy: having a key does not mean you know the building. An intruder with a stolen badge still has to navigate the guard, guess which floors they can reach, and find the vault — and because an attacker lacks the deep, localized context of real employees, their behavior eventually betrays them. The post walks through how a credential-driven attack unfolds on the platform, how to detect the identity's behavior shift with Databricks verbose audit logs and Genie, how to respond once a compromise is confirmed, and which controls limit what a stolen credential can do in the first place.

The detection model assumes non-linear attack sequencing. Adversaries validate one set of credentials to map the environment, then retrieve another set to return through a new identity or workspace carrying no reconnaissance history; they may gather information without establishing persistence, or establish their way back in before finishing. The running example: a data engineer's PAT is copied from a misconfigured CI/CD log; within minutes it is used from a new IP to list secret scopes, catalogs, and repos the engineer has never touched; a secret holding a service-principal credential is read; an hour later that service principal — not the engineer — creates a function, adds itself to an admin group, and exports a handful of init scripts. Each event passes as routine platform use; taken together, they are one intrusion moving through the attack chain, and Databricks' model connects them into a single investigation.

Investigation runs on telemetry you already have: control-plane activity lands in the system.access.audit system table, and the Detection App adds a Genie agent for audit logging with more than 30 built-in detections, from token misuse to admin grants. You can ask in your own words ("Which notebooks did user@example.com export yesterday?") and Genie writes and runs the SQL, or give it a goal ("Were we compromised in the last 90 days?") and it works through the detections one at a time. Two limits apply: an empty result does not mean nothing happened (the event may be outside your time window, or verbose audit logging may be disabled), and audit logs record that something changed and who changed it, but often not the old or new values.

## Key points

- Stage 1 — access with valid credentials: the login itself rarely looks unusual because the credential is real; the tell is what the identity does in the minutes after signing in. Start the timeline from the authentication event (user_identity, source_ip_address, user_agent, sessionId, request_id, workspace_id), flag accounts.tokenLogin bursts from an unfamiliar client followed by the same token from a different source IP, and flag service-principal OAuth tokens minted from the expected network with an unexpected user agent — especially when new-IP activity follows within a minute.
- Stage 2 — discovery: five or more distinct discovery APIs called within one minute is unlikely to be a person navigating the platform; compare activity to the identity's baseline (a first-ever secret enumeration by an established identity, or a SQL session shifting from business queries to SHOW TABLES against access-control and configuration schemas); include failed requests — a first IpAccessDenied after accounts.oidcTokenAuthorization often is the earliest record of a credential being replayed from a blocked network.
- Stage 3 — harvesting more credentials: the adversary lists secret scopes, lists their secrets, reads the useful ones, and returns as a different, often more privileged identity. Alert on the progression (listScopes → listSecrets → getSecret repeated across scopes in quick succession), correlate denied reads with later successful ones, monitor compute-side access (submitted commands calling secret utilities or read_files() against credential paths), and connect every secret read to what happens next. The investigation must now follow two identities; during response, rotate every credential the identity could have reached.
- Stage 5 — persistence: treat security-control changes as high severity even when they fail (setSetting/deleteSetting, IP access-list edits, createPrivateAccessSettings), review SCIM group changes from anything other than the expected IdP, flag generateDbToken calls from unfamiliar sources or during job runs, and watch for workloads that continue on their own — a job that keeps modifying groups, permissions, or settings after the adversary's interactive activity stops may be maintaining access by itself, so preserve its definition before terminating it.
- Stage 6 — collection and cleanup: judge exports by context, not count. workspaceExport records the path, format, and whether it was a direct download; prioritize exports by unfamiliar identities, to unusual paths or formats, from unseen networks, or following secret access — a small targeted init-scripts export can leak more than a bulk dump. Record short-lived object churn (createFunction followed by deleteFunction, or create/delete/recreate cycles), and connect temporary-credential and data-read bursts to earlier reconnaissance rather than alerting on encoded commands alone.
- Investigating with Genie: work from Genie One in two ways — natural-language questions that Genie turns into SQL, or the Detection App's 30+ built-in detections driven by a goal such as "Were we compromised in the last 90 days?".
- Limit what a credential can reach (ingress): restrict privileged service principals to trusted networks while applying a different policy to human users, apply policies to both workspace and account requests (workspace policies do not cover the account console or account-level APIs), start in dry-run mode reviewing denials in system.access.inbound_network, test serverless and private-connectivity workflows before broad enforcement, treat IP access lists as an additional layer, set expiration and rotation for service-principal credentials, integration credentials, and PATs, and retire dormant credentials — a compromise of a dormant credential can go unnoticed for months.
- Limit what a credential can run or change: restrict high-impact operations (UDF creation, secret-scope ACL changes, workspace permission changes, job administration, ingress policy changes) to approved identities and controlled automation; separate administrative paths from compute paths so ordinary job runtimes cannot modify account and network controls; protect cloud execution environments with hardened metadata-service configurations and compute egress restrictions; and retain audit logs, version history, UDF definitions, permission-change history, and ingress denial logs long enough to reconstruct a campaign.
- The closing principle: a stolen credential's blast radius is only as damaging as the privileges assigned to it.

## Details

- Audit events are written as service_name.action_name (for example secrets.getSecret); the six-stage guidance is a starting point to tune against each environment's normal automation, and the stages are rarely traversed in a straight line.
- In the running scenario, the data engineer's PAT copied from a misconfigured CI/CD log leads, within an hour, to a service principal creating a function, adding itself to an admin group, and exporting init scripts — three individually routine events that together mark one intrusion.
- Stage 4 (finding a workable execution path) sits between harvesting and persistence: the adversary identifies where the stolen credentials can actually execute, which is why compute-side telemetry (submitted commands) matters as much as the secrets API.
- The secrets API caveat: when verbose audit logging is enabled, submitted command text shows what ran, but it does not confirm that a secret value was actually returned — so compute-side evidence and API evidence must be read together.
- For cleanup detection, objects that survive an adversary's session are often given more plausible names than the ones deleted, which is why preserving definitions and timing before reverting anything is part of the response guidance.
- The article closes with three concrete steps: review the Databricks security best practices, turn on verbose audit logging plus context-based ingress control for both planes, and deploy the Detection App to ask Genie "Were we compromised in the last 90 days?".
